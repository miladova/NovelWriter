# Chapter Eight: The Dead City

*Warsaw — Wednesday, 14 July 1943*

The order came on a Monday: the Kommandantur was to provide a survey of quartering possibilities in the "former Jewish residential district" for a work detachment of eight hundred men, in connection with a clearance and salvage programme in the ruins. Leutnant Adler brought the file in and put it on Friedrich's desk, and Friedrich looked at the order for a long time, at the letterhead of the Bauleitung of the Waffen-SS and the Hauptamt für Raumordnung, and then looked out of the window, and said, "Very well. Wednesday. The car."

"Why not Oberzahlmeister Wollweber?" Adler said. "He has the quartering lists."

"Because Wollweber has not gone east of the cordon since 1941 and would come back and tell me that the district is full of valuable salvage and I should requisition it for the shops," Friedrich said. "And because I want to see it myself. Bring the sketch map from 1939, and the key to the Gęsiówka gate, and Kellner."

"Shall I come, Herr Major?"

Friedrich looked at the young man for a moment. Klaus Adler had a nice face, brown and open, and he had been in Warsaw seven weeks; before that he had been a company officer in white Russia, where, in the winter of 1942, he had been in a village on the Beresina with a rifle company and had, according to the letter his own colonel had written him, conducted himself well; and before that he had been in a Labour Service camp and in the Hitler Youth and in a family in Pforzheim that had a shop and four sons in the Wehrmacht. He was eager and clean and punctual and had never once, in seven weeks, said a false thing about the General Government in Friedrich's hearing.

"No," Friedrich said. "You will stay and dispose of the morning's mail. Write to the commandant's office that the survey will be delivered on Friday, with a covering note explaining that the district contains no quartering of any military value."

"Officer quarters?"

"None of any military value," Friedrich said, "is what you will write."

He said it mildly, and Adler looked at him for a moment with the small, closed, watchful expression that came over his face whenever Friedrich made the flat statements that officers were not supposed to make, and then said, "Yes, Herr Major," and gathered the files and went out; and Friedrich sat for a moment looking after him, and was aware, as he had been aware since the day in the Saxon Garden, of the young man's eyes.

*Tuesday, 13 July 1943*

Ritter sent for him at noon and did it with coffee, which was a bad sign.

"Sit down. Help yourself. It is real; my wife sends it from Silesia in the field post and I am reduced to hiding it from my adjutant." The General waited until Friedrich had the cup in his hand, and then took off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and said, in the tone of a man discussing garden drainage: "Now. Szucha."

"Herr General."

"Obersturmführer Dörffel has written to me — not to you; to me, in my own name, which is the courtesy of a man who wants an answer that has been given some thought — and he has asked me, in eleven lines of perfect German, whether the Wehrmacht-Kommandantur Warschau has employed its staff officers to intervene in the racial and labour classification of Polish civilians not in its service; and whether the pattern of correspondence in the Referat für Arbeitseinsatz is the pattern of an office doing its work or of a man doing his." Ritter put the spectacles back on. "I have known Dörffel's type since 1913. He was a lawyer before the war, in Kiel, and he took a bullet in the leg at the Somme, and he wears no decorations, and in the five years since this business began he has never once been in a hurry. A man who is not in a hurry is a man who intends to still be in the room when everybody else has left. He is not going to arrest you, Friedrich. He is going to watch you until the day he can arrest you, and on that day he will have everything, because he will have had a year to collect it, and the reason he will have had a year is that he enjoys the collecting."

"What do you advise, Herr General?"

"I advise the truth, but not to him." Ritter pushed a sheet of paper across the desk: the carbon of Klara's answer to the first Szucha letter, the one in May. "This is a good document. It gives the facts, it gives the regulation, it gives nothing else, and it was typed by a girl who cannot be broken because she does not know anything. There are four more like it on my file now, and the file makes a shape I do not like, and I am going to say a thing to you that I have said once before in my life, in 1917, to a captain on my own staff, and it was true then and it is true now." He leaned forward. "You are a Prussian officer and you are behaving like a missionary. Missionaries die, and they take their sergeants with them. If you cannot stop — and I look at you and I see that you cannot stop; it is your father's face, he had it too, he had it at Verdun and it is the reason he was not a general — then for God's sake be better at it. Buy nothing in cash. Write nothing in your own hand that you would not read out at a court martial. Never go twice to the same door. And never, ever let anybody see you care about one particular Pole, because the moment a man in that building sees that you care, that Pole is not a person any more, that Pole is a lever."

"Yes, Herr General."

"Now get out, and take the coffee cup with you, and tell my adjutant that I want the Polish-language newspaper files for the last fortnight. I am going to be reading about the cinema." Ritter began to sign letters, and then, as Friedrich reached the door: "The Führer's birthday is in April, Friedrich, and the staff's ceremonial duties for next year will be worked out in the autumn. If the war is still going on then, I intend to be a general with a clean record and a very dull file." Sign. "I mention it in connection with nothing at all."

*Aleja Szucha 25A — Tuesday afternoon*

The Institute for Racial Hygiene occupied the fourth floor of the building next to the Security Police, with its own staircase from the courtyard, so that persons visiting the Institute did not have to walk past the cells of the Security Police, which was, Friedrich thought as he went up, a piece of architectural thoughtfulness that told you everything about what the German state thought of science.

A clerk took his card and left him in an anteroom for twenty minutes with a copy of the *Deutsche Allgemeine Zeitung* and a poster on the wall that showed a family of clean-limbed children standing in a field of rye, under the words *Haltet rein Euer Blut* — keep your blood pure — and Friedrich read the newspaper's financial page, and turned it, and turned it again, and did not look at the poster.

The file on Maria Anna Wojtera was a folder two centimetres thick, and he was given it on the written authority of the Referat, and he sat at a small table in the anteroom and read it from the beginning, and it took an hour and a quarter, and at the end of it he had one name, one number, and a coldness in his stomach that he recognized from the dead city.

The contents were in order, and that was the horror of them. A four-page Familienbogen, with the mother's statements under oath. Three photographs of the child, front and two profiles, taken in the basement at Skaryszewska on the twenty-seventh of May, and three of the mother, taken on the tenth of June. A sheet of measurements. A note of the mother's family history, with the villages of four grandparents. A note, in Teichmann's hand, on the discrepancy of the ear, and the sentence *Fall vorläufig offen* — case provisionally open. A note on the child's musical aptitude, with a doctor's arrow and the single word *verwertbar?* — exploitable? And on the last page, a form with a stamp in the top left corner, an identification number typed at the top, and, in the space marked *Anträge und Vorbehalte* — applications and caveats:

*Petition 2188/43 (Rasse- und Siedlungsamt Posen): claim of suitability for Germanization of a female child of German blood, applicant: Brandt, Ilse, widow, Posen, Karlstrasse 9. Registered 2 June 1943, priority by date of application 21 June 1942. Recommended for consideration on completion of the racial finding. Cross-reference: list 4 of 9 September 1941 (childless women, Reichsgau Wartheland); the applicant's original entry, superseded by this claim. Priority by date of application: 21 June 1942.*

Friedrich read that last paragraph four times in the anteroom with the poster of the rye field on the wall, and it was not the identity of the applicant that struck him first, though that came in the second breath, with the force of a blow in the chest: his own sister-in-law, Konrad's widow, Ilse, at the family's table at Falkenhorst at Christmas, asking him across the soup whether he had thought any more about the estate, and whether he and Erika might ever consider a child from the Lebensborn, and whether the East was really as bad as people said.

No: what struck him first was the date.

The twenty-first of June, 1942. A petition registered a year ago in Posen, in the fourth week of June 1942, by a widow of the family von Brandt, on a form, in the ordinary course, at the routine request of an office that was collecting names of childless women for the allocation of orphans from the eastern territories — a thing she could not have known anything about; a thing she had done, no doubt, the way she did everything, quietly, decently, with a woollen dress, a kind word for the maid, and a completed form.

A petition registered twenty-one June 1942. And the child's name had gone into the racial file in the last week of May 1943 — a distance of eleven months — and the petition's file number was 2188, and it lay in the folder with a priority by date, and the priority was at the top of the list, and the woman in the green suit who had put the child's photograph on her table had been waiting a year for the list to produce a girl, and the list had produced one, and the girl was her brother-in-law's daughter.

Friedrich closed the folder and sat with his hands on it, and heard the clerk's typewriter in the adjoining office, and looked at the poster of the rye field, and thought, with the last clear thought he had that day: *this is how it happens. Not with a knock on the door, not with a policeman, not with anybody intending anything. It happens with a form, in a year, and a date, and a stamp, and nobody in this whole apparatus is doing anything except his job.*

He gave the folder back to the clerk and walked down the four flights of stairs into the courtyard, and stood in the afternoon light for a moment, and then took out his notebook and wrote, because his hand was steadier than his mind: *Go to Posen (Karlstrasse 9) first opportunity. Do not write. Cannot write.* And under it, crossing out the first letters, he wrote: *Nothing can be done through the family. The family is the instrument.*

***



They drove east in the Opel at eight in the morning, over the Kierbedź bridge, along the streets of Praga to Ząbkowska, across the Pelcowizna road, and turned in at the cordon gate on Bonifraterska Street at half past nine.

The gate was two barriers of planks and wire and a sign in two languages; and the guard was a Landesschütze of the 386th, a man of about fifty-five with a broom of a mustache, who looked at Friedrich's pass, and then at Friedrich, and then at the pass again, and said, in the voice of a school caretaker, "Do you want to go *in*, Herr Major?"

"I do."

"Then I will tell you the thing everybody tells everybody, and you can do as you like with it." The old man leaned on the barrier. "Breathe through your mouth for the first hundred metres, and do not, whatever happens, do not go into the courtyards. The courtyards are where the cellars are, and the cellars are where they flood, and there is a gas in the air down there from the burning that collects in the low places at night, and in the mornings you can see the flies. If the flies rise, get out. Also there is the stink and the stink is not the danger, the stink is only the stink." He raised the barrier. "Three o'clock is the relief. Do not be here at four; we shoot at movement in the ruins at four."

They went in.

Friedrich had been a soldier for eleven years and had been in three countries and had seen a village burned by a punitive expedition in Poland in 1939 and a church full of people burned in white Russia in 1941, from three hundred metres, with the field glasses passing from hand to hand along the line, and he had believed, until he walked into the dead ghetto on the fourteenth of July 1943, that he knew what destruction looked like.

It does not look like destruction. That was the first thing, and he understood it in the hundred metres of Bonifraterska Street, and the rest of the day only confirmed it: the dead ghetto did not look like destruction, it looked like a *plan*. The Germans had knocked the buildings down with a method — with burning, with explosive, with the labour detachments, from a map, street by street — and the result was not chaos. The result was a city read at a level of the horizon: at the level, Friedrich thought, of a city seen from an aeroplane, except that you were walking in it. The streets were still streets. The junctions were still junctions. You could stand at the corner of Muranowska and Zamenhofa and see, on the ground, the four corners of an intersection; you could read the plan of the district in the stump-fields of brick; and the scale of it, the vastness of the emptiness contained between standing street lines, was worse than the collapse would have been, because the emptiness had a ruler to it, and the ruler said: *this was done by people who took their time.*

They walked north up Zamenhofa. There was brick dust on everything, fine and grey and slightly warm, and it got into the nose and the throat and the shoes; and there were places where the ground was hot; and there were places where the ground gave, and Kellner, who had grown up in Berlin and had been at the front in France, walked where Friedrich walked and said nothing at all.

At the corner of Miła, near where the bunker had been under the house at number eighteen — Friedrich knew that street name from the reports, and from the newspapers, and from conversations in the mess at which the men who had been in the operation said the things they said — the salvage detachment was at work: a hundred and forty men in two groups, guarded by Ukrainian auxiliaries, pulling down a chimney stack with a system of wires and winches, carting the bricks into neat iron frames and stacking the frames, sorting the iron and the tin and the lead into separate heaps, dredging the cellars with a long pole fixed across a cart; and it was all so orderly that it might have been a municipal improvement scheme, and at the corner there was a small tin hut with a telephone, and a sign on the wall of the hut that said, in German: *Sammelstelle — sorting depot — 8b. Buchhaltung — accounting — second door.* And among the ruined walls stood a wooden notice board painted black with white letters, put up by the German administration for the benefit of polite visitors, and on it were the words:

*Der jüdische Wohnbezirk ist nicht mehr vorhanden. Dieser Bereich wird von der Stadt Warschau zur Neuanlage von Wohnflächen für deutsche Siedler überplant.*

*The Jewish residential district no longer exists. The area is being re-planned by the city of Warsaw for residential development for German settlers.*

Friedrich stood in front of that board for a long moment, and Kellner waited behind him with his cap in his hands, and the work detachment went on pulling down a chimney three hundred metres away, and the man who had put up that board had used a spirit level; the letters were straight.

They went on. On Smocza they passed, under a piece of orphaned standing wall, a chalk mark in a child's hand — the street game of hopscotch, drawn a hundred years before — and Friedrich looked at it because the chalk was so bright against the burnt brick, and then he saw that it was not old at all: it had been drawn within the last few days, by a child, on a patch of clean paving that the wind had swept. Which meant that a child had been standing there within the week. Which meant there were people in the ruins still, in the heat and the flies and the dust, and the man in the tin hut at the corner was supposed to be looking for them with his dredging pole, and either he was not looking or there were more of them than the whole apparatus could count.

He did not write that in his notebook. He wrote: *Street line intact at Smocza/Źelazna; no quartering; water table high in the cellars; no rebuilding before 1945.*

At noon, in the shade of a railway arch on Stawki, they ate the sandwiches Kellner had brought; and across the main road, beyond the wire of the cordon, in the strip of waste ground that had been the big pre-war goods station, they could see the Umschlagplatz — the loading place — a platform and a rail siding and a great loop of track, entirely empty, entirely ordinary, with grass already up between the sleepers, and at the far end of the platform, bolted to two posts, a long inclined plank, at the exact angle of a cart ramp. Friedrich looked at the ramp for a long time, because a ramp is a thing a man cannot look away from once he has understood it: a freight wagon's floor stands a metre above the platform, and a plank, and children of nine and old women of eighty walked up it, and it was, in the German language, *die Rampe*, and everything had been arranged, with German thoroughness, so that a person could be shifted from a cattle truck to a labour camp to a shed to a gate to a wall without any German ever having to raise his voice.

"By the ramp," Kellner said, "that's where the boy Peltz got his wound. The Leutnant. Eleven days before he died. He was standing there on the platform, looking at the wagons after the last one left, and somebody up in the goods shed, in the dark, put a bullet through his neck. He was a nice lad, Herr Major. He kept a tally in a book and he never once raised his hand to anyone. The sniper shot him because he was wearing grey, and because the man in the goods shed wanted to shoot somebody wearing grey before he was caught." Kellner packed up the paper from the sandwiches into his pocket, because he never left litter in a place where litter was noticed. "They caught the man. Jewish, from a bunker. They shot him in the ruins at four o'clock on the same day, and it was Leutnant Peltz's own company that did it, and the sergeant-major cried while he gave the order, and that is the whole story of the Polish war."

"Get the car," Friedrich said.

They were on the way back, following the sketch map towards the cordon gate on Bonifraterska, when he saw the wall.

It was the wall of the Gęsiówka: the old military prison on Gęsia Street, which had been inside the ghetto, and which had stood at the edge of it, and which the SS had taken over in 1943 as a labour camp for Jewish prisoners from the eastern districts, who were still being brought there in hundreds in the summer of 1943 — everybody in Warsaw knew that by then, in the way that a city knows a thing — and the walls of the Gęsiówka were three metres high with the wire above and the gate with its ironwork, and beside the corner of the wall, in the yard of a house that had been knocked down for salvage, there stood the house's last surviving wall, alone, its three neighbours gone, with the plaster still on the inside and the ghost of a staircase going up it into the open air; and in the shadow of that orphan wall, on the ground, there was a man.

Friedrich saw him at thirty metres through the dust: an old man, curled on his side in the shade, with his coat over his head, quite still. He might have been asleep, or ill, or dead; and the whole apparatus said that a person in the ruins was a fugitive, and a fugitive was a matter for the Sicherheitspolizei, and an officer of the Wehrmacht who saw a fugitive and did not report him was a man who had chosen a side.

Kellner had seen him too. Kellner's hands on the wheel were quite steady, and he drove at the same slow speed, and he said, in the voice of a man passing a church: "Herr Major, the gate's four hundred metres."

Friedrich looked at the old man in the shade with his coat over his head.

"Stop the car," he said.

He got out. Kellner stayed at the wheel with the engine running, and Friedrich walked back fifty metres along the street in the hot grey dust, and the whole time he walked he was thinking, with the cold clarity of a man walking into a fire, that this was the single stupidest thing he had ever done in his life: that there was a Ukrainian guard post two hundred metres away, that he had no food on him, that he had no water, that he could not carry the man anywhere, that if the man was a Jew and was seen with him, then the file would have a Wehrmacht Major in it, and the file with a Wehrmacht Major in it would go to Szucha, and a Major from a Prussian family in a Szucha file was a scandal that would destroy his name and his father's name and his sister's marriage and put him on the Russian front or against a wall; and he knew that he had to do it anyway, and that he had known it in the first three seconds, and that the last fifty metres of walking had been nothing but the price of pretending otherwise.

The man was alive. He was, Friedrich saw as he came up, not old: perhaps sixty, perhaps fifty-five worn to sixty, with a grey beard and a shirt that had been white in 1939, and the coat over his head was not on his head for sleep but against the sun and the flies, and when Friedrich's shadow fell on him he said, in Polish, without moving, in a voice that was dry and calm: "I am not here. There is nobody here. Go away, and God bless you, and do not come back."

Friedrich stood over him in the ruin of the courtyard of a house on Gęsia Street in July 1943 and did not know a single word of Polish that was of any use to him in that moment. What he had was a piece of sandwich and half a bottle of water and, in his tunic pocket, a bar of German army chocolate; and he put all three down on the ground, the chocolate on top, at the man's head, and said, in German, in the voice of a man reading a sentence from the files, because whatever else he was, he was a man who had learned that in this country a kindness spoken loudly gets two people killed: "There is a manhole in the courtyard of the corner house on Miła. It is open; the water below is low; it runs south to the Leszno sewer. Tell them in the bunkers that the surveying party at Narutowicza comes at six, and the guard posts are relieved at three. Do you understand German?"

There was a long silence. Then the coat moved, and a face came out of the shadows: narrow, grey-bearded, with the dirt of twenty months in a cellar in it, and two eyes that were still entirely clear, and entirely unafraid, and which looked at Friedrich for the length of perhaps four breaths, and then the man said, in German with a Galician accent:

"I understand German. I understand you too. You are not a good man and you are not a German; a German would have reported me. Go away now." And then, quietly, as Friedrich turned: "*Herr Offizier.* The chocolate. You have put it down with the paper on. Without the paper it will melt into my beard, and there is nothing worse than chocolate in a beard."

And Friedrich walked away without turning round, and did not hear the man's sentence, in dry Polish, into the dust after him: that they had buried the last of the fighters under the wall eight days before, and that God, in His infinite humour, had sent a Prussian.

Half a kilometre up Gęsia Street, in the courtyard of the Gęsiówka, behind the wire, a work party of the camp's prisoners was being counted for the evening roll call: three hundred and eleven men in striped jackets, with their shirts off in the heat, standing in five rows with their hands at their sides; and behind them the camp's Jews, who had dug and carried and sorted and been killed in the ghetto clearance since May, and in front of them an SS Unterscharführer on a box with a list and a stick; and Friedrich, walking to the car with his cap in his hand, did not look at them, because it was the middle of the afternoon, and the guard's duty was to look outwards, and an officer of the Wehrmacht standing and looking into a camp of Jewish labourers in July 1943 was exactly the kind of small ordinary thing that brought men to Szucha; and he got into the car and said "Bonifraterska," and Kellner drove, and neither of them said anything at all for four kilometres, until they were back on Bonifraterska Street with the gate in sight, and then Kellner said, without looking at him, in a voice of complete ordinariness:

"Herr Major. In the box behind the seat there is the bottle of schnapps that the quartermaster's office gave me for the men at Christmas. I mention it because I do not drink and because you have to go back into an office, and because this afternoon in this car is the only place in Warsaw where a man can be alone, and if you would rather I stopped the car here and went for a walk, I will go for a walk."

"Drive on," Friedrich said.

They came back over the bridge at four o'clock with the sun on the river behind them and the whole of the left bank in front of them, spires and chimneys and the one great bald place in the middle of it, and the city looked, from the river, exactly as it had looked a hundred years before: a city of roofs, with a line of smoke over the far edge and the crosses of the churches up over the town like the masts of ships.

Friedrich sat with the sketch map on his knee, and looked at Warsaw, and did not think about the old man, and did not think about the ramp, and did not think about the board with the straight letters and the street plan drawn for German settlers; and found after ten minutes that he was thinking, without mercy, and with the whole force of a mind that had been trained to solve problems and forbidden to feel them, about a small ledger of cases in a steel cabinet in a room on Nowy Świat where a girl of nine had a card whose renewal came round every month.

Klara Seidler was waiting in the corridor with three telephone messages on a slip in her careful, upright, clerical hand; and she gave him the slip without saying anything, and stood with her hands folded while he read it, which she had never done before.

*1. Obersturmführer Dörffel's office telephoned 11.40, asking whether the Major has been across the river. Answered: no reply.*

*2. Oberzahlmeister Wollweber requests the quarterly statement of the reserve renewals, for the fund.*

*3. Skaryszewska camp office telephoned 15.00. The camp commandant requests the Major's instructions: the children of detail three have been catalogued as *Sortierung (Rüstung)*, and the case of the minor Wojtera, Maria Anna, has been placed by the Sicherheitspolizei under the custody of the camp pending a decision of the Reichssicherheitshauptamt; the child is therefore transferred from the reserve group to the custody of the camp at the order of the Obersturmführer, and the mother has been informed by post today. The commandant asks whether the Major wishes to lodge an objection.*

Friedrich read it twice, standing in the corridor in his dusty boots, and then he looked at Klara Seidler.

"When was the child moved?"

"This afternoon, Herr Major. The order came by telephone to the camp at two o'clock and the child was moved at three. Her mother is on the afternoon shift at the laundry until six. The letter is waiting for her at the camp gate for when she comes to visit on Thursday." Klara's hands stayed folded. "They have put her in the prison block, Herr Major. In the room with the shutters, over the gate. The room they keep for the ones who are going to be examined by Szucha."
