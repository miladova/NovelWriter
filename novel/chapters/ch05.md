# Chapter Five: The Card Index

*Warsaw — Friday, 28 May 1943*

The month-end was always the worst three days of the Referat's month, and this one was the worst of the war, because the Arbeitsamt had raised the Warsaw quota by a third and because the news from the south had gone through the army like a boot through a window.

Tunisia had fallen on the thirteenth. A quarter of a million men, the Italian First Army and the Afrika Korps and everything that fed them, had gone into the bag at Cape Bon, and every mess in Warsaw had been quiet for a week; the officers who had been in Africa were quiet because their friends were in it, and the officers who had not been in Africa were quiet because they had heard the things that the men who had been there said about the way it had ended. In Friedrich's office, where the maps on the wall were of Poland and White Russia, they did not talk about it at all, in the way of a family that has had bad news and organizes the day around the fact that no one is to mention it.

The quota was 1,150 hands for June, which was a number, and the number was not the problem; the problem was that 1,150 hands did not exist in Warsaw any more. They had all been counted before. The metal workers were at the Ostbahn works; the tailors were in the workshops at the Gęsiówka and the Flak barracks; the tradesmen's names were on Böhm's ledger at the Arbeitsamt, where they had been bought and sold four times, and the men themselves had been sent to the Reich the week after they had paid for their exemption, so that the same man could be sold twice, which was, when Friedrich looked at it coolly, the most Polish thing about the whole occupation: a system in which everyone, the occupied and the occupiers, sold the same person over and over until the person disappeared and the last buyer was left holding an empty paper.

At nine o'clock Generalmajor Wolfram Ritter sent for him.

The Kommandant's office was on the first floor at the front, with two windows onto Nowy Świat, a portrait of the Führer on the wall behind the desk and a photograph of Ritter's own two sons in front of the window with the light full on them, and the light fell on the photograph in a way that showed you, before you knew anything else, that the elder son had been killed at Smolensk in 1941 and that his father had never moved the frame.

"Sit down, Friedrich. Coffee?" Ritter poured without waiting for an answer. He was fifty-six, bald across the top, with the heavy eyes of a man who had been sleeping badly since 1939. "The quota."

"I'm aware of it, Herr General."

"Everybody is aware of it. The Arbeitsamt is aware of it, which is why their man in the corridor this morning — what is his name, the one who wears the hat —"

"Böhm."

"Böhm, yes, told my adjutant that the General Government's labour reserves in Distrikt Warschau are 'exhausted' pending 'a further cleansing action.'" Ritter put the coffee down. "You know what a cleansing action is, Friedrich."

"I know what they call it."

"They call it that, and it means that they will cordon the streets and take whoever is in them, and then the Arbeitsamt will give us a share of what they have taken, and we will be able to say that our workshops are full and our quotas are met, and everybody will be very satisfied, and in August we will be running lathes with fourteen-year-old boys who have never seen a lathe and the accident rate at the shops will double and I will have to write a report about it." Ritter sat back. "I have been in the army for thirty-eight years. I've commanded a regiment, I've commanded a garrison, and I have never once been asked to write a report explaining why the generals have lost me a battle in an office. This city is going to make me an expert."

Friedrich did not smile. He had learned, in the eleven months of working for Ritter, that the old man's bitterness was real and that it cost him nothing and that it meant nothing: Ritter was a decent man who had grown old making the machine run and had discovered that decency is a private opinion and not a policy.

"There is a second thing," Ritter said. "The Warsaw district office has asked for a list of all cases in which this command has intervened in the labour disposition of persons who are the subject of a racial examination. That is the language they use. It came in writing."

Something moved in the room, very quietly, like a cat.

"I have no cases of that kind," Friedrich said.

Ritter looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked down at his desk and moved one paper to the other side of it, and when he spoke again his voice was the voice of a man giving a piece of fatherly advice over a garden wall.

"Friedrich, I knew your father at the Academy. He was a great man with maps and a difficult man with people. I say that with respect, because he did what he said he would do and he was never loved for it, and when I am asked what kind of officer he was I say that his division was where it was supposed to be, every day of the war. So you will understand me when I say that I know the look of the von Brandt family when it has decided that it is right and everybody else is complicated." He raised his eyes. "There are two kinds of officers in this army. There are officers who are useful, and officers who are correct. The useful ones are watched. The correct ones get promoted. Do not be useful, Friedrich. Be correct, and be correct in writing."

"And if a thing is both wrong and correct?"

"Then do it very slowly and hope the war ends." Ritter picked up his pen and began to sign the day's letters, and did not look up. "The file that came from Szucha on the twenty-first — the Obersturmführer's inquiry — went back out of this building two days ago. I saw the carbon on the adjutant's desk. You answered it."

"Klara Seidler answered it."

"Klara Seidler answers everything. I have read her since she came here; she is the best clerk in the General Government and she has no more idea than a cat what she is signing." Ritter waved his pen at the door. "Send her my compliments and tell her that everything written in this building about persons under racial examination is to come to me as well as to you. As of now. Before it goes anywhere."

"Yes, Herr General."

At the back of the outer office, past Klara's desk and past the typist with the lame hand, past the two clerks who played chess at lunchtime with a set they had made out of buttons, was the room that held the card index.

It was a room the size of a pantry, with a window the size of a packet of cigarettes, and it held four steel cabinets with eight drawers each and a row of box files on top, and it was, in its way, the most powerful room in Warsaw: because in Warsaw under the occupation a person was not a body, a person was a card, and a card could be moved from drawer to drawer by anyone with the key to the room.

Klara Seidler had the key.

She was standing at the open drawer C–Z when Friedrich came in, with a card in her hand and the drawer pulled out to her waist, and if he had not known her as he did he would have said that she was reading the card because she was filing.

"The Wojtera card," he said.

"Yes, Herr Major." She did not pretend. She never pretended; it was the most disconcerting thing about her. "I saw the name on the summons last Monday. The child's card is here because we hold the reserve list, and the woman's card is here because she is war-employed at the hospital laundry, and I looked at both of them because it is my job to know what is in the drawers, and I have never in eleven months seen a reserve case from Skaryszewska that was not a widow with five children."

"You have a reason for telling me this."

"I have a question." Klara closed the drawer with her knee and looked at him, with the pale, flat, unhurried eyes, and Friedrich understood that he had underrated her for eleven months. "The card of the child is marked *Reserve III/Skaryszewska* and it is renewed weekly by us. It costs the Wehrmacht nothing: the child is fed by the camp, worked by the camp, housed by the camp. The only thing our card does is take her out of every other list in this city, including the transport lists, and there is a regulation that says a person who is in a reserve cannot be transferred out of the district without the signature of the referring office." She said it precisely, the way a person reads a railway timetable. "That is what the card does. It holds a gate shut."

"Yes."

"Then the question I want to ask, Herr Major, is whether a card in a drawer can be renewed in a month's time. Because the Arbeitsamt renews the camp's reserve list only if the camp's retained quota is met, and the camp's retained quota is met by the commandant's discretion, and the commandant's discretion is — I am sorry, I do not know a polite word for this in any language — for sale." Klara folded her hands. "Herr Wollweber sells it. He has been selling it since February. The price is a hundred and fifty zlotys a head for a labourer, four hundred for a skilled man, and the money goes to a fund for the widows of the Feldgendarmerie, or into his boot, whichever is closer. There is no proof in this building that would interest a court martial, because the proof is a Form 22 in triplicate and the triplicates are in his coat."

Friedrich looked at the steel cabinets and at the little window and at the girl with her hands folded and the silver church pin on her collar.

"How do you know the price?"

"Because I take the money in an envelope and put it on his desk," Klara Seidler said. "He thinks it reassures me to be included." She looked down at her hands and then up again. "Herr Major, my father is a railway inspector in Kassel and my mother has a bad hip and my brother has been missing at Stalingrad since January. I have been in Warsaw eleven months and I have filed four thousand cards. I am not telling you this because I am a good person. I am telling you because the arithmetic is that on the present rota, the camp's retained sector will be closed and re-opened at the end of June, and the child in question will be on the list that closes, and there is nothing on any card that will stop it, and I would like to know whether there is a form for that."

There was a long silence in the pantry-sized room. Somewhere along the corridor a telephone rang and a man laughed at something and the laugh came down the passage and went away.

"There is no form," Friedrich said. "There is a price."

"Then I would like to know the price, Herr Major."

"Not today." He took the card out of her hand; the card of Maria Anna Wojtera, b. 3.12.33, with its small blue stamps and its weekly pencil marks. "In the drawer behind this card, is there a list of the camp's retained detail three?"

"Yes. Sixty-one children under fourteen."

"Take it out. Not to remove it; to read it." He handed the card back. "I want to know how many of the sixty-one can be entered on the reserve list as war-employed before the end of June, and what it would cost in Wollweber's envelopes. Then type it as a schedule, with a cover sheet that says it is an armaments-labour utilization plan for the workshops, and bring it to me, and the first item on the schedule is to be the child Wojtera." He turned to go, and then stopped, with his hand on the doorframe. "Klara. When you type the cover sheet, type it so that a stranger can read it and find nothing but an office doing its work. Do you understand? Not a word in it that a man could use."

"I understand, Herr Major."

"Then that is all."

"Herr Major." She had picked up a buff envelope from the shelf. "This came from Szucha at four o'clock."

He opened it in the corridor, because he did not want her to see his face.

It was one page, typed, with the letterhead of the Kommandeur der Sicherheitspolizei, and it was addressed to the Wehrmacht-Kommandantur Warschau, Referat für Arbeitseinsatz, for the attention of Major von Brandt, and it was signed by an Obersturmführer of Department IV whose name Friedrich had heard three times in eleven months and each time in a context he had not wanted to know about.

It said: *In the matter of the detained minor Wojtera, Maria Anna (case 4/1411/43), the undersigned would be grateful for a report from the referring office as to the grounds for the reserve entry of 20 May, together with the personal particulars of the applicant mother, and would consider it a courtesy if the Herr Major would call at Aleja Szucha 25 at his convenience, where the matter may be disposed of orally and without formalities.*

*Without formalities.* Friedrich stood in the marble corridor of the commandant's building on Nowy Świat with the letter in his hand, and he knew, as plainly as he knew his own name, two things: that the word *courtesy* in that letter meant an ambush, and that if he went to Szucha and sat down in a chair in that building and answered questions about Helena Wojtera, he would be signing the register of his own death, and hers, and the child's, in that order, and that he was going to have to go, because a refusal was also an answer.

At one o'clock he sent Kellner for the car and went to Praga.

He told himself that the reason was the quota: sixty-one children in the sorting detail were sixty-one wasted hands, and it was his duty to see what the camp was doing with the labour reserve. He even believed it for the length of the bridge, and then the school came up on the left with its red brick and its wide windows and its wire, and he stopped believing anything at all.

The sorting room was in the basement, in what had been the school's gymnasium: a long low hall with a vaulted brick ceiling and windows high up at ground level, so that the light came in grey and flat and full of dust. There were long tables down the length of it, and on the tables were mountains of rags: German army rags; hospital rags; uniforms of men killed in Russia, cut and sorted for paper and for wiping and for the fibre mills. There was a smell of dust and rot and carbolic. There were, Friedrich counted, twenty-nine children at the tables: from about six years old to about fourteen, in grey shirts or in their own clothes, and they worked with their hands moving, and they did not look up when he came in with Bierbaum and the female guard, because work was the thing that made you invisible and every child in the room had learned that in the first hour.

Maria Anna Wojtera was at the third table on the left, and she looked up, which was the first thing. She was sorting buttons out of a heap with a small wooden scoop and putting the good ones into a can, and there was a pencil behind her ear, worn down to a stub, and a rag tied over her head with a knot at the back; and when the door opened she looked up the way a bird looks up, and then, seeing officers, she went back to work like the others, and then — and Friedrich saw it happen, and it was the first thing in Warsaw that had ever put tears in his eyes without his consent — then, having looked down and gone on working for the space of ten seconds, she looked up again, not at him but at the wall beyond him, and her lips moved, silently, twice: *bar eleven. Bar eleven.*

She was practising. In the rags and the dust and the buttons, with a pencil behind her ear, she was playing bar eleven of the Szymanowski, from memory, on a violin that was not there.

Bierbaum was talking about the quota, and about the fuel for the bath house, and about the price of the Ukrainian guard company's rations, and Friedrich heard it all and answered it all, and while he was answering it he watched the child's hands move, quick and sure, and thought of the photograph in his pay book and of a room in Munich with a band playing beyond the trees.

"Number nine," he said, when there was a pause. "The girl at the third table. What is her disposition?"

Bierbaum looked. "Detail three, labour reserve. She's a case from the racial office; we hold her. There is talk of an examination on the tenth."

"Her hands are worth more than that." Friedrich said it in the voice he used for the war, the flat, unarguable, staff-officer voice. "Look at the speed of her. I have lathes in the workshops at Wolska; the Ostbahn is short of hands for fine sorting and they have German overseers who cannot count. I want her on my reserve as war-employed, with a trade entry: *Sortiererin* — sorter, light metalwork." He was writing it down as he said it, on his own pad, in his own hand, because a thing said is nothing and a thing written is a fact. "And the whole of detail three. The children of detail three are a labour reserve of this command as of today. Every one of them under trade entry, and every card to my office, and no transfer without my signature."

Bierbaum's face did something small and knowing. "The Major is building an empire of small hands."

"The Major is meeting a quota," Friedrich said, "which is a thing the Major is asked about every day and which the Unterstrumführer is not. There is a fee, of course."

"There is a fee," Bierbaum agreed, and named it: it was a modest fee and it was, Friedrich understood, exactly the price of the girl's life, expressed in the currency in which such lives were sold in the General Government in the spring of 1943: five kilos of coffee and two thousand zlotys, to be paid at the camp office, weekly, for the maintenance of the camp's cultural fund, by which Bierbaum meant his own pocket, and Friedrich agreed to it without blinking, because in that moment he would have agreed to anything.

And then, because he was who he was, and because the thing had gone on too long and had begun to burn a hole in him, he walked down the length of the sorting room to the third table on the left, and stood at the end of it, and looked at the child, and said, in Polish, in a voice pitched for a schoolroom: "You there. What is your name?"

The hands stayed on the buttons. She said, in Polish, with her eyes down, in the singsong of a child reciting: "Maria Anna Wojtera."

"And your father?"

"Adam Wójcik. He was a railwayman. He died before I was born."

The room had gone very quiet at the two tables nearest them. A small girl at the next table — three years old, Friedrich saw, three years old and sorting rags, with a face like a fist — began to make a low keening sound and stopped herself, and put her hand over her own mouth, and went on sorting.

"Where did you learn to count in German?"

"From the guards." She said it to the table. "I can count the buttons in German and in Polish. *Eins, zwei, drei…*"

"Good." Friedrich said. He looked at the pencil behind her ear. It was a stub no longer than a joint of a finger, and it was sharpened with a knife at both ends, and he knew, without knowing how he knew, that the mother had brought it through the gate in a packet of two hundred grammes, past the guard, in the visitors' yard, on Saturday morning. "What else do you know?"

"Nothing, Herr Officer."

"Nothing?"

"Only music, Herr Officer," the child said, and looked up at the ceiling, at the high windows with the dust coming down through them, and closed her eyes, and moved her left hand a little, four fingers, on the edge of the table, in the air, as if on the neck of a violin. "Only bar eleven. From the top. Slowly."

Friedrich von Brandt stood in a school gymnasium in Praga, in the presence of twenty-nine children and an SS-Untersturmführer and his own driver, and put both his hands behind his back so that no one could see them, and held on, and said, in German, in a voice of complete administrative calm: "The girl is transferred to the reserve of the Referat für Arbeitseinsatz as of today. The whole of detail three. Prepare the schedule for my signature."

Then he walked out into the yard, past the coal heap and the shed and the wire where, nine days before, a mother and a shorn child had held hands through the fence, and there, standing by the car with his cap in his hand and something unhappy in his fat face, was Kellner.

"There's a thing, Herr Major," Kellner said. "I didn't want to say in there. The young gentleman — Leutnant Adler — he telephoned the office while we were on the bridge. He said Szucha had been enquiring at the motor pool about the Major's movements, and about the Major's journeys across the river, and he said —" Kellner turned his cap over, the way a man turns over a coin he does not like the look of — "he said it kindly, Herr Major. He said it as if he was warning you. But he said it into the telephone in the office, where two clerks could hear him."

Friedrich got into the car and sat looking out at the wire.

He did not stay in the car. He went back into the school, past the queue at the bath house and the Ukrainian at the door, and up the stairs to Bierbaum's laboratory office, and shut the door behind him, and stood in front of the desk, and said, without preamble, the thing he had been composing all the way across the yard: "The fee for the reversal of a disposition. How is it paid?"

Bierbaum looked at him for a moment with the small, flat, professional interest of a man who recognizes a first-time customer. Then he opened the lower drawer of his desk, and took out a form — a real one, printed, with the eagle on it — and put it on the blotter between them, and turned it round so that Friedrich could read it, and it was a printed receipt for a donation to the *Kameradschaftskasse der Schutzpolizei*, with spaces for the amount, the date, and the donor's unit.

"You will pay nothing to me, Herr Major, and I will not take anything from you, and there will be no conversation of the kind we are having now, ever again," Bierbaum said, in the voice of a clerk instructing a newcomer in the office's procedures. "There is a fund of the camp, for the welfare of the men of the guard company: their Yule, their tobacco, their cinema, their letters home to Ludwigshafen. Contributions to the fund are voluntary and are acknowledged by this receipt. The receipt is made out to a name that I write myself and file in my own safe, and there is a second copy, which I give to the donor, and if the donor loses the copy, the donor has lost nothing, because the copy is in the safe and the copy is my funeral." He put the form back in the drawer. "To your question: six thousand zlotys and five kilogrammes of coffee, paid in two parts, half on the first of the month and half on the fifteenth; coffee in the tin, roasted, not ground, because ground coffee is weighed with sand by every Pole in this city, and the men of the guard company are not Poles, they are Ukrainians, and they prefer tea, so the coffee goes to my mother's cousin in Lemberg, which is not your business or the fund's. Now: the case of the girl."

"Detail three. The whole of it."

"The whole —!" For the first time, Bierbaum's face did something unprofessional. "Herr Major, there are sixty-one children in detail three. At the fund's rates —"

"I am aware of the arithmetic. The Wehrmacht requires hands; this command's workshops at Wolska and the Ostbahn's sorting depot at Praga are short of fine-assembly workers; an armaments labour plan for sixty-one persons, drawn as a schedule, signed by me, with the trade entries and the reserve numbers, is a document that the Arbeitsamt will countersign and the *Rüstungsinspektion* will approve, because it makes the Warsaw district's labour forecast look better in Kraków." Friedrich heard his own voice, level, fluent, completely shameless. "There is no payment at all for the plan. The payment is for the commandant's *discretion* in reassigning the persons on the plan to the reserve group, and that is a fee, and I will pay it, and I will pay it in coffee, in tins, in the second half of every month, and for that fee the commandant will keep his boot out of the reserve group's files, and no card in the reserve group will be altered, and no name in the reserve group will be exchanged, and no person in the reserve group will be transferred anywhere on any list, at any time, for any reason, without my signature; and if the commandant wants to say no, he may say no, and I will take the plan down the corridor to the Oberfeldkommandantur and the plan will be signed by another signature, and the *Kameradschaftskasse* will never see a tin of coffee again."

There was a silence. Out in the yard the counting went on, and the whistle of the bath house, and the sound of a work party's boots on the gravel.

"You have been doing this a long time, Herr Major," Bierbaum said at last, with what sounded, horribly, like admiration.

"Six weeks."

"Hm." Bierbaum wrote something in his notebook, short, and underlined it twice, and then looked up, and his face had gone back to being a whippet's face, and he said, conversationally: "Then, since we are both professionals, you should know the following, as a courtesy of the house. The room over the gate is not part of the camp. It is under the direct control of Aleja Szucha; the files of the persons kept in it are kept not here but there; and the persons who go into it are, in the language of the SS, *Befragungshäftlinge* — not prisoners, but a file. And this morning I received a telephone instruction from Szucha to hold the room in readiness for a case of theirs." He looked down at his notebook. "I mention it because when a Major of the Wehrmacht makes a labor-utilisation plan for sixty-one children, it is the kind of plan that goes on a desk in a different building, and one finds, in this city, that plans make echoes."

"Thank you," Friedrich said. "You will prepare the schedule."

He did not ask whose case it was. He had understood, from the first three words of the sentence, that asking was a way of hearing the answer out loud, and that once he had heard it, he would be a man who knew the answer, and knowing the answer out loud was a thing that could be written on a form; so he bowed slightly, the way Prussians bow in offices to other men's junior officers, and went down the stairs and out to the car, having paid for it with coffee and with the part of himself that could still be said to have clean hands.

At the end of the day, in the office, the schedule came back from Klara's typewriter, with its cover sheet in the bland language of an office doing its work: *Armaments Labour Utilisation Plan No. 61/A: transfer of reserve group III (light sorting) from camp Skaryszewska to the establishment of the Wehrmacht-Kommandantur Warschau, effective 1 June 1943. Cases: 61.* He signed it at the bottom and initialled the annexe, and then he sat with the pen in his hand and read the covering sheet from the labour office that had come in with the afternoon post, the routine monthly return of "transfers effected under plan 22" — the plan of the previous month, his own plan, his own signature at the foot of it.

Three hundred and forty persons. He had signed it on the second of May and had not looked at the annexe for more than thirty seconds, because on the second of May he had been in a hurry.

And there, at line 118, in the alphabetic list of trades, was a name he had heard that morning in a basement in Praga: the boy of twelve at the end table with the mouse in his pocket, who could whistle the whole of a march and had a mother in the camp, whose name Friedrich had heard because the female guard had shouted it at him across the room for dropping a button. And the trade entry against the boy's name was *Hilfsarbeiter* — helper — and against the destination was written, in the neat hand of a clerk of the Arbeitsamt, the words *Reichseinsatz, Braunschweig*.

He put the paper down on the desk, and sat looking at it for a long time, while the building emptied around him and the trams went past on Nowy Świat and the light went out of the sky over the city in the way it does in June in Warsaw, slowly, and he thought:

*My signature put a child on a train.*

And then he thought:

*My signature is the only thing that can keep one off.*

*Powiśle — the same evening*

The room Klara Seidler rented was in a house on the Browarna, below the escarpment, with a stove she fed with coke she bought on the black market and a bed, a table, a chair, a wardrobe, a crucifix, and on the table two photographs: her parents in front of their house in Kassel, and her brother in a labour-service uniform in 1940, before the army took him, standing with a spade over his shoulder and his cap over one eye, grinning, nineteen years old.

Since January he had been *vermisst*: missing. The letter from the company had said, at Stalingrad, in the industrial sector, in the fighting for a factory, of which nothing further was known; and her mother's letters asked the same question every week in careful German sentences — *is there anything new, have you written again to the Wehrmacht information office, they say that when the spring comes they will finish the lists, and the priest in Veckerhagen says that a missing man is not a dead man* — and Klara answered every letter with facts, because facts were what she had: the case number, the date of the inquiry, the address of the office, the sentence in the reply, the date of the next reply. She had written the facts down in a notebook. That was her way with pain: it went into a notebook, with the dates in the margin.

She was twenty-eight years old and she had been in Warsaw eleven months, and she had learned, in eleven months, the things that the clerks of the General Government learn: that a stamp is a weapon, that a drawer is a country, that a schedule can be signed with a pencil or with a pen and that the difference between those two signatures is the difference between a person who is going somewhere and a person who is going nowhere; and she had learned it not from anybody's teaching but from the daily observation of a sheet of paper, and the paper, on this Monday, had a name on it that had frightened her in a way that no paper in eleven months had frightened her, because in eleven months she had filed four thousand cards and she had never once seen a Major of the Wehrmacht write his own name in the margin of a card about a girl of nine.

She sat at the table in the light of a single lamp with the blackout paper up, and ate the bread and the sausage from the parcel her mother had sent with the field post and that had arrived that afternoon, stale, with a smell in it of the house at home; and then she took out her writing case and wrote to her mother the ordinary letter, the letter of the facts: the weather, the work, the new winter coat she had had made from an old blanket and the sergeant of the motor pool's sister, the price of eggs; and under all of it, in the same hand, between the lines of the ordinary, in the place where she always wrote the thing her mother would not read until the letter was twice folded — "Dear Mama, I am well, and I am being useful, and there is a Major here in the office who is a proper officer; his name is von Brandt, and he is from a family near Küstrin, and he has given me a good reference to the commandant; he is the only man in this building who says good morning to the Polish cleaner" — because her mother, in Kassel, in 1943, collected such sentences the way other women collected stamps, and because a proper officer, in Kassel in 1943, was a thing to be believed in, like the spring and the lists.

Then she put the letter in her bag, and sat for a long while, and did something she had not done before.

She took out of the drawer, from under her underclothes, a small notebook, black cardboard, ruled, the sort a shopkeeper uses for his accounts; and she wrote, in her careful upright hand, with the date in the margin, the thing that had happened that day, completely, with the names and the numbers and the times, and the words *detail three* and the words *war-employment schedule, see File 61/A*, and, at the foot, like a clerk's summary of a case:

*The Herr Major is doing a thing which, if it is found out, will end him. He did it in front of me and in front of Kellner and in front of the camp commandant, and he did it in such a way that I have signed the schedule as typist and the schedule bears my initials. I am therefore inside the thing whether I wish it or not, and I have decided that I wish it, and that the reason I wish it is not the girl and not the Major but the fact that in three years there has been one paper in this office that I could look at in the evening without wanting to wash my hands; and the further fact that if my brother is in a factory in the east, then somewhere there is a clerk who writes schedules, and I would like the clerk who writes Henri's schedule to be a person who once wrote a schedule for a child.*

She read it over, and then she did the thing that a careful person does with such a page, which is to keep it, because the alternative — to burn it — is a decision that no clerk in the General Government should ever make about the only record that a thing was done; and she put it under the underclothes, in the drawer under the drawer, and went to bed at half past nine, and lay in the dark listening to the trams on the river side, and did not sleep.

And then he picked up the telephone and asked Klara Seidler to come back and bring the whole of the reserve file, and to bring the light, because the work of the night was to find out how many other names had gone on to trains under the cover of the papers he had written to save one girl.
