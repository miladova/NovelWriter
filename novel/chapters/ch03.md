# Chapter Three: Hoża Street

*Warsaw — Saturday, 22 May 1943*

At six in the morning they were counted in the yard, and at half past nine a clerk came out of the school with a clipboard and began to read names, and Helena Wojtera heard her own name read out in the flat German of a man who had read four hundred names that week and would read four hundred more, and she stepped forward, and it was over in eleven seconds: a card, a pass, a rubber stamp on the back of her hand, and a voice telling her to stand by the west gate, she was going out with the eleven o'clock.

She said, "My daughter is here. Wojtera, Maria Anna, labour detail three."

The clerk looked at his list. "Detail three remains."

"I am her mother."

"Detail three remains." He had a small scab on his lip and he picked at it while he spoke, the way a man picks at a thing that is not his business. "You will be at the hospital laundry, Wiejska Street, six to six. You will report with the card. Next."

The woman behind her in the queue was called Dorota Rybak, and she had a son of fifteen in the men's block, and she said, from the side of her mouth, without moving her lips, "Take what they give you. Don't ask twice. Asking twice is how my sister went to Ravensbrück."

They let her see the child at the west gate for the length of time it takes a man to smoke a cigarette and a half.

The west gate opened onto the yard behind the school, where the coal heap was and the sheds, and there was a wire fence along the inside, waist-high, and the children in the sorting detail were brought past it in a line on their way to the basement, and the guard with the cigarette leaned on the fence post and watched the wall of the building and did not watch the two of them, which was the only mercy anybody in the place could offer and which Helena understood to be worth more than gold.

Marysia had a smear of dirt down one cheek and her shorn head was covered with a grey rag tied in a knot at the back, which she had done herself, Helena could see at once, with exactly her father's stubbornness, to stop the other children laughing at the hedgehog heads. There was a scratch on her forearm and a bruise the size of a plum on the back of her hand. She was holding a bread crust in her fist and she had eaten half of it and had saved half.

"Give me your hand through the wire," Helena said.

"It's all right. It's only on the other side of the wire anyway." The child put her hand up, and Helena took it, and it was small and cold and gritty, and the two of them stood with the wire between them like people at a railway station in a film.

"They're sending me out this morning," Helena said. "To a laundry, on the other side. It's a pass. I can come back — I can bring things — I'll come every day if they let me."

"You can't come every day. They'll hit you."

"I've been hit before."

"Not by them." Marysia looked at her with her father's eyes. "Don't come every day. If they think we're interesting, they'll look at us. If they don't think anything, they'll forget us. That's the best thing in here, being forgotten."

Helena heard her own voice coming out of the child's mouth, and had to look away for a moment, at the coal heap, at the shed, at the wet grey wall.

"Who told you that?"

"Pani Rybak. Her son says the ones they remember are the ones they send." Marysia's grip on her fingers was very tight, and she said, quickly, in a low voice, looking at the ground, "Mamo, I need things. Don't bring bread, they take it at the gate and sell it. Bring a pencil. Bring two pencils, one for me and one for Fela — she's three, she can't talk yet, she just holds the pencil and makes a noise, it's the only thing that stops her crying. Bring sewing thread, white, and a needle, because if you can sew you're a worker and they keep workers. And if you can, and if it isn't dangerous, bring the little case." The little case was the violin case. Marysia's violin, three-quarters size, had been left at the Szymanowski school when the Germans closed it, and had spent two years in the pawnshop on Chmielna, and had come home in 1941 because Helena had sold her mother's ring to get it. "Not for playing," the child said, urgently. "Because a girl who can play the violin is a girl who is worth something to somebody, and when they come to see who to keep, I want to be the girl who can play."

The guard threw down his cigarette and stepped on it.

"Two minutes," he said, in bad Polish.

"Two minutes," Helena said. She looked at her daughter and there was no time to say any of the things she had been composing in her head since Thursday: that she loved her, that she was going to get her out, that there was nothing in the world she would not do.

So she said: "Bar eleven. From the top. Slowly."

And Marysia laughed — a shout of a laugh, right there at the wire, in the yard of the transit camp at Skaryszewska, with the guard and the coal heap and the rain and the four thousand people — and Helena walked out of the west gate with her chin up and her hands in her pockets and her own hair shorn and a pass to the left bank, and she did not turn round, because turning round was one of the things the child had told her not to do.

She walked across the pontoon bridge with a crowd of workers, because the tram took Germans and the tram was therefore a trap, and the bridge was long and the water under it was brown and there were gulls sitting on the pontoons like gulls in peacetime, and on the other side Warsaw came up out of the river slowly, street after street, Krakowskie Przedmieście, Nowy Świat, and the whole city was grey and neat and full of posters: *KRIEGSWEIHNACHT*, *GESUNDHEIT*, *EINE STUNDE FÜR DIE EWIGKEIT*, and over them all, pasted fresh that week so that the paste still shone, the notice that named the men shot at dawn on Tuesday in the ruins of the ghetto, fifteen names, in Polish and in German, with a line at the bottom that said that further offenses would be met with the same severity.

*The same severity.* Helena walked past it. In Warsaw, in 1943, you walked past the wall with the names on it and you did not stop, because stopping was a signal, and reading was a signal, and there were men who stood at the corners and watched for signals; but in another part of the head, behind the eyes, you read the names anyway, the way a person reads a gravestone in the dark, and you counted them, always, the way a woman counts stitches.

At Hoża 14, the courtyard was loud with washday. Four women at the well with their skirts tucked up and their arms red to the elbow, and one old man carrying a copper of hot water, and two boys up on the shed roof because it was Saturday. And when Helena came through the gate, one by one the arms stopped moving, and the courtyard went quiet in the way courtyards do when a person comes back from a place where people do not come back from.

Pani Halina Rowińska said, "Jesus and Mary," and crossed herself with a wet hand, and then, at once — she could not help it, it was her nature, and her nature had been the ruin of people before — she said, "Your hair. *Mój Boże*, your hair. Where is the child?"

Helena walked across the courtyard without answering.

Zofia Mrożek came out of the caretaker's door with a broom in her hand and stood in front of her, and looked at her, up and down, at the shorn head and the camp smell and the pass and the wet coat; and then, without a word, she opened her arms, and Helena went into them like a child, at last, there in the middle of the courtyard in front of everyone, and Zofia held her and did not ask a single question, and over Helena's shoulder she said, to the courtyard, in the voice she used for rent and for the water supply and for closing gates at curfew: "The washing's gone cold, ladies. Boys, if you're on that roof when I come out with the stick you'll come off it. Halina, make tea, and use the real tea, not the carrot, or I'll know why."

In the caretaker's kitchen, with the good tea and the smell of onions, Helena told her.

When she came to the part about the child being kept, Zofia put both her hands flat on the table, the way a person braces against a door, and she did not speak for a long moment. Then she said, "So you're out and she's in."

"Yes."

"That's not an accident, Helena." Zofia's eyes were old and pale and very hard. "When the Germans let one out and keep the other, they've made a decision. Something about that child interests somebody. Now." She held up her hand before Helena could speak. "Don't tell me. I don't want to know; and if I don't know, my face can't say it, and there are men in this city who read faces better than they read Polish. What I know, I'll help you with. What I don't know, I can't lose. That's how we live now. You understand?"

"I understand."

"Good." Zofia got up and turned to the stove so that her back was to her, which was how that generation of women granted permission for the telling of unbearable things. "Then tell me your plans, and I'll tell you which ones are stupid."

At noon, she went down to the cellar with a piece of the real tea and half a loaf and a small tin of milk she had got from the janitor of the Szymanowski school in exchange for nothing but a favour to be named later, and sat down on an upturned box in the dark behind the coal bin, and there was a long silence before Rachel Perel put out her hand.

"You came."

"I came."

"Is Marysia with you?"

Helena had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it all the way home over the bridge, on the tram tracks, past the names on the wall: that she would say *she's with her aunt*, that she would say *she's gone to the country*, that a child of eight in a cellar needed a smooth lie that would carry her through the weeks, and she opened her mouth to say it, and instead, into the coal-smelling dark, she said the truth.

"They took her. On Thursday, at the market, in a round-up. She's in a camp in Praga, and I've been let out, and she's still in."

There was silence. Helena heard the girl's breathing, quick, and then the small sound of her moving the tin so that it stood upright in its nest of coats, and then, in a voice that was eight years old and forty years old at once:

"Aunt Zofia says you mustn't cry in the courtyard. You can cry down here. There's no one down here but me and the mice, and the mice don't tell anyone."

Helena Wojtera, who had not wept at the wire, and not on the bridge, and not in Zofia's arms, sat in a coal cellar with a loaf of bread in her lap and wept, and a Jewish child in a Polish nest put out one hand and patted her arm, in the dark, with small steady pats, three or four of them, and then took a crust of the bread, because she was hungry, and crying did not fill the belly, and there was not much bread.

That afternoon the queue at the pawnshop on Żelazna had twenty people in it, and Helena stood in it for two hours, and it was in the pawnshop that Warsaw's information market was at its thickest, because a pawnshop is one of the few places in a starving city where people have to talk about what things are worth.

She learned, in those two hours, the following:

That the Arbeitsamt at Skaryszewska was paying nothing at all and taking everything, and that a family in Ochota had been emptied of four sons in three weeks.

That the Gestapo at Szucha had opened a new department for the "Jewish question on the Aryan side" and that the price of silence had gone up twice since Easter.

That the price of a labour exemption — a real one, a stamp with a signature and a counter-signature, a *Familienausweis* — was between four and six thousand zlotys, or gold of equivalent weight, or an introduction to somebody whose name you were not to ask aloud; and that the man to see was a German official at the Arbeitsamt called Böhm, whom everyone called *Doktor* on account of nobody knew what, and who drank his coffee at the Europejski on Tuesdays and Fridays at eleven.

That a woman three places ahead of her in the queue, who had been in Skaryszewska for eleven days in March, had got her son out by proving that he was the sole support of a widowed mother with tuberculosis, and the proof had cost her a cow that she did not own and a doctor's paper that cost three hundred zlotys, and the boy had been out for three weeks and had been taken again on Tuesday, at night, at home, on a new list.

And that the children in the camps, the small ones, the ones with no parents left alive or no parents who could claim them, were taken away in a different kind of lorry to a different place, and stayed for a while in a house on a street nobody would name, and were then sorted — the word they used, this woman said it in a whisper, because she had learned it from a German, and German words had made it real — sorted by race.

"That's not real," a man behind them said, loudly, the way men who are afraid talk. "That's not true. They don't do that."

"They do it in Poznań," said the woman. "They do it wherever they've decided to make us into Germans. There was a woman on Wolska, her boy was taken, she went to every office in Warsaw with his photograph, and they told her at Szucha that the boy had been found to be of German blood and was being brought up as a German, and when she made a scene they told her that if she made a scene again, they would consider her an enemy of the German people, and she was an enemy of the German people, because she was Polish, and so she stopped. She stopped three years ago. She still keeps his shoes."

Helena's own business in the pawnshop was small: her father's watch, a Błonie, nickel, with a chipped enamel face, worth, the man behind the counter said, two hundred and forty zlotys, or a hundred and ten if she wanted it in her hand and not on a ticket; and her mother's gold ring, which, since it was gold, was worth more than the man was prepared to say at once, which is how Helena knew that the man was lying and that the ring was worth perhaps seven hundred; and the man in the black coat at the end of the counter, buying, not selling, who watched her hands with his chin in his collar, and whom she would remember later, when it mattered, as the man from the pawnshop.

She took the hundred and ten, and kept the ring, and as she put the ring back into the hem of her coat she felt, through the cloth, the small square hardness of the photograph, and something happened in her chest that was like a key turning in a lock in a house that had been left empty for years.

*Six thousand zlotys,* she thought. *And behind the money, a German who drinks coffee at the Europejski on Tuesdays and Fridays at eleven.*

She walked home the long way, because the long way went past the welfare office on Marszałkowska, and outside the welfare office there was a queue of a different kind: women with prams, women with children by the hand, women with the printed yellow cards of the RGO — the welfare council, the only Polish institution the Germans had let stand, because it was useful to them; and among those women, in a grey coat with a red cross armband, moving along the queue with a clipboard, asking a question here and there, stopping to write something down, was a heavy-faced woman of forty with her hair cut short and boots like a man's: Wanda Ostrowska.

Helena had met her twice, three years before, at the Szymanowski school, on a committee that had raised money for the families of prisoners and had dissolved itself when a member was arrested.

"Pani Wojtera," Wanda said. She had a memory like a filing cabinet and had not been given a single chance to use it. "The violin teacher. Your daughter's the little one with the plaits."

"She hasn't got the plaits any more," Helena said, and watched Wanda's face go still, and then said, quickly, quietly, standing at a tram stop with her hand on the pole, "She's in Skaryszewska. Detail three. Since Thursday. I'm out. They let me out and kept her."

Wanda did not react. That was the first thing Helena noticed about her: that no news, however bad, changed her face; the face stayed ordinary while the mind behind it turned over, at great speed, like a machine in a factory.

"Have you been told anything?"

"Nobody has told me anything. The clerk said detail three remains, and one woman in the pawnshop told me they sort them by race, and this morning a man in the street said something about a train to Rassenpolitik —"

"Ssh." Wanda's hand closed on her forearm, hard. "Not here. Two hundred metres. The courtyard behind the shop with the yellow sign. Five minutes."

The courtyard behind the shop with the yellow sign had a well, a cat, and a litter of bricks; and leaning against the wall of it, in the rain that had started again, the welfare worker talked to the violin teacher as if she were giving her a lesson: quietly, evenly, in the tone of a doctor giving a diagnosis to a patient who is going to need all her strength to hear it.

"This is what happens to children who are taken without their parents, if nothing is done," Wanda said. "They are processed at the labour office. They are washed and fed and put to light work in a German office, because a fed child is a presentable child. Then a commission comes from the racial office in Posen, and a man looks at the child with calipers and photographs, and asks the child's height and the shape of the skull and the colour of the eyes, and asks the mother — if there is a mother — questions about the family that go back three generations. If the child passes, the child is a German. They give the child a German name and a German family, usually in the Warthegau, usually a family with no children, and the child is not told. Many of these children do well. That is the terrible part: many of them are happy. The Polish girls who were taken from here in 1941 are fifteen now, and they sing German songs, and they don't remember their mothers." Wanda looked at her. "If the child fails — if the mother refuses to cooperate, or the blood is wrong, or the file is inconvenient — the child goes to labour. Labour for a child of nine means a farm in the Reich, and the farms in the Reich are not all the same, and the ones they send the children to are not the good ones."

"You are telling me there is no way out."

"I am telling you that there are three ways out, and you will not like any of them. The first is money: a labour exemption from the Arbeitsamt and a paper that says the child is a skilled worker in Warsaw in a reserved trade. That takes about six thousand zlotys and an official's signature, and it works until somebody looks at the file, and then it does not work.

"The second is hiding. The child comes out — maybe, if we can buy somebody at the camp gate, maybe if the child gets sick enough that a doctor sends her to the hospital, and from the hospital they lose her. Then the child goes to a house in the country, with false papers, and you never see her again except by arrangement, and if she is ever recognized, the farmer who took her is shot, and the village that knew is burned. I have buried people who tried the second way. Some of them are alive in the countryside right now and will never come back to Warsaw.

"The third way is the one nobody takes." Wanda's eyes were steady on hers. "If the child has a German father, then the file changes. Acknowledge the father and the child leaves the racial list; she becomes a case of *Abstammung* of a serving officer of the Wehrmacht, a scandal, a file for the Reich Security Office. It means a court-martial for the father: forbidden contact, racial defilement, in wartime, in an occupied country; at best a demotion and the Russian front, at worst a wall. It means the child is taken from you in a different way: at best made a German, at worst made nothing, and mixed-blood cases in the Reich are a file the doctors argue over for years. And it means, Pani Wojtera, that you would be signing the death warrant of the man you named, and I have never in three years of this work met a Polish woman who was willing to do that, and I have met many Polish women who were willing to do anything at all."

Helena stood in the wet courtyard behind the shop with the yellow sign and felt the world tilt, very slightly, like a table with one short leg.

"It is not a German father," she said. "It is the lodge secretary's son from Nowogrodzka and he died of typhus in 1939."

"Yes," Wanda said. She did not believe it. "Most of the time, it's not a German father. Most of the time it's a labour exemption or a hiding place. Come to me on Wednesday; the office is open on Wednesdays and I can put your name on a list for the Zamość children's fund, which gives you a right to buy milk at the depot, which is something. And Pani Wojtera —"

"Yes?"

"You are going to need an ally inside the German apparatus. Every case that has ever come out of Skaryszewska alive has come out because some German clerk needed something at the same time, and there was a trade. Not a betrayal necessarily — a trade. A signature for a signature. You have a face that men look at and a German that Rybak's wife says is better than the clerks'. Somewhere in that building on Nowy Świat there is a man with a paper in his hand and a reason to move it, and if you find him you must not waste him, and you must not trust him, and you must never, ever write his name down."

That evening, Tomek came.

He came at the curfew hour, which was when he always came, because a tram conductor's shifts were arranged by a man who knew what Tomek did with his evenings, and he came up the back stairs and knocked on Helena's door with the pattern of a neighbour, two and one, and he came in with his cap in his hand and his coat collar up and the smell of the tram on him, oil and wet wool and the smoke from the generator.

Tomasz Mrożek was twenty-four years old and he had his mother's flat wide face and a slow way of moving that was partly a manner and partly a warning to anyone who thought that slow meant soft. He had been at the university when the university closed. He had been a boy with a cello when he was sixteen, and Helena had taught him for a year and had told his mother, privately, that he had no gift, and it had been the only unkind thing she had ever said about him, and he had found out, and had never once mentioned it, and had brought her coal in January from a place he never named.

"You're out," he said.

"I'm out. She isn't."

He stood in the middle of the little room with his cap in his hands and said nothing for a while, and then he said the thing that she had not let herself hear from anyone else that day, because he was the only person in Warsaw who could say it without it being a consolation: "Then we'll get her out."

And because he said it simply, as a statement of fact, and because he was, she knew, a person who was in the habit of making true statements about impossible things, Helena Wojtera put her hands over her face and stood in the middle of her own room and heard the whole day come out of her at once.

Tomek did not touch her. He waited until it was over, and gave her his handkerchief, which was clean, which she noted even then, and which he would want back.

"There are three things," he said, when she was quiet. "First: the camp. There's a nurse at the infirmary in Skaryszewska who has helped us twice — she certifies people unfit for transport and they get moved to the hospital on Jagiellońska, and once they're in a hospital bed they're a different kind of problem. Second: papers. I know a printer who does baptismal certificates for children, very good ones, for two thousand zlotys, which is robbery, but he doesn't drink and he's never been caught. Third — and this is the one that isn't mine to say — there's a man in the labour office. Our people call him the Loaf, because he sold us a list once with the names of the ones he was about to take, and we got to eleven of them before the lorries came. He's German. He's a Hauptmann or a Major or something, in the workshops. In the Referat, on Nowy Świat."

Helena said nothing. Her face was, she hoped, doing what Zofia's face did: nothing at all, in the dark, in a room with the lamp turned down.

"Now," Tomek said. "I've got another thing to say and then I'll go. You know what I do, and Mama knows, and Mama doesn't sleep because of it, and men who know what I do and are caught talk in the cellars and hang on the wall, and I don't ask people to know what I do unless I need them to. I need you." He looked at the floor. "There's a girl who escaped from the country at Stutthof — no, that's not the story, that's another story. What I need: someone who can sit in a café and be a music teacher, with sheet music in her bag, and pass a thing from a man to a man, and be looked at and forgotten, and speak to Germans without her hands shaking. We have nobody like that in the whole city. Everybody useful is a boy with a pistol or a woman in an office, and the boys get caught and the women get followed home." He looked up. "You'd be paid nothing and you'd be dead in three minutes if they take you with a paper in your shoe."

"My daughter is in detail three at Skaryszewska," Helena said, "and I have a hundred and ten zlotys and my father's violin."

"I know," Tomek said. "That's why I'm asking. Because a person with nothing to lose is a fool, and a person with something to lose is a soldier." He put his cap on. "Bar eleven, from the top. Mama says you've been teaching that to the child since she was five. I heard it through the window once. It's a beautiful piece."

"It is a very beautiful piece," Helena said. "It is not the piece's fault that it is difficult."

When he had gone she sat for a long while without lighting the lamp, and then, by touch, in the dark, she lifted the fourth floorboard from the wall of the alcove, where the joist and the brick made a gap the size of a shoebox, and took out the tin.

There was not much in the tin. A certificate from the conservatory in Munich with her name on it in German script. A programme from a recital in the Residenz, in which she had been the second violin, in an orchestra of sixteen, in December 1932, playing Bach for an audience of a hundred and eleven people. A dried rose, flattened, from a garden on the Nymphenburger Straße. Three letters in envelopes, addressed in a young man's careful hand to Fräulein Helena Wojtera, in Warsaw; all three returned unopened, all three with the same stamp, all three with her father's handwriting on the back — *oddać* — give it back — because her father, who was a railway engineer and a proud man, had decided that his daughter was not going to be the kind of person who opened letters from the country that had ruined her.

She had never opened them. She had carried them home in her trunk in the autumn of 1933 with the certificate and the rose, and put them in the tin, and put the tin under the floorboard, and in ten years she had taken the tin out exactly twice: once when the Germans came in 1939 and she needed to know what to burn if the house was searched, and once, at three in the morning, in the winter of 1940, when the child had pneumonia and Helena had stood at her bed and bargained with God.

She sat on the floor with the tin in her lap and thought about the man at the pawnshop, the man with his chin in his collar who had watched her hands; and about the German official called Böhm who drank coffee at the Europejski at eleven; and about the welfare woman's unsmiling diagnosis, delivered in the rain in a courtyard behind a shop with a yellow sign, of the three ways out of a locked room.

And she thought, and this was the thought she had been holding away from herself with both hands all day, about the entry in the register at Skaryszewska in the flat German hand, and about the words *rassische Überprüfung*, and about what a man might do, if a man was a Major in a German office with a signature, and the child was his, and the mother was a liar with a half photograph sewn into the lining of her coat.

She put the tin back under the board and laid the board flat and put the rug over it and stood up and looked at her own room, at the bed that was too big, at the little violin on the shelf with its half-size bow, at the window with the blackout paper on it, at the place on the wall where the child had grown two centimetres in a pencil line in February.

*Give me until Wednesday,* she said to nobody, to the room, to the window, to the city outside with its names on the walls. *Give me until Wednesday, and I swear to you I will not do it.*

Which was, and she knew it as she said it, the kind of promise a woman makes to a mirror, on the first night, before the long war of the heart begins.
