# Chapter Seven: The Photograph

*Warsaw — Thursday, 10 June 1943*

The invitation to the racial examination came on Tuesday in a grey official envelope with no stamp on it, delivered by a Polish postman who had learned, in three years, to hold such envelopes delicately, as if they might go off in his hands.

It said that the examination of the minor Maria Anna Wojtera would take place at the Institute for Racial Hygiene, Aleja Szucha 25A, second floor, room 4, on Thursday 10 June at 10.00 hours; that the mother should attend with the child, if the child were at liberty, or without the child, if not, in which case the child would be examined at the place of detention; that the mother should bring all documents relating to the family history of the child, in particular any documents concerning the father; that statements would be taken under oath; and that non-attendance would be noted and would be considered a refusal to cooperate in the establishment of the racial status of the child, with consequences.

The words *consequences* and *refusal to cooperate* had been printed in the same weight of type as the words *Aleja Szucha* and *10.00 hours*, and Helena had read the whole of it standing in the courtyard at Hoża 14 in the middle of the day, with the loaf in her other hand, and had then gone up to her room and put the envelope on the table and sat down in front of it and looked at it the way a person looks at a train ticket to a country they have heard about.

On Thursday morning she put on her best blouse, and the good grey skirt that she had kept for the conservatory, and her mother's small cameo, which was not valuable and had no gold in it and was therefore still in the family, and she did her hair, which had grown out into a short dark cap with a fringe, and she looked at herself in the little mirror in the corner of the room for a long minute, and she thought, as she had thought every morning for ten years when she had looked into a mirror: *bar eleven, from the top, slowly.*

Then she walked across the city to Aleja Szucha.

The children were examined at ten and the adults at eleven, and so the waiting room on the second floor of the building at Aleja Szucha 25A was, when Helena came into it at a quarter to ten, full of Polish women with children: eleven women and fourteen children, in a room with a bench along the wall, a table with a lamp, a portrait of the Führer with his mouth slightly open, and a smell of new paint over an older smell of mould and cigarettes.

In the middle of the room, on two chairs that had been pushed together, sat three German women in the grey of the Rasse-und-Siedlungswesen service: a typist with a card index on her knee, a nurse, and, standing with her back to the window and her hands folded, a woman of about thirty in a well-cut green suit, with a white blouse and a little bunch of lilac pinned to the lapel and fair hair rolled at the back of her neck, who was looking at the children with a very particular expression.

It was the expression, Helena thought afterwards, of a woman looking at goods in a shop window. Not greed. Not cruelty. Something worse and simpler: the look of a person who had decided what she was going to buy, who had come with the money, and who was now examining the wares with the practised eye of someone who has been told that her money is good for one purchase only.

"Maria Anna Wojtera?" the typist said, reading from her index.

"Here."

"And the child?"

"The child is at Skaryszewska. The labour office has her in the sorting detail."

The nurse and the typist exchanged a look; the woman in green wrote something on a small pad with a gold pencil. And then the typist said, "Both files are here. Take a seat on the bench, and wait." And the woman in the green suit turned round and looked at her, Helena Wojtera of Hoża 14, in her best blouse and her mother's cameo; and a small movement crossed her face, sharper than anything else that would happen to either of them that day, and was gone.

The examination was at eleven, and it took an hour and twenty minutes; and when Helena came out of room 4 into the corridor, with her back cold and her hands shaking and her ears ringing, she walked to the end of the corridor where the window was, because she could not be seen at the window and could not see anybody either, and stood there and put her forehead against the glass, and made herself be breathless two or three times, and then stood straight again and looked at the courtyard below, where two young men in shirtsleeves were unloading a lorry of cardboard files.

The room had been small and hot, with the window shut, because the doctor from Posen did not like draughts; and the doctor from Posen had been a man of about forty-five, a doctor of medicine and philosophy called Alfred Teichmann, with a soft, patient, absent face, and hands like a woman's, and an air of gentle exactness that was the most terrifying thing Helena had ever been examined by. He had had a notebook and a pair of calipers and an eye for skulls; and on the wall behind his desk there had been a chart of the SS race classifications, and on the desk beside the inkstand a small brass instrument for measuring the angle of the nose; and he had said, in the quiet voice, the first sentence, which was: "Pani Wojtera, this examination is not a punishment. It is a service to the child. We are here to establish whether the child's race allows her to be saved from the fate of her mother's people. Please be seated."

He had asked her about her mother and her father, about her grandparents, about their names and dates and places: her father Stanisław Wojtera, born Żoliborz 1878, an engineer on the Wilno line, died 1939; her mother Zofia née Kwiatkowska, born Piaseczno 1882, died 1938; her grandparents, four of them, with their four villages; and about the child's father, about whom she gave him Adam Wójcik, born Grodno 1904, a railwayman, killed by a shunting engine in the spring of 1933, the month before the child was conceived.

"Killed by an engine," Teichmann said, and wrote it down. "In Grodno."

"In the yards at Grodno."

"And the parish register of Grodno would show the burial."

"He was a Calvinist," Helena said. "There was no burial in a Catholic register, and the Calvinist books were taken away by the Russians in 1940. There is nothing. That is the truth of it."

"That is very convenient," the doctor said, gently; and looked at her for a long moment with his soft patient eyes and tapped the brass instrument on the blotter, one small tap, and said: "Now, Pani Wojtera, we will do the part that you were not told about in the letter. Please stand."

The child, of course, was not there. So they had used the mother; and the mother they had put in the middle of the floor and taken to the window, where the light was best, and measured: the height of her, the span of her arms, the length of her skull and the breadth of it, the circumference of the head before and behind, the nasal angle, the shape of the jaw, the colour of the eyes and the colour of the hair and the height of the shin; and they had asked her to put out her hands, palms up, one then the other, and had measured the length of each finger against each other finger; and they had asked her, in the middle of it, to unbutton her blouse for the examination of the chest, which she did, in front of the nurse, and then to turn round, to the light, to the light, and down to the straps, which she refused in Polish, and Teichmann, after a moment, had said, "It is of no consequence. We shall have the child's photograph; the mother's measurements are corroborative only."

Then the photographs: front and both profiles, with her hair held back by the nurse with two fingers. And then he had put the sheet in front of her, the *Familienbogen*, and read out to her the three questions, slowly, in the quiet voice, and had written her answers in his neat German hand.

*German ancestry within three generations: none, to my knowledge.*

*Father of the child: Adam Wójcik, railwayman, of Grodno, deceased.*

*Consent of the mother to examination and to the entry of the child in the register of persons of doubtful race: given.*

And then he had done the last thing, the thing she had not been prepared for, and it was the reason she was standing with her forehead against the window of the corridor of Aleja Szucha 25A with her back cold and her ears ringing: he had taken from the file, and put down flat on the desk between them, side by side, like two cards in a game of patience, both halves of her life.

Two photographs and one card index entry.

The first photograph was of Marysia: taken, she could see at once, in the basement of the labour office at Skaryszewska in the last week of May, three views, front and two profiles, in the striped rag-sorting shirt with the collar open, the shaven head grown out into a dark fuzz, the eyes grey and steady and enormous in the thin face. The second photograph was of Helena herself: one of the three the nurse had taken twenty minutes earlier in front of the window, in which she looked, she thought, like a woman being arrested.

And the card, in the typed German of the labor office, said:

*Wojtera, Maria Anna. b. 3.12.1933, Warsaw. Mother: Wojtera, Helena, b. 1910, Warsaw, violinist, war-employed. Father: allegedly Wójcik, Adam, deceased Grodno 1933. Physical findings: dark hair with a slight wave; grey-blue eyes; oval face; high forehead; stature for age above mean; musical aptitude noted in mother. Photogrammetric comparison of mother and child: the ear form does not correspond. Reference: Rasse-und-Siedlungsamt Posen, petition 2188/43.*

And under that, in Teichmann's own hand, at the bottom of the card, the sentence that Helena had read upside down on the desk before the doctor turned it round with two fingers, the way a man turns round a card he is about to play:

*Übereinstimmung geprüft — kein. Fall vorläufig offen.*

*Correspondence checked — none. Case provisionally open.*

He had let her read it. That was the thing: he had turned the card to her for one second, deliberately, gently, like a nurse showing a patient the wound; and then he had said, in the quiet, patient voice, "You may dress. The child will be examined at the place of detention on the twenty-fourth. You will be informed of the finding. Do you have anything to say to me?"

And Helena had stood in the middle of the room with her blouse in her hands and her back to the chart of the race classifications on the wall, and had said: "Only that the child's father was a Polish railwayman of Grodno, and that if you write anything else in that register, you will be writing a lie in a book that will outlive both of us." Which was, she understood at once from the flicker of surprise in his soft eyes, the single most dangerous sentence she had ever said to a German, and the only sentence in the whole hour and twenty minutes that had been entirely true.

Now the woman in the green suit came along the corridor and stopped in front of the window, and stood looking out at the courtyard, and the two of them were side by side with a metre of polished floor between them.

"You are the mother," the woman said, in German.

"I am."

"Then we shall not pretend. It is always better not to pretend; I discovered that in Posen." She had a small gold pencil in her fingers, and she turned it over and over, and her hands were white and cared-for and shaking, very finely, the way a person's hands shake after a long wait. "The petition in the file at the bottom of your daughter's card is mine. Petition 2188. I made it a year ago, after the armistice in the east was announced and we had a Te Deum in Posen, and my husband was killed in Russia, and my brother-in-law's little boy died that same winter, and there has been no child in the family since; and I said to the office that if a suitable child of German blood were found in the General Government, I would want to be considered. That was a year ago. I did not know your name. Nobody knows the names in the file; the file has numbers in it, and the numbers have photographs when they are old enough, and I have had the photograph of the girl in the striped shirt on my table for eleven days."

The corridor was empty. At the far end, behind the closed door of room 4, a woman was crying with the flat, unending monotony of a person whose crying has become a place she lives in.

"What do you want?" Helena said.

"I want to say two things to you, and then I shall go and we shall never speak again, and it will be as if I had never spoken." The woman's hands went on turning the pencil. "The first thing is: the file says *correspondence checked — none*. That means the doctor found that the girl's ear does not match your ear, and he has written it down, and a doctor's note of a physical discrepancy in a doubtful case goes to Posen, and the men who sit in Posen with the papers have a waiting list of one hundred and eleven families. The girl's photograph is on that list now, and so is mine; mine is at the top, because I have been waiting a year, and the others know it, and they are angry about it, and last week somebody wrote to Berlin about me." She turned the pencil over. "The second thing is a question, and if you answer it truthfully, you may save the child's life; and if you lie to me I will know, because I have spent four hours in a room with your daughter's photograph. Is the father of that girl a German?"

Helena looked at the courtyard below, at the two young men unloading the files, at the lorry with the blackout lamps, at the small patch of pale June sky over Warsaw.

"No," she said.

"No." The woman nodded, slowly, as though she had been given a piece of paper with a sum on it and had checked the sum and found it correct. "Then I shall tell you the thing you are going to need, and you will remember, if you live, that it was given to you by a woman you do not know, in bad faith, out of decency, and that those three things can be true at the same time." She put the gold pencil into her bag. "There is a charitable organization in the General Government: the Zentrale für Kinderfürsorge, a woman named Frau Doktor Fischer runs its office in Kraków. Every child on the Posen list who passes the examination and is found German comes to the Zentrale before it goes to a family, and every child whose case remains open — like your daughter's — is transferred to the Zentrale's custody when the labour office closes the case with the note *kein* on it. The Zentrale's custody is a building on Krakowskie Przedmieście, a very clean building, with a very good kitchen, where the children are held for four to twelve weeks, and it is the only institution in the whole apparatus where a child's file can be made to go quietly to sleep, because the Zentrale is run by a woman who counts nothing. If you want your daughter to live, she must get to that building; and the way through that building is not a mother's tears, it is a man's signature; and you look to me like a woman who has found herself a man's signature."

And she turned and walked back down the corridor in her well-cut green suit, with the lilac on her lapel, and went down the stairs and out; and Helena stood at the window for two minutes more, and then she went down the stairs and out of the building herself, past the great door and the sentry box and the dark green trees of Aleja Szucha, into the ordinary June morning, and walked west towards the centre of the city; and it was at the corner of Marszałkowska that she noticed the black car.

She noticed it the way a person notices a thing they have already been waiting for: a black pre-war Adler saloon standing at the curb with its engine running and the driver at the wheel and the windows down, in a street where a car standing at the curb was an event. It had been behind her, she thought, at the corner of Bagatela: she had heard it, and had not turned her head. It had passed her at Plac Zbawiciela and then slowed, and now it was standing.

She walked on. She crossed Marszałkowska at the tram stop, and went up towards Krucza with her head down, and the car came alongside and kept pace with her, and the rear window came down, and a man's voice said, in German: "Pani Wojtera."

She did not break step. She looked round because a person to whom a black car speaks must look round; and inside the car was a man of about thirty-six in a grey civilian overcoat and a brown hat, with a long pale face and a small mustache and a folder of buff folders on the seat beside him, and he was eating a piece of chocolate, and he broke off another piece and offered it to her through the window in its silver paper.

"Obersturmführer Dörffel," he said. "Security Police, Department Four. You need not take the chocolate; most people do not, and I do not mind; I am told I am sinister with chocolate." He broke the piece off, small and tidy. "I have been reading about you this morning. The Institute sent us a note, which is a very proper thing for the Institute to do, because a signature from the Wehrmacht came into the Wojtera file in the last week of May, and when a signature from the Wehrmacht comes into a file in this city, my department is required to consider whether it was a mistake. Almost always it is a mistake. A clerk in the Wehrmacht office is told by a friend, and he puts his signature on a paper, and the paper goes into a drawer, and no one ever looks at it, and the whole thing is a mistake, and it dies quietly." He looked at her with pale eyes that were neither friendly nor unfriendly, and his voice was the voice of a man who has once been a lawyer and has never forgotten how to sound reasonable. "But this paper was not a mistake, Pani Wojtera, and I am looking at you, and you are not a mistake either. So may I ask you, unofficially, in the street, on a Thursday: what business does an unmarried Polish music teacher who was released from a transit camp on the twenty-second of May have with a Major of the Wehrmacht commandant's office in this city?"

The tram went past, full, with a German sitting on the back platform with his legs dangling; and two boys went by on bicycles, and a woman with a pram, and a man selling cigarettes out of a box slung round his neck, and the city went on its way around them in the June sun.

Helena looked into the car at the man in the brown hat, and felt, all at once, not fear but a curious cold steadiness, the feeling of a person who has been working all her life for a thing and now hears the key turn in the lock.

"He was an old friend of my father's," she said.

"An old friend of your father's," Dörffel repeated. He did not smile; he wrote, or seemed to write, nothing; he ate the last of the chocolate in two bites, folded the silver paper into a square and put it into his pocket, like a man tidying his desk. "Very well. I am going to do something now that I should not do, and you may take it however you like, and if you mention it to anyone I shall deny it." He leaned forward so that his shoulder was against the window frame, and for a moment, with the sun on it, his long pale face looked almost kind. "The Wehrmacht office on Nowy Świat is going to receive a letter from me asking for the Wojtera file. I already know what the file says; I have the copy. What I want to see is what the Major does with the letter. You may think about that however you please; there is no answer to it. What matters to you — and this is the only thing I have said today that I mean — is that a Polish woman who becomes the reason for anything in this city becomes a file, and a file is read by everybody, and in this city a file that has been read by everybody is a wall with names on it." He tapped the window frame twice with two fingers, the way one knocks on wood. "Good day to you, Pani Wojtera. Give my compliments to your daughter's teacher of music."

The window went up. The car pulled out into the traffic of Marszałkowska and turned right towards the river, and Helena stood at the edge of the pavement, in the middle of the flowing current of people that goes up and down that street all day, and heard her own heart, and looked at her own two hands, which were perfectly steady.

Then she thought, with a clarity that felt like a hand closing round her throat:

*He said: give my compliments to your daughter's teacher of music.*

*Marysia has no teacher of music.*

*Unless it is the one who comes to the wire on Tuesdays and Saturdays, and stands in the visitors' yard with the guard five metres away, and says nothing for a whole hour except the number of a bar.*

*Warsaw — Saturday, 12 June 1943*

The man from the pawnshop called at the laundry on the Saturday, which took a certain kind of nerve, and he did it in the right way, which Helena noted: he came at the change of the shift, in the crowd, and he stood at the gate with a newspaper, and when she came out he fell in beside her and walked with her as though he were her cousin from the country, and said, before she had even decided to look at him:

"Don't stop walking and don't put your hand in your bag. I'm not going to rob you; I'm going to make you an offer, and you are going to want to hear it, because I already know one thing about you that you would pay to keep quiet, and I have not sold it, and I have not sold it because I am a businessman and not a butcher, and the difference between a businessman and a butcher is that the businessman wants you alive next year."

"You know nothing about me," Helena said.

"I know that your child is at Skaryszewska in the sorting detail, which half of Hoża Street knows; and I know from a clerk at the labour office — I buy him cigarettes, he tells me nothing, it's the other way round: he tells me everything and I buy him cigarettes — that a Wehrmacht Major in the Referat has written your daughter into a labour plan, and no Wehrmacht Major writes a child of nine into a labour plan. So what I know," said Wacek Górski, "is that there is a German officer in this city who has a reason to do something about your family, and in this city a reason is a thing that people pay to hear about, and there are two prices at which I could sell it. The cheap price is to the men who buy names for Szucha: eight hundred zlotys and a receipt, and they would have your daughter out of the camp in a week and you would never see her again, or you would see her once, at an address in Aleja Szucha, and then never. The expensive price is to you: I keep my mouth shut about the Major, and about the child, and about the woman from the welfare office with the boots, and I do it for a modest standing arrangement."

They walked past the tram stop and past the church. Helena looked straight ahead, at the grey street, at two Polish policemen on the corner with their hands behind their backs, at the queue outside the butcher's shop, at the wet paper in the gutter.

"How much?"

"Two thousand, now; four hundred a month after; and I will tell you what you are buying: silence, and, more useful to you, information. I am a bad man, Pani Wojtera, and a bad man in this city hears things, and the things I hear are for sale to my clients before they are for sale to Szucha, because Szucha pays in favours and clients pay in gold. Also I will buy the violin."

"What violin?"

"The Hopf. Your father's. You pawned the watch on the twenty-second of May at Kostecki's and you kept the violin, and the man at Kostecki's mentioned it because men who pawn watches at Kostecki's always have a violin somewhere, and I looked it up in the register of the Szymanowski school: the instrument was entered by your late father, Stanisław Wojtera, engineer, Wilno line, first violin, and the school's register notes it as a Hopf of about 1790 with a repaired wing. I'll give you eighteen hundred for it and you can keep it in the flat and play it on Sundays, because what I want is the paper, not the wood; I sell the paper to a man in Kraków who sells it back to the estate of the family it was stolen from in 1939. Everything in this city is stolen twice. That is the whole of my philosophy."

"Eighteen hundred zlotys for a violin worth six thousand."

"Eighteen hundred for a violin worth six thousand, and I pay it in a week, and you keep the violin, and the money is real, and it is the only offer of real money you will get this year that does not come with a German in it." He stopped walking. They were at the corner of Krucza and Hoża, and the man from the pawnshop took off his hat and looked at her with a small, honest, entirely conscienceless face. "Think about it until Thursday. But think about the first price too, Pani Wojtera, because the first price is the price of a thing I already know, and I am going to go on being a businessman on Thursday whether you have made a decision or not."

She walked home. She went up to her room and opened the case, and took out her father's violin, which she could only play at night with the window shut and a cloth in the f-holes, and held it in her two hands, and looked at the wing, which had been repaired in 1921 by a man in Wilno with a piece of wood from a church door, and thought about how many things in a person's life can be sold before the person herself is sold, and made a small, exact, practical list:

The violin: she would not sell it. That was the only thing on the list that was not negotiable, and not for sentiment: because the child needed to know, whatever else was taken from her, that her mother had kept something; and because a violin in the house was a future, and there was no other object in Warsaw in 1943 that could be called that.

The rest of the list was: the room, the furniture, the coat, the ring, herself.

She did not have a plan yet. But she had, from that afternoon, the beginning of the only kind of plan that anyone in Warsaw had in the summer of 1943, which was a ledger: a page on which she wrote what she still had, and what each thing was worth, and what each thing could buy, and in what order, and from whom, and the leaving of a space at the bottom of the page for the price that had no name yet, the price that she would pay to the man in the brown hat or to the man with the notebook or to the Major in the grey tunic, when the day came that the child's name was the only object she owned that anybody in the Reich was willing to buy.

***

*Skaryszewska — Thursday, 24 June 1943*

They came for her at the breakfast hour, and it was, Marysia thought, the best time of the day, because at breakfast the bread was warm and you could sit next to Fela and put the crusts in her pocket for her, so of course that was when they came.

The room over the gate was three and a half metres by two, with a barred window and a table and two chairs and a plaster saint's head in a niche, from which a school child had long ago scratched out both eyes, and Marysia had seen it from below in the yard and had thought it was the prison, and now here she was in it, sitting on the chair with her feet not touching the floor, and there was nobody else in the room except a German nurse with a card of printed numbers and a man in a grey uniform with a long thin nose and soft white hands who was called Herr Doktor and who had brought a bag with instruments in it.

The nurse measured her. The doctor sat in the other chair and watched, and asked questions in a low voice, and wrote the answers in a notebook, and the things he measured went into a printed card with squares, and the squares were for the length of her skull and the width of her skull and the length of her middle finger and the height of her forehead and the angle of her nose, and when the measuring instrument was put on her nose the metal was cold, and Marysia thought about the buttons: *eins, zwei, drei, vier*, and did not move, and did not laugh, and did not cry.

"Now the eyes," the nurse said.

"*Bitte*," the doctor said. "The child is quick; let her answer in German; it is a good sign for the file. Your name?"

"Maria Anna Wojtera."

"A Polish name. And the German for what you are doing in the camp?" He said it gently, spreading his long hands. "The work?"

"*Sortieren*. *Knöpfe*." Marysia said it flat and dull, the voice of a girl repeating the shouted orders of guards: "*Eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf, sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn.* I can do it fast if you want it fast."

The doctor's pen stopped. He looked at her, and for the first time he looked at her as a person, which was worse than all the instruments, because when he looked at her as a person he had a face, and the face was patient and kindly, and it said, without any words at all: *I am going to open you up now.*

"Who taught you to count like that?"

"Nobody. I learn from the guards."

"And who taught you the words in German?"

"Nobody."

"Child, you spoke German in the yard last week. I have the note. You said a whole sentence to the girl with the fever: *du musst Wasser trinken*. Somebody taught you that."

"Nobody. The doctor is mistaken. Fela doesn't speak, and I don't speak, and the sentence was Polish."

"The sentence was in German, and there is a note, and it is in the file; and in the file there are also your measurements, and the photograph of your mother, and the note of the Institute in the case of your mother, and the note says that the ear in the photograph of the mother does not correspond to the ear of the child; and there is a card of my colleague's, in Posen, which says that a child of this age with this brow and these hands sings on pitch." He put down the pen and folded his hands, and his voice was quiet and entirely reasonable. "Maria Anna, I am not a policeman. I am a doctor. Nobody in this building is going to hurt you, and whatever you tell me here will not be used against your mother. Now: who was your father?"

"Adam Wójcik," she said. "He was a railwayman. He died before I was born."

The doctor looked at her for a long time, and then he smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has finally confirmed an arithmetic. "Yes," he said, and picked up the pen, and wrote, and the nib went over the paper in long clean strokes. "Every child in Warsaw says that sentence with exactly your face. It is the best-taught sentence in the General Government."

He gave her a chocolate — a real one, in silver paper — and she did not eat it, and did not throw it away. She carried it in the pocket of the grey shirt for four days, and in the end she gave it to Fela, who did not eat it either, and who put it, with the other things she had been given in her life — a pencil, a button, a piece of blue glass, a doll stitched out of army cloth — in the nest she had made under the corner of her mattress; so that the chocolate of the Institut für Rassenhygiene went, in the end, into the archive of a three-year-old child.

Going down the stairs, past the door of the camp office, Marysia looked into the outer room through the open door, and there were three German women sitting on the bench with their gloves on their laps, and one of them was a woman in a green suit with a small bunch of lilac pinned to her collar, who had been in the office corridor when they brought Marysia in, and who looked at her now as she went past with an expression that Marysia did not understand and would remember for the rest of her life: the expression of a person looking at a window in a house they cannot afford, in a street they will walk down every day for years.
