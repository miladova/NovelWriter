# Chapter Ten: The House of Wolves

*Falkenhorst, near Küstrin — Wednesday, 22 December 1943*

The estate lay on the low ground by the Oder, where the river branched and the water stood in the meadows all winter and the frost came up out of the ground in long white feathers, and the house was a low, long, solid Prussian house of 1812 with a pillared porch and a hundred and ninety years of additions at the back, and it had been in the family for four generations, and it was, Ilse von Brandt thought, standing at her window on the morning of the twenty-second with a shawl round her shoulders, exactly as beautiful as it had been when she had first come there as a bride in the spring of 1935, and exactly as cold.

In the yard the Polish labourers were bringing in the last of the firewood for the holiday: the Scharwerk men from the village, in their coats and their wooden shoes, with the sledges, and the foreman, Kaczmarek, who had been the estate's foreman since 1911 and who had a son in the Polish army in England and a daughter who cleaned in the house and was, everybody assumed, the reason the silver had not been stolen; and there was snow on the roofs and smoke on the air and, from the kitchen wing, the smell of the goose that had been hanging since Sunday and the good hot smell of the bread.

Ilse stood at the window for a long time.

She was thirty-eight years old. She had been born Ilse von Woyrsch, of a family with more name than acres, in Stettin, in a house with a good piano and a set of Heine that her father kept in the back of the bookcase; she had married Konrad von Brandt, the eldest, the one with the laugh, in 1935, and they had had the ground floor at Falkenhorst and a flat in Berlin and no children, and in the autumn of 1939 Konrad had gone to the war in Poland with his regiment and had come home on leave at Christmas and had been unable to sleep; and in June 1941 his regiment had gone east, and in July there had been a letter in his handwriting from a place with no name that Ilse had looked up in an atlas and not found; and on the fourteenth of September 1941, at a supper party in Posen, an officer of the Ordnungspolizei who had had too much wine had said, in front of nine people, "The Kamerad of the cavalry — Konrad von Brandt — my condolences, Frau Brandt," before anybody had told her; and she had gone home, and telephoned, and on the twentieth of September the telegram had come, and it had said KILLED IN ACTION IN THE EAST, and there had never been a grave.

That was two years and three months ago. She had learned to live with a hole in the middle of her life, the way you learn to live in a house with a tree through the roof: you moved round it, you stopped seeing it, and then three or four times a year something fell out of it. She had learned the German widow's ritual: the black, the year of the black, the deliberate cheerfulness of the fourth year, the photographs, the letters, the field post, and the silence; and she had learned, in those two years, the thing she had never told a living soul, which was that grief is not the worst of it. The worst of it was the emptiness of the days, which had no shape left at all: nothing to be got up for, nothing to be counted on, no child at the table, no reason to be careful with the household money, no reason to keep up with anybody, no reason, she thought sometimes, to eat.

She had wanted a child since 1936, and had spent four years in the indifferent hands of a professor in Berlin who had explained the mechanics of her body to her with a brass instrument, and had spent 1940 and 1941 at Bad Nauheim taking the waters and the injections, and had taken, in the end, the advice of the wife of a party official, which was that a woman of her family and background, whose husband had fallen for the Reich, need not necessarily adopt a child from an institution of the sort one associates with the cities. There were children, the official's wife said, of good blood, whose parents had been lost in the East; there were lists.

Ilse had put her name on a list in September 1941. She had done it in the way she did everything: quietly, with a form, and a letter of support from her father-in-law, and an attestation of her own ancestry as far back as 1750, which had taken a lawyer in Stettin six weeks. And then she had waited, and mended the linen, and organized the Christmas parcels for the men from the estate who were in the army, and read the letters that came and the lists of the dead in the newspaper, and been, on the surface, a decent, careful, useful woman in a house full of people, with nothing to hold on to at all.

And then, in the last week of May 1943, a clerk at the Rasse- und Siedlungsamt in Posen had written to her to say that a case had become available, in the Warsaw district, which might in his view correspond to the particulars of her application; that the case was a female child of nine years of age, of suspected German descent on the paternal side, and that her name was not to be given to the applicant, as the finding was not complete; and that a photograph could be seen, under the rules, on one of the days when the office received applicants, in Posen, on the second floor.

Ilse had gone to Posen on the eighth of June and had stood in a corridor with three other women, all older than herself, all of them in black, and had been given, for eleven minutes, in a room where a typist was in the corner and a lamp was on a table, one photograph of a girl of nine in a striped shirt with a shaven head and grey eyes.

She had gone home on the train with her hands folded in her lap, and had not been able to eat in the dining car, and had looked out at the Warthegau going by — the great confiscated farms, the neat German settlements with the names changed on the signposts, the Polish labourers coming back from the fields in the last light — and she had understood, in that hour, something about herself that she had not known before, which was that she was going to want that child more than she had ever wanted anything, and that a woman in her position who wants something that much should be watched, because a woman in her position had the paper and the office and the Reich behind her, and paper works.

She had, that autumn, in fact, done one more thing. She had gone to Warsaw.

It had taken her three days there in October, and she had not told the family, and she had told Erika that she was visiting a cousin in Dresden; and she had stayed in the Hotel Bristol, where the Germans stayed, and eaten in the dining room among officers, and heard the piano play; and she had gone to Aleja Szucha 25A with a letter from the office in Posen, and had sat in a corridor with the others, and had been given, for a quarter of an hour, in a small hot room, by a doctor with soft white hands and a theory of ears, the whole file of Maria Anna Wojtera, including, in a folder, the letter which she was permitted to read but not to copy, and which made her sit in a taxi afterwards on Aleja Szucha with her gloves in her hands and her heart beating in her throat, because the letter was in the file and the letter was from the child's own father's family — her own family, the von Brandts, in the person of her father-in-law, the Generalmajor, and it was dated 1933, and it was not the sort of letter that was ever supposed to have become a file, and the hand that had signed the receipt for it, at Szucha in 1943, was the hand of the same doctor who had written a note about the child's ear; and the letter was in the folder because the Reich, in the fifth year of the war, was now collecting everything.

So when the family came to Falkenhorst for Christmas 1943, Ilse knew a great deal more than any of them; and she carried the photograph of Maria Anna Wojtera in the back of her writing case, in a paper envelope, between the blotter and the stamps.

*Posen, the week before Christmas*

The house on the Karlstrasse had been built in 1905 for a Polish merchant called Wojciechowski, who had made his money in timber on the Warta, and it had a good staircase and a conservatory and a garden with a lime walk, and Ilse von Brandt had lived in it since the autumn of 1941, when the office had allotted it to her as the widow of an officer of the Reich: four rooms and the garden, with the merchant's furniture still in it because the merchant had gone, with his wife and his two daughters, to the General Government in a sealed railway car in November 1939, and because furniture is heavy and the war was long and nobody had come for it.

The merchant's piano was still in the drawing room. It was a good one: a Bechstein, out of tune since 1939, with the candle brackets and the yellowed keys, and Ilse played it twice a week, badly, in the evenings, because playing an instrument that belonged to somebody else's disappearing life was, she had found, the only thing in the house that made her feel anything; and once, in the second winter, she had opened the little lid under the keyboard and found, on the felt, a child's exercise book with the name *Jadwiga Wojciechowska, klasa III* in it, and the sums done in pencil in a careful hand; and she had sat on the piano stool with the book in her lap for a quarter of an hour, and then she had put it back on the felt under the keys, in the same place, and had gone on playing the same waltz.

There were three servants in the house, because a widow of a fallen officer with an application before the Rasse- und Siedlungsamt was entitled to three: an old German woman from Stettin who did the linen and the accounts, and two Polish girls from the resettled villages, Genia and Wanda, who did everything else, and whose family lived in the town and were permitted to be there because their father was a skilled man and the office had left the skilled trades of Posen alone in 1940 and had come back for them in 1943. Genia was nineteen. She had been at school with the merchant's younger daughter, and she did not know where her friend was, and she had never once in four years asked Ilse a question about any of it, and she made the beds and put the flowers in the drawing room and sang under her breath in the kitchen in Polish, and she ironed Ilse's black dresses with a precision that was, Ilse understood perfectly, a form of judgment.

On the Monday before Christmas, Ilse came home at four from the office, and Genia met her in the hall with her hands folded and said, in German, the way she always spoke in the hall, "Frau Brandt, the post has come. There is a parcel from Falkenhorst and there is a letter from the Amt, and I have put the letter on the desk in the study."

It was a thin official envelope from the Rasse- und Siedlungsamt in the Warthegau, reference 2188/43, and it said, in three sentences, that her application regarding a female child of the Warsaw district was under active processing; that in consequence of the entry of a caveat by an office in Warsaw of which the undersigned did not at present know the detail, the case would not be determined before February 1944; and that the applicant should hold herself in readiness to receive the child for a probationary period of six months at fourteen days' notice.

Ilse read it standing at the desk with her coat still on.

Fourteen days' notice. In the new year, perhaps in February, a car would come from the station, and a woman from the League of German Girls would bring a child up the path, and the child would be ten years old, and she would have dark hair and grey eyes and hands that played on the table; and the child would come into the drawing room and see the Bechstein, and be told that she was Frau Brandt's niece from a family of the Reich, and that her parents had died in a raid; and she would be given the bedroom at the end of the corridor, which had been papered in blue in 1905 for the merchant's younger daughter and repapered in the small once more in November, because Ilse had done it herself, with her own hands, in the week when the merchant's daughter's wallpaper had begun to look, suddenly and unpardonably, like the wallpaper of somebody else's life.

She sat down at the desk and put her hand over her mouth, and wept for a minute, quietly, the way she had learned to weep in the years since 1941, with the door shut and the curtains straight.

Then she took the letter upstairs and put it in the writing case with the photograph, and came down, and ate a supper of liver sausage and potato, and went into the drawing room, and opened the piano, and played, in honour of a thing that had not yet happened, the same waltz, very badly, twice.

The house filled up on the twenty-third.

The Krämers came first, from Posen, in the big Mercedes with the SS pennant, which Hermann always took off at the gate of the estate, a courtesy to the Generalmajor's opinions about the party; and Margarete, who was Grete, who was Friedrich's sister and 34 and had the practical energy of a farmer's wife, came in with her arms full of parcels and the two children ahead of her stamping the snow off their boots in the hall; and Wolf, who was nine, immediately began to be a storm on the staircase, and Dorle, who was six, sat down in the middle of the hall floor to take her boots off and had to be carried; and the house, which had been quiet for months, filled up in ten minutes with the whole apparatus: the wet wool in the boot room, the smell of the fir tree being carried through the hall in the arms of the foreman, the argument about whose turn it was to wind the clock, the singing of the third verse of the carol by the cook in the kitchen.

Erika arrived at four in the afternoon with the mail car from Küstrin, a small brown suitcase and a bunch of hothouse carnations, and she was, as always, correct: in her black, with the brooch, with the suitcase packed in the way that only a woman who has been a soldier's wife for six years packs a suitcase; and she kissed Ilse on both cheeks and said the right things, and went upstairs, and did not come down for two hours, and when she came down the little redness round her eyes was gone, and she was ready for the evening; and Ilse, who had lived in the same house with Erika for the first two years, knew exactly where she had been for those two hours: in the room at the end of the corridor, the nursery, which Erika kept in order, with the little bed made up and the toys on the shelf and the photograph of Konrad — the little one, Friedrich and Erika's boy, who had died at three in February 1941 of meningitis, in the week when his father was on the road between Lodz and Warsaw and came home too late — and the dust on the little horse because nobody but Erika was permitted to touch the horse.

That evening, when the house was asleep and the last of the kettles had been filled in the kitchen, Ilse went up the corridor to the end, because she did it every Christmas, and because it was the only thing she knew how to give the woman in the room beyond it.

Erika was sitting in the little chair by the little bed.

The room had a nightlight in a china shoe, because Erika kept it burning, and the bed was made up with the counterpane turned back, and the horse and the wooden animals and the ball were on the shelf in the order in which a three-year-old puts away his own toys, which is to say no order at all; and Erika had the photograph in her hands, and she was not looking at it, she was simply holding it, the way a person holds a stone in the sea.

"I cannot be at this dinner," Erika said, in the flat voice.

"You can. You have been at eleven dinner parties since 1941 and every one of them was worse than this one will be."

"That is not what I mean." Erika put the photograph down on the little bed, face down, which was the custom of the house, because in that room photographs were never allowed to look at anybody. "I mean I cannot be here at all, in this house, at this table, being a German wife whose husband is on duty. Every year they ask me why Friedrich does not take leave, and I have learned to say that the Referat is short of officers, and every year your father-in-law looks at me as though he knows that I am lying and is grateful for it." She looked up. "Do you know why he does not come?"

"I think I do," Ilse said.

"Then you know more than I do, and I live with it." Erika's face did not change; she had the face of a woman of thirty-two who has been taught by three years of a German Christmas never to change her face in front of the family. "I am not a fool, Ilse. I am a wife with a room in her house that has a dead child in it, and I know perfectly well that my husband has been, since the spring, doing something that a man of his name should not be doing, and I have not asked him about it because I am afraid of the answer; and this week Grete has been looking at me across a piano the way a woman looks at a woman whose husband has a photograph in his writing case." She smoothed the counterpane, one small stroke. "So. When the war is over, one of three things will be true. Either Friedrich has done something honourable and it will cost us everything; or he has done something dishonourable and it will cost us everything; or he has done nothing at all and we shall have to go on living with the kind of man who could do neither. I do not know which of the three frightens me more, and I have not slept properly since the middle of June, and I would rather be at this table tomorrow with you than be anywhere else in the world, because you are the only person in this family who has ever looked at me as though I were a person and not a room."

Ilse sat on the floor with her back against the wall, with the nightlight between them, and said the truth, or half of the truth, which was all she had ever said to anyone: "I have applied for a child. There is a girl in Warsaw, and the papers are moving, and I think that in the spring there will be a car from the station."

Erika looked at her for a long moment. The little horse on the shelf cast a horse's shadow on the wall, and the snow ticked at the window.

"A girl from Warsaw," Erika said. "Then you had better tell me her name, because I am the only one in this family who will still be able to say it out loud in twenty years, and somebody in this house ought to know the name."

Ilse told her. And Erika, who had not wept in front of another human being since October 1941, sat by the nightlight in her dead son's room and took hold of Ilse von Brandt's hand and held it while the name was said, and said afterwards, quite steadily, in the voice of a woman giving a diagnosis: "Then God help all of us, and God help the child, and God help my husband; because in this family we are all of us doing the same thing now, and it is called wanting something, and it never ends well in the letters of a Prussian."

The Generalmajor was seventy-three, and he was in his study with the door open, listening to the house fill with his family, and he came out to dinner in his old black tunic without the badges, because he had worn the badges off, and made the sign of the cross at his place for form's sake in a house where no one had gone to church since 1918, and sat at the head of his table with his four grandchildren's grandchildren's worth of food in front of him — the goose, the red cabbage, the potatoes — and said grace, which he did twice a year, and then said, in his dry voice, lifting his glass:

"To those who are not at this table."

And they drank; and there were five of them who were not at the table, and they all knew their names, and nobody said them.

Friedrich was not at the table, and that was talked about, in the way that the family talked about Friedrich, which was with a mixture of formality and grievance: he had sent a telegram — *Duty forbids; the Referat must be manned through the holiday; God bless the house; Friedrich* — by the field post, fifteen words; and Grete said, "He is always on duty. He has been on duty since 1933," and the Generalmajor said nothing, and Erika looked at her plate, and Hermann Krämer said, "We are all on duty, Grete," and turned the subject, because Hermann was in the habit of turning subjects.

At the table, after the goose, the family talked as German families talked at Christmas 1943: the talk that the war in the East was a war of the world against Bolshevism, and that the Bolshevists were not men in the way that we are men, and that the Führer had had no choice after 1933, and that the bombed-out families in Hamburg were being well cared for, and that the English were flying at Christmas to kill our wives and children deliberately, and that the Americans were rich but would lose interest, and that the war would end in the spring, or in the autumn, or not in our lifetime; and mixed with all of it was the ordinary, tender machinery of a prosperous family at a festival: the request to pass the salt, the smallest child's cough, the card game after supper, the servants in the corridor with the plates, and the silver, and the candles burning down.

Hermann Krämer was forty-five years old, from a family of Hesse schoolmasters, with a doctorate in law and a face like a good-natured hawk and careful hands, and he was, in the Rasse- und Siedlungswesen in Posen, a man of real importance: it was Hermann who organized the ethnic screening of the annexed territories in the Warthegau, who determined which Polish families were to be registered in lists one to four, which were allowed to remain, and which were to be resettled; it was Hermann who had signed, on the third of May 1942, the order that had moved the Kaczmareks' neighbors and their four children and their father's grave to the Lublin district; and it was Hermann who, in the sitting room at Falkenhorst on the twenty-third of December 1943, with the schnapps and the cigars, explained the whole business to his father-in-law, the Generalmajor, in the voice of a farmer explaining a harrow.

"It is a public health measure," he said. "That is what the Führer's judgment amounts to. We have a hundred thousand children in the annexed territories of mixed blood. They cannot stay Polish, because a child who is Polish in Germany is a sickness in the state; they cannot go to Poland, because most of Poland as an entity will not exist. So the office separates the good blood from the bad, and the good blood goes into German families, where it will grow up German and useful; and the rest goes where the rest goes. The proof that the system works is the work itself. A child of eleven from Łódź, screened and placed in 1941, wins the prize at the school in Posen this year for German composition." He turned his brandy glass. "I do not expect you to like the details, Father. You were in the old army; the old army preferred to look away. That was a luxury of an age when the state did not have to make judgments. Now it does, and somebody has to be the man with the signature."

"Somebody does indeed," said the Generalmajor, dryly, and looked into the fire.

And Ilse sat in the corner with her embroidery, because she always sat in the corner with her embroidery, and listened to all of it, and felt the envelope in her writing case, in the room above, as though it were a lamp burning somewhere in the house.

It was Grete who did it. Not Hermann, and not the Generalmajor, and not Erika, but Grete, at the piano on the afternoon of the twenty-fourth, when the house was full of the smell of the fir tree and the shortbread and the two boys had been sent up to wash and Dorle was on the floor with the wooden horses and the room was, in that arbitrary way of houses at Christmas, happy.

Grete was playing; she was the one in the family who played, badly and cheerfully, and she had just finished singing the same carol three times, and into the warm room, over her shoulder, she said, in a voice pitched for nothing at all: "Ilse. Show Erika the girl."

The room did not stop. That was the terrible thing: a Christmas room does not stop. The Generalmajor went on looking into the fire, and Hermann went on pouring, and Erika, in the corner, looked up from the piece of work in her hands with a polite expression.

"I don't know what Grete means," Ilse said.

"The photograph," Grete said, and turned on the piano stool, and smiled, and it was a sister's smile, and there was nothing in it but interest and a certain impatience with other people's secretiveness. "The photograph in your writing case, which I have seen twice in your hand since October, and which you do not know you have looked at under a lamp the way a woman looks at a piece of jewelry. If the family is going to talk about the girl from Warsaw for four days, let us all look at her."

Ilse sat quite still for three seconds with her embroidery in her lap, and then she got up and went out of the room and up the stairs, and came down with the envelope, and took out the photograph, and put it on the small table between the lamp and the bowl of nuts, and the family looked at it.

A girl of nine in a striped shirt with the sleeves rolled, standing against a grey wall, with her hair grown out into dark fuzz and her hands at her sides and her eyes straight at the camera, and a chin held up.

There was silence for a moment. And then Erika, who had not spoken for the whole day except to say the right things, leaned forward a little and looked at the photograph for the length of four or five heartbeats, and said, in a voice quite without colour, the sentence that turned the house of wolves over on its back:

"She has Friedrich's eyes."

The Generalmajor's fire-irons stopped. Out of the corner of her eye Ilse saw Hermann Krämer look up from the decanter with the whole of his mild face turned to stone, and she saw the Generalmajor a.D. Ewald von Brandt, seventy-three years old, the man who had commanded a division in 1917 and a garrison in 1939 and who had buried a son and a grandson, go on looking at the fire, and his hands stay perfectly still on the arms of his chair, and his eyes never leave the flame, and know everything.

"Well," Grete said, after a moment, in the cheerful voice of a woman who wants to put a room back on its feet. "Half the family has the same eyes. The Woyrsch eyes; my father-in-law always said it." And she began to play again, and the carol went on, and Hermann poured the schnapps, and Dorle drove her wooden horses across the rug, and the room resumed, in the way that rooms resume, the appearance of a Christmas at home in Prussia in the fifth year of the war.

Nobody spoke about it again, in the house, for two days; but Ilse, lying in her bed above the hall with the snow ticking against the window, knew that a family, having seen a thing, never unsees it; and on the morning of the twenty-sixth, at the breakfast table, Hermann Krämer said, in his gentle, reasonable, careful-man's voice, the following:

"Friedrich is in Warsaw. The file with the girl's photographs is in the district office in Posen and the Institute in Warsaw, and the girl is a case of doubtful racial ancestry, and the case is open. I have looked at the case myself. There is a signature of a Wehrmacht office on the child's reserve papers, and the signature is going to have to be explained by somebody, and if it is not explained by somebody in Berlin it will be explained by somebody at Szucha, and one way or another somebody in this family is going to be standing in a room explaining it." He buttered his bread. "I shall write to Posen and ask for a full report on the file. That is what an office does. And you, Ilse, will write to the Amt yourself, since it is your application, and say that your interest in the case of the child Maria Anna Wojtera of Warsaw is unchanged and immediate, and that you wish the case to be completed in the ordinary course before the end of the winter." He looked at her with his mild eyes. "Because an application that lies still is an application that someone else fills. You understand?"

"Yes," Ilse said. "I understand."

And on the twenty-seventh of December, in the small writing room off the hall at Falkenhorst, with the snow coming down over the Oder and the smell of the goose fat and the fir tree, Ilse von Brandt sat down at the desk and took out two sheets of good paper with the monogram at the top, and began her letter to the Rasse- und Siedlungsamt in Posen, and the letter was short, and the letter was correct, and the letter contained one sentence more than was required by the form.

She wrote, under her signature, in her neat, upright, widow's hand:

*I have seen the child's photograph and I am satisfied as to her suitability so far as the racial finding is concerned. I am told that the case is connected with an officer of the Wehrmacht stationed in Warsaw who is a member of my family by marriage. For the sake of the family's name and its standing in the Warthegau, and to prevent a scandal which would be of no use to anyone in Potsdam or in Berlin, I ask that the office determine the case speedily, and that the father's name, if it is to be entered at all, be entered without the mother's knowledge and with the child placed in the Reich before the mother can contest the finding.*

She read it over twice. Then she sealed it, and rang for the maid to take it to the post, and went back into the hall, where a nine-year-old boy was being a horse and a six-year-old girl was the rider, and stood in the doorway with her hands folded, watching the children scream with laughter under the tree, and she thought — and the thought was quite calm, and quite clear, and it never left her afterwards, for the rest of her life — *Whatever is done now, I did it myself, in my own hand, and I was of sound mind, and I wanted it, and that is the only truth about me, and I shall have to carry it the way one carries a stone in the stomach, all the days of my life.*
