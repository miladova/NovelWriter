# Chapter One: The Net

*Warsaw — Thursday, 20 May 1943*

The queue outside the bakery on Krucza Street began to form at half past four in the morning, and by six it was sixty people long, and Helena Wojtera was thirty-first in it, which meant that she would get bread and would not get much of it.

She had learned the arithmetic of hunger the way other people learned a language. The legal ration was two hundred grammes of bread a day, which was a joke the Germans told with a straight face. The black market wanted fifty zlotys for a kilo, which was not a joke at all, because fifty zlotys was two days of the money she earned teaching the violin to children whose mothers paid her in eggs, in lard, in glycerine soap, and once, memorably, in a jar of goose fat that had been hidden in a grave since 1939.

She stood with her hands in the pockets of her coat, because her coat had pockets and the woman behind her had a handbag, and a handbag was a thing that could be snatched by a boy on a bicycle, and the boys on bicycles worked for other boys who worked, in the end, for men who sold whatever they were given to whoever would pay. Warsaw in the third spring of the occupation was a city of very long queues and very short tempers, and the trick was to stand in the queue with your shoulders turned, so that nobody could see what you had and nobody could take it from you before you got home.

The woman in front of her was Pani Halina Rowińska, who lived on the same landing at Hoża 14 and who knew everything about everybody, and who had the news from her sister-in-law's cousin, who cleaned for a German family in Żoliborz.

"They say there's going to be an action," Pani Halina said. She said it the way she said everything, with the air of a woman handing out sweets. "A big one. Like January."

In January the Germans had closed the city for three days, and when they opened it again there were fifteen thousand fewer people in Warsaw, and the trams ran empty, and there was a silence in the courtyards for a week.

"They always say that," Helena said.

"They say this time they're taking the young ones. From the schools." Pani Halina looked sideways at her. "You should keep the child at home."

Helena said nothing. There was no arguing with Halina Rowińska, who was not a bad woman, only a woman with a mouth that ran like a tap, and in a city where a word could be worth a life, a running tap was a dangerous thing in a neighbour.

The baker, a small man with flour on his eyebrows and the look of a saint who has stopped believing, unlocked the door at six twenty and began to hand out bread through a gap the width of a hand, so that only one person at a time could reach it. When Helena's turn came she put down her money and her card and received a loaf the size of a boot, gritty and grey and slightly warm, and she put it inside her coat, against her stomach, and buttoned the coat over it, and walked away fast, because the last hundred metres from the shop door to her own gate were the most dangerous metres in Warsaw and everyone knew it.

Hoża Street was waking in the way of the occupied city: a tram grinding round the corner with three Germans sitting on the roof, a boy with a wooden box of cigarettes at the gate, an old man sweeping the gutter because the Germans had decreed that the pavements would be swept and the gutters swept and the city kept clean, and the Poles kept the city clean, and the Germans took photographs of the clean city for the newspapers in Berlin.

Number 14 had a courtyard with four acacias, a well, and an iron gate that Pani Zofia Mrożek closed at night with a key she wore on a string around her neck between her breasts, because a key on a string was harder to steal than a key in a pocket. Zofia was sixty-one and had been the caretaker's wife and then the widow of the caretaker and now she was the caretaker, with a broom and a tin badge and an air of authority that could stop a thief, a drunk or an officer of the Ordnungspolizei at ten paces.

She was at the gate when Helena came in, and she said, "You got bread."

"Two kilos."

"Then God exists." Zofia spat twice, for luck in a courtyard, and lowered her voice. "The child wanted to walk with you. I said no. She wanted to go to the market with you at noon, too. She's in the stairwell, playing again, with the window open. You'll hear her from the second landing."

Helena heard her from the gate, in fact: four bars, tenderly scraped, the same four bars over and over, with a wobble on the third that she had promised to fix and had not had the heart to fix, because the wobble was Marysia's, and Marysia was nine.

She went down the steps to the cellar first, the way she did every morning, with the loaf under her arm.

The cellar under Hoża 14 smelled of coal, damp plaster and a cat. Behind the coal bin there was a gap in the brickwork, and in the gap there was a cavity the size of a cupboard, and in the cavity, in a nest of old coats, there lived a girl of eight called Rachel Perel, who had been Renia Zielińska for three months, and who had not seen the sun since February.

Helena put her hand into the dark and the girl's fingers closed round hers, small and dry, like a bird's foot.

"Bread," Rachel whispered. It was not a question. In the cellar, everything was either bread or not-bread.

"Half. I'll come at noon if I can." Helena tore the loaf, and felt the two pieces, and gave away the larger. "There's a tin of milk behind the loose brick. If you hear them on the stairs, you go into the pipes. All the way down. You know."

"I know."

"There'll be a day," Helena said. "Not this week. But a day."

"Yes," Rachel said, in the voice of a child agreeing to a story she has heard too many times to believe and too many times to give up.

Helena went up the stairs, and on the second landing her daughter's violin stopped, and a face appeared round the door of their room: thin and brown and quick, with her father's eyes, which were grey, and her mother's mouth, which was wide and always ready to smile, and a brown plait down her back as thick as a rope because she had refused to have it cut since she was six.

"Did you bring it?"

"Look in my coat."

Marysia put in her hand and pulled out the loaf like a conjuror pulling out a rabbit. For a moment the two of them looked at it — two kilos of grey bread, a week of life — and then Marysia laughed, and Helena thought, as she thought four or five times a day, that it was an obscenity to keep such a laugh in such a city, and an obscenity not to.

"Eat the crust," she said. "Before Halina comes up and smells it. Then practice. Bar eleven, from the top. Slowly."

"It's horrible."

"It is a very beautiful piece of music. It is not the piece's fault that it is difficult."

They said the same thing to each other almost every day, and it gave them both enormous comfort.

At noon, with the sun out and the day feeling like a normal day, they went to the market.

The fires across the wall of the Jewish quarter had gone out less than a week before, and the city still talked of nothing else; but on a Thursday the market at the corner of Krucza and Żurawia filled up with cabbage, sprouting potatoes, beetroot, a woman selling sunflower seeds by the glass, another woman selling shoelaces and old keys and a single boot, a man with a barrow of horse bones, and a crowd of two hundred people buying and selling and shouting the way crowds do when they are trying very hard to believe that an ordinary thing is happening.

Marysia carried the basket. She was tall for nine and liked to be trusted, and there was a fluttering in her shoulders whenever she was, like a bird deciding on a branch.

"Beetroot," she reported. "That one wants thirty for the pair. That's robbery."

"It's Tuesday."

"It's Thursday. She thinks because we're from the other side of the street we don't know the days."

Helena had to laugh. Then she heard the whistles, two of them, near, from the direction of Bracka Street, and the sound of shutters coming down, and her stomach dropped as if she had stepped into a hole.

"Marysia," she said. "The gateway. There. Go."

The gateways were the only hope. There was a law of the łapanka, learned the hard way: at the first whistle you did not run. Running drew the eye. You walked like a person on an errand, and you went into a gateway, and you went through the gateway into the courtyards at the back, and if you were clever you went over a wall into the next street, and if you were lucky there was a man in the next street with a barrow and a coat you could put on.

The crowd turned into a hundred animals at once. A pregnant woman sat down in the middle of the pavement and screamed; a man with a barrow abandoned it and ran and was shot, in the leg, and fell and rolled, and the sight of it undid whatever remained of the arithmetic in the crowd and the street became water in a channel.

Helena took her daughter by the wrist and drove into a gateway, and through it, into a courtyard lined with the back walls of three buildings, where a boy was up on a wall already, holding his hand down to help, and behind them the crowd came in, and the courtyard had one exit and they were standing in front of it.

"No," Helena said. "Not that way. Up."

She was not strong but she was desperate, and desperate can carry a child up a coal-shoot to a low roof over a shed. They got to the shed roof as the first gendarmes came into the courtyard: green uniforms, boots, carbines held across the body, and behind them the Polish police in navy blue with their hands down and their eyes on the ground, and behind them the auxiliaries — the Ukrainians, in stained tunics, with a trident on their caps and dogs on chains — and the noise of forty people being sorted into a corner like washing.

A rope skipped across the tiles above her head and the boy on the wall said, "*Szybko, szybko* — fast — the other roof." He had a face like a ferret, one ear bandaged, maybe twelve years old, and he held the rope with both hands while Helena got her daughter over the ridge tiles and down onto the shed on the other side, which creaked under their weight like a ship.

Then the second whistle came, from the street in front of them, and Helena understood, with the terrible clarity of the trapped, that the Germans had not made one net, they had made two, and she had walked out of the first and into the second, and behind the shed there was only a wall and a drop of four metres, and the drop was full of gendarmes.

The boy said, "*Psia krew*," and let go of the rope and ran along the ridge and was gone.

A German voice, very calm, said, "Kommen Sie herunter. Sofort."

They came down. That was the thing Helena remembered afterwards, when she remembered anything at all: that her daughter came down first, because the jump was not so bad for a nine-year-old, and turned and lifted her arms to her mother, and a gendarme with a face like a boot laughed at that, and Helena, who had never in her life hit a man, wanted to kill him with her teeth.

Afterwards there were the lorries.

They were wood-burning lorries with great iron generators on the back, and the smell of them was the smell of burnt pine and hot iron, and they stood three of them nose to tail on Krucza Street, and the people taken were put into them with their hands on the shoulders of the person in front. A woman began the prayer for the dying, and another woman told her to be quiet, and ten minutes later everybody on the lorry was praying too, quietly, in the rain that had started, the first rain of the week, a fine grey drizzle that made the street look like a photograph of itself.

Marysia's hand was in Helena's hand, and Marysia's mouth was closed tight, and the piece of beetroot she had bought, and the shilling change, and the basket, and the loaf of bread were all gone, and Helena's mind was running through the empty rooms of what she had and what she could sell and who she knew, the way a hand runs along a wall in the dark.

"I'm not frightened," Marysia said suddenly. "Are you?"

"No," Helena said. That was the first lie.

"You're squeezing."

"I'm sorry." She loosened her grip. "Listen. Say nothing. Answer nothing. If they ask, you are eleven."

"Why eleven?"

"Because eleven is a worker and nine is a child, and children disappear." She had heard it from Halina, from Zofia, from the woman at the pawnshop on Żelazna: the ones who were small, and fair, and orphaned-looking were sent to a room in a big building, and after the room, nobody. "Eleven," she said again. "And your father is dead. He died before you were born. He was a Pole. Say it."

"He was a Pole," Marysia said. "He died before I was born." She looked up. "What was his name?"

"Adam," Helena said, as she had said a thousand times. "Adam Wójcik. He was a railway man. He was killed by a train, the month before you were born." She said it in one breath, and it was not true in any particular, and she had never told the child a single false thing in her life except this one, and this one she had told so many times that it had the smoothness of the true.

The lorry crossed the river.

Helena saw the Vistula from the top of the load because she was near the tailgate and taller than the women around her: a brown river, and the pontoon bridge, and the Kierbedź bridge, and the tugs, and the far shore with its warehouses and its chimneys, and the whole city on the left bank, dark and low, with a great bald patch in the middle of it where the ghetto had been, a place of cinders and broken wall and blackened emptiness the size of a small town. She had seen it from a rooftop, once, in April, when the distant firing went on all night and the sky over Muranów turned the colour of an apricot. She had not wept.

She wept now, and it was not about the raid.

The place they took them to was a school.

It was a real school, on Skaryszewska Street in Praga, built a few years before the war, three storeys of red brick and wide windows, and it still said on the wall over the door, in letters you could read through the paint, SZKOŁA POWSZECHNA NR 91. The Germans had turned it into a camp, and the camp had a name, the one everybody used, which sounded Polish and German at the same time: *dulag*, from the German word for going through.

They were counted in the yard. Then the men were taken to one side of the building and the women to the other, and there was a moment when Helena's hand was opened, and it was opened by a guard's stick, and she had a vision, very clear and very brief, of herself biting that stick into splinters — and then Marysia's fingers were let go, and were gone, and were in the crowd, and the child's voice called, "*Mamo!*" once, the way she had called when she was three and fell off the wall, and Helena screamed her name, and a man's hand came across her face and she stopped screaming, and the drizzle went on getting everything wet.

"How old?" a clerk asked in German, an hour later, in the line for the bath house, as women queued with their clothes in their arms and their ribs showing, and Helena answered "Eleven," and the clerk wrote it.

The bath house had tiled walls and twelve shower heads and one temperature, which was cold. After the shower the women went through a room where their clothes hung on rails, and a man with a mask sprayed them with something that stank, and after that they were given back, damp, and Helena found her coat and her skirt and her blouse, and the loaf was gone, of course, and so was her purse, and so were her mother's earrings, which had been in the watch pocket, and she stood holding the coat, wet, in a room full of wet women, and she was, she found, more relieved than she had ever expected to be to be in the third and deepest circle of hell with her clothes on.

Then they cut their hair.

The women went into the next room with their heads held like queens and came out with their heads like the heads of hedgehogs, and the machine the Germans used buzzed and stopped, buzzzed and stopped, and the pile of hair on the floor grew to the size of a bonfire, and there was a boy with a broom whose job was to sweep it into sacks, because hair went to the Reich as well, hair went into mattresses and stockings and felt, and nothing that came through Skaryszewska went out of it unused.

Helena held herself straight for the razor and did not weep, and afterwards found that her hands were shaking so hard she had to tuck them into her armpits.

And then they brought the children.

They came in a line through the door from the yard, the small ones, and they were shorn already, all of them, boys and girls, and in a line of thirty shorn children it was, for a moment, impossible to know your own. Helena's eyes went along the line, quick, quick, quick, and did not find her, and the scream was already rising in her throat when a small hand closed round her wrist, and she looked down at a boy with a long neck and enormous grey eyes, and it was her.

Her plait was gone. Marysia held it, in her fist, folded, like a rope.

The look on the child's face was not fear and was not weeping. It was something older: the look of a person who has recently learned an important thing about the world.

"They did it with the big machine," Marysia said, quite calm. "It didn't hurt at all. The woman before me screamed and they laughed at her."

"Did they touch you?"

"They made us stand in a row with nothing on and a man walked along the row and counted us with a stick. He had a stick and he counted our heads. Then another man came and looked at our teeth."

Helena crouched and put her arms round her, and the wet, shorn head went onto her shoulder, and the child held the plait against her mother's back so that it would not be lost, and they stood like that in the middle of the room among the wet women and the rain and the smell of sabadyl, and Helena said, in Polish, quietly, into her daughter's ear, the only promise that she had left: "I am going to get you out of this."

She had no idea how. She had a violin and half a photograph and a name she had not said aloud in ten years, and none of those things was money, and money was the only language spoken in the third circle of hell.

The Wehrmacht came for the workers at four o'clock.

They came in a grey car and two lorries, and the officer who got out of the car was not old; he was about thirty, with a face that had been handsome before the war and was now merely competent, and a uniform that fitted him like a uniform should, and a leather map case, and a civilian interpreter, and the air of a man who has an appointment to keep and does not intend to miss it. The commandant of the camp came out to meet him and saluted and was not saluted back with any warmth, and there was a conference, and a list.

They were looking for hands. That was the thing that the Poles in the yard understood at once, in the way that prisoners understand everything at once: they were looking for hands, for metal-workers and fitters and carpenters and turners and seamstresses, because the Wehrmacht had workshops and the workshops had quotas, and a quota was a reason for a German officer to walk along a line of Poles in a wet school yard and look at their hands. Men held up their hands. Women held up their hands. An old man who had been a lathe operator at the PZO works was taken; a young man who had been a clerk said he was a welder and was taken, and would die for the lie or be saved by it, nobody could say; and Helena heard a woman behind her whisper, "*Ręce, ręce*" — hands, hands — and looked at her own hands, and thought about the violin, and the pads on the fingertips, and did not put them up, because the violin was a thing she had never sold.

The officer came down the line.

He walked slowly, with the clip of paper in his hand, and the interpreter beside him, and he said something to a woman about her card, and something else to a man about his trade, and his voice was a cool even voice with the north German vowels in it, and Helena, who was not looking directly at anything, heard it, and felt the whole world go quiet.

She looked up.

He was nine or ten paces away, in profile, his head bent to the clipboard, and the light was behind him so that the grey of his tunic and the grey of the sky were the same colour, and for a moment she had the illusion that she had seen him on the street a hundred times since the occupation and had simply failed to see him: the same height, the same shoulders, the same trick of standing with the weight on the left leg.

Then he turned his head, because the man beside him had said something, and she saw his face.

The world did not stop. That was the strange thing. She always thought afterwards that for a moment she had expected the world to stop, the way it does in the storybooks — the clock, the traffic, the rain in the air — and it did not: the rain went on falling, the count went on, a boy in the corner coughed with the sound of a boy who would not last the month, and Helena's heart went on beating so hard she could feel it in her wrists and her neck and her eyes.

Ten years. He had been twenty. He was thirty-one. There was grey in the temples that had not been earned by age. There was a scar, small, on the left cheek, that she had kissed once, in a garden, in Munich, in another century.

His eyes, going along the line of women the way an officer's eyes go along a line of women, stopped on her face.

Nothing happened in his face. It was, in its way, the most frightening thing she had ever seen, that nothing happened — no shock, no flush, only a stillness that went deeper than the stillness of a man at his work, and a muscle, in his jaw, once, moving.

The interpreter said something about the next woman. The officer did not move. A gendarme, seeing the pause, came forward and said something loud in German and pushed Helena back a step with the flat of his hand, and the officer said, in the voice of a man who has noticed nothing at all, "*Lassen Sie sie*" — leave her — and then, in the same flat voice, looking at her properly now, so that the interpreter wrote nothing down because there was nothing to write: "Follow me."

The room they took her to was the headmaster's office. There was a photograph of the headmaster's family on the wall, upside down, and a cupboard full of the school's registers, and a desk with a green blotter, and a window with a view of the yard where the women stood in five rows with their shorn heads, and a Ukrainian auxiliary on the other side of the door with his rifle across his knees.

The officer stood with his back to the window, and looked at her, and said nothing for a long time.

"You have the advantage of me," she said at last, in German. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "I don't know how I should address you."

"You always did that," he said. He spoke German, but she heard the shape of the Polish words underneath, which he had tried to learn for her, in a cold room in Munich, with a grammar book and a hand in her hair. "You always put on the voice of a railway clerk's daughter from Żoliborz when you were frightened."

"My father was an engineer."

"He was." He looked down at the paper in his hand, and she saw, for the first time, that his fingers held it too hard. "Wojtera. Helena. Hoża 14. Music teacher. Born 1910." He read it the way a man reads a page he has read a hundred times already. "Do you have papers?"

"You took them."

"Of course." He put the paper down on the blotter, squaring its edges, an act of such ordinariness that she was suddenly, obscurely angry. "You have a child."

"I have a child."

"Nine years old, the clerk wrote. Or eleven." He looked up. "Which is it?"

The room was very quiet. Out in the yard someone was crying with the terrible monotony of a woman who has stopped being able to stop.

"Eleven," Helena said.

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, without any particular colour in his voice, as though he were reading the weather from the wall of a station: "In May of 1933 you were in Warsaw."

"I was in Warsaw."

"You wrote to me from Warsaw. Once." He was not asking. "The letter said that your father was ill. The letter said that you would write again when you could. It came in the morning post on the twenty-eighth of May. I carried it in my tunic for a week, and then I wrote to you three times, and then my father told me what you had said to him, and what you had taken, and I—"

He stopped.

The silence went on so long that the auxiliary's rifle butt moved on the floorboards outside and Helena flinched.

"You never wrote to me again," she said.

"No."

"I don't know what your father told you."

"I know what he told me." He said it evenly. "I am not going to ask you who the child's father is."

"Then why am I here?"

"Because the clerk wrote nine," Friedrich von Brandt said, "and in this building, nine is a leaf in a different drawer. Nine goes to the race office, and from the race office to a home in the Reich, and there is a family in Posen waiting for a girl like that, and the man who runs the race office here keeps a book with photographs in it." He picked up the paper again. "Eleven goes to the labour list. Eleven goes, at worst, to a factory, where a clever girl can survive if she is strong and a cleverer one can survive if she is not."

Helena put her hand on the edge of the desk. It was not dramatic; she simply could not stand without it.

"You are telling me to lie to you," she said.

"I am telling you that the number in the register is a name that is mine to write, and that I wrote eleven, and that I am now going to sign it." He did sign it. He took a pen from the inkstand and wrote her daughter's name and a number, and the nib made a small sound on the paper like a fly against glass. Then he looked up, and his face was still nothing at all, a mask of administrative calm, and his eyes were the eyes of the young man in the dark in Munich, and that was the worst thing: not the cruelty of a German, which she had expected, but this, the tenderness of a man doing an ugly thing very carefully in order to be kind.

"Friedrich," she said. She had not said the name out loud in ten years. She heard it leave her mouth and go into the room. It was like a coin thrown down a well; there is no telling, from the top, whether anything has disturbed the water.

He put the cap back on the pen.

"At the gate," he said, "there will be a transport for the labour camp at four o'clock tomorrow morning, and your name will not be on it, and the child's will not be, because you are a teacher, and the Reich needs teachers, and I need interpreters, and there will be a paper." He laid his hand flat on the desk. "That is all."

It was not all. It was not remotely all. But she saw his hand, and the hand was shaking, and on the pale inside of the fourth finger, where a wedding ring would not be, there was a mark, a thin white line, the mark of a ring worn for years and removed — and Helena, who was the only person in Warsaw who knew what that ring had looked like, because she had chosen it herself, in a shop on Sendlinger Straße in the spring of 1933, stood in the headmaster's office with her shorn hair and her coat still damp through, and knew the worst thing of all.

He had understood her lie. He had understood it because he had been counting the years too, and the count had come out the same as hers, and he had signed the eleven on the paper and would now carry the nine, and the nine in the middle of his chest, for the rest of his life.

"At the gate," she said. "Tomorrow. Yes."

He turned to the window and looked out at the yard where the women stood in five rows with their heads like hedgehogs, and did not turn round when she was taken out, and Helena walked with the auxiliary back along the corridor, past the headmaster's inverted family on the wall, past the room where the hair was swept into sacks, and she was thinking, with an accuracy that frightened her, about money, and the violin, and the tin box under the fourth floorboard, and how much time a man's kindness could buy before it was collected with interest, and where in Warsaw in the spring of 1943 a person could go to find someone who knew how to make a child vanish and reappear alive.
