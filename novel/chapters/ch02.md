# Chapter Two: The Man in Grey

*Warsaw — Friday, 21 May 1943*

They buried Leutnant Horst Peltz at eleven o'clock in the morning, in the corner of the Evangelical graveyard on Młynarska Street that the Wehrmacht had taken for its own, and it rained the whole time, a steady grey rain that made the black coats blacker and the white faces whiter and turned the soil they had dug into something the colour of coffee grounds.

Friedrich von Brandt stood in the third rank with his cap in his hand and the rain running off his hair and down the inside of his collar, and listened to the padre say what padres say.

He had known Peltz, in the way an officer knows a subaltern of his own regiment from peacetime: not well, but completely, because in the Seventh Cavalry everybody knew everything about everybody, and young Peltz had been a cheerful idiot who played the accordion badly and had once ridden into the regimental pond on a bet. He had been twenty-three. Eleven days ago a sniper had shot him through the throat as he walked along the edge of the burnt-out Jewish quarter with a clipboard in his hand, going to look at a building, of all things, and he had drowned in his own blood on the pavement of Bonifraterska Street while a sergeant tried to hold the wound shut with a field dressing.

At the graveside, Peltz's company commander, a captain whose hands shook, tried to say something and could not, and then read out a letter from the boy's mother, which was the closest thing to the truth that anybody in Warsaw that morning was permitted to say.

Friedrich looked at the coffin, at the tilted earth, at the brown puddles in the footmarks, and he did the arithmetic that soldiers do at funerals. Nineteen hundred and fourteen. His father a major at the Marne, thirty-one years old, with two sons already. Nineteen thirty-one, his own commissioning year. And now this, in a graveyard in a city that hated the ground he stood on, in the fourth year of a war that the family had greeted as the beginning of the family's third great chapter.

His father had said, when the Polish campaign ended: *Now, Friedrich, the map is being redrawn by men who understand that history is a matter of will. Be useful.* And Friedrich, who had wanted to be useful since he was eleven years old, had been very useful indeed: staff work, forms, quotas, requisitions, the war of paper that runs a war of iron. He had never fired his pistol at a man. He had never been shelled, except once, in the Ardennes, when the road ahead of him had been shelled and he had lain in a ditch wishing the mud were deeper. He had organized billets for eleven thousand men in France, and a rail movement of two armoured divisions in Poland, and he was, by any measure his profession used, a first-rate officer.

And what he had actually done, in the four years of this war, was to arrange for other people to be cold, hungry and afraid a little more efficiently than would otherwise have been the case.

He did not put it to himself in those words. He put it to himself in the useful phrase that his generation had taken from their fathers' mouths and polished until it shone: one did what could be done. But he had learned, since coming to Warsaw, that this phrase has a bottom to it. He learned it first the previous summer, when the trains went east from the Umschlagplatz day after day and the city smelled of hot iron and lime, and he had said to himself, in the language of his class: *it is not my department.* Ulm had not been his department. Lidice had not been his department. The ghetto had not been his department, and the wall along Żelazna Street had not been his department, and the notices on the walls that named the men who had been shot at dawn for the crime of being Poles were not his department, and the fact that he could walk from his hotel to his office every morning through a city where a man could be shot for smuggling an egg, and feel *nothing much*, was the worst thing he had ever learned about himself, and he had learned it, as he learned everything, without ever saying it out loud.

Afterwards he went back to the office and dictated his report on the funeral, because his report on the funeral was expected, and then he dictated two letters that mattered and one that did not, and then he went to find Klara Seidler, who had the lists.

The commandant's office occupied the upper two floors of a pre-war insurance building on Nowy Świat, with a marble staircase, a brass lift cage that no longer worked, and a smell of wet wool and floor polish. Friedrich's own kingdom was the second floor, four rooms: his office, an outer office where Klara worked and where Oberzahlmeister Wollweber had a desk in the window, a map room, and a small room that was called, on the door, *Referat für Arbeitseinsatz* — the section for labour deployment — where two German clerks and one Polish typist worked under a lamp all day long on the great business of Warsaw under the occupation: the business of causing the right number of the right people to be in the right place at the right time, whether or not they wished to be.

"Herr Major." Klara Seidler stood up when he came in, which she always did, and which he had stopped asking her not to do, because it made her unhappy. She was twenty-eight, from Kassel, the daughter of a railway inspector, with pale eyelashes and a long neck and the flat, careful, tidy competence of a girl who had been brought up to be a secretary and had turned out to have been built by nature for something else entirely. She had been in Warsaw eleven months. She had a photograph of her parents on her desk and a photograph of nobody else, and she went twice a week to the German church on Szpitalna Street, and on the other five days she did things at her desk that Friedrich had never asked her to do and never once asked her about.

"The Skaryszewska list," he said.

"On your desk, Herr Major. The commandant's office wants the labour allocation confirmed by this evening. The Arbeitsamt claims we already have their quota, and Wollweber says we don't. There is also the question of the twenty-two men from the Festungs-Pionier school, and Herr Doktor Böhm telephoned about the hospital laundry."

"All of that can wait."

Klara said nothing. She had learned, in eleven months, that "it can wait" was the sentence that preceded the interesting things.

Friedrich sat down at his desk, in the good chair, under the wall map of the General Government with its neat red pins, and put his cap down on the blotter and pulled the papers towards him.

It was a list. It was, by any measure, an ordinary list: the camp at Skaryszewska sent its intake register to the labour office at the end of every week, and the labour office sent a copy to the Referat für Arbeitseinsatz, because the Wehrmacht had first refusal on certain trades, and because the Wehrmacht always needed hands, and because the hands in Warsaw belonged, by the rules of the occupation, to whoever in the German apparatus made the best claim on them first.

He read it the way he read all of them, at speed, running his pen down the columns — number, name, born, trade, disposition — and the names slid past him as they always did: Kowalski, Nowak, Wiśniewska, Zieliński, the four hundred names that Polish Warsaw endlessly produced. He stopped at forty, for a glass of tea, and went on.

*241. Wojtera, Maria Anna. 3.12.1933. Warsaw. —*

The pen stopped. He read the line three times, and then a fourth, and there was no change in it. The child of a Polish mother, born the third of December, nineteen hundred and thirty-three.

He put the pen down very carefully, as though it might go off, and sat quite still and did the arithmetic.

Thirty-six weeks before the third of December, 1933, is a morning in March.

He knew the morning in March. It was a Sunday, the twelfth, his twentieth birthday, and he had walked with her from the Isar along the gravel paths past the ice houses to the Englischer Garten, and she had been wearing a grey coat with a collar that buttoned up the throat, because she was always cold, and she had been laughing at him for something — he could not remember what, he could not remember what, and it made him want to pull the whole memory up out of the ground by the roots — and they had gone into the little pavilion by the lake, out of the wind, and stayed there while the light came down through the shutters in bars. He had been able to hear a band somewhere beyond the trees playing a waltz. He had unbuttoned the collar of her coat, one button at a time, and she had said his name, and after that neither of them had said anything at all for a very long time.

The Isar. The lake. The pavilion and the band. The bars of light coming through the shutters, and the small brass ring her hair left on the pillow afterwards, and the cup of chocolate they had shared on the way home, and the way she had walked backwards away from him at the door of her uncle's flat, waving, and how she had been at the window when he turned round at the end of the street, and put her hand flat against the glass.

He had had the whole life in his head, then. He could see it now, still, if he let himself: a small house somewhere south of Munich, and a garden, and children with her hands and his eyes, and a piano, and the violin. He had been twenty years old and he had thought that a life was a thing you could choose. Nobody had told him that a life is a thing that other people choose for you, in a room you are not in.

Klara was standing in the doorway with a tray and the tea he had not asked for. He did not look up and after a moment she set the tray down on the corner of the desk and went out.

Friedrich put his hand flat on the paper, over the line, and held it there, the way one holds a hand over a wound.

The child had his eyes in the photograph. That was the thing he could not put down. He had seen the child for nine seconds across the yard of a transit camp with her head shaved, and she was thirty feet away and the rain was in the way, and he had seen his own eyes — the heavy lids, the set of the brow, his mother's eyes, the eyes of a woman who had been dead for eight years — in the face of a nine-year-old girl in a disinfected coat, and had refused to know what he knew.

He sat with that for a while. Then he did what he always did with a catastrophe: he considered the file.

There is a kind of man, and Friedrich had been one all his life, for whom the world's worst news arrives as a problem of administration. If the child is on the race office's list, then the race office's list must be altered, or the child must be removed from it, or the race office must be satisfied that the child is not what the list claims she might be. Every one of these was a thing a Major on the staff could, with care, arrange. Not one of them was a thing a Major on the staff could arrange for nothing.

He opened the drawer where he kept the Skaryszewska papers from the day before and took out the carbon of the dispositions sheet he had made on Thursday afternoon, and read it, and there in his own hand, in the neat column, was written:

*Wojtera, Maria Anna, b. 3.12.33 — labour detail III (Sortierung), Skaryszewska, retained.*

Which was, in Friedrich's grammar, a sentence meaning: this child will be kept in a basement in Praga sorting rags for the German army until somebody decides what to do with her.

And in the margin of the carbon, in the hand of the camp's office clerk, a pencil note, which he had not noticed yesterday because yesterday he had been looking at the mother's face:

*Rassische Überprüfung beantragt?* — racial examination requested?

Requested by whom? He turned the page. There was no name. There was only the small blue stamp of the camp administration, and, in the corner, a number, which was the number of a file at the office on Szucha Avenue, and the date, the twentieth of May, which was yesterday, which was the day he had stood in the yard in the rain and looked at a woman he had not seen for ten years and told himself that he was doing a hard thing well.

Somebody had been faster than he was. Somebody in Skaryszewska, or somebody in the labour office, or a clerk with a form, had seen a child of nine in a transit camp for adults and had done the thing the regulations required, which was to ask the question that the whole apparatus existed to ask, and the question had gone to the race office, and the race office would now ask its own questions, and it would ask them of the mother, and of the child, and of Friedrich von Brandt, whose signature was on the dispositions sheet, in ink, at the bottom of the page.

He put the papers down and stood up and looked out of the window at Nowy Świat, at the trams, at the people walking with their shoulders hunched, at the two boys who were standing on the corner selling newspapers whose mastheads were printed in German, and he thought, with a clarity that had been a long time coming: *there it is. That is what a wall looks like from the inside.*

He had spent ten years being careful about Helena Wojtera. He wanted to believe that he had been careful for her sake. He had, on the fifth of June, 1933, gone to his father in the study at Falkenhorst, the study with the map of the old Prussian provinces on the wall and the smell of cigars and cold ash, and told him that he intended to marry a Polish violinist from Warsaw who had no money and no family and no religion worth speaking of, and his father had listened with his hands folded and his eyes half shut, and then had explained to him, kindly, with the patience of a man explaining the multiplication tables, what that decision would cost — his commission, his name, his allowance, his mother's memory, his sister's marriage, the estate — and what it would cost the girl, who was, after all, a foreign national, and whose residence in the Reich depended on the signature of a police official who took his coffee with the Generalmajor's adjutant on Thursdays.

And Friedrich, twenty years old, who had thought a life was a thing you could choose, had said that he would manage it, and had gone back to Munich with a plan, and his father had reached the girl first.

That was the part Friedrich had never been able to make into a story he could live in. Not the ultimatum; ultimatums were a father's trade. The part was the letter.

It had come to him in Munich on the first of July: four lines, no address, in her handwriting, which he could have recognized in the dark of a cellar; it said that she had been mistaken in her feelings, that she had accepted the sum the Generalmajor had offered her for a passage home and the settlement of her uncle's debts, that what had happened between them had been an autumn and a winter of a young girl's foolishness, and that he was not to write to her again.

He had not believed it. He had gone to Warsaw. That was the thing he had never told anyone: he had got thirty-six hours' leave in September 1933 and gone to Warsaw with eighty marks in his pocket and stood in a courtyard on Hoża Street under her windows, and had not gone up. He had stood there for an hour like a man in a novel, in the rain, and then he had gone back to the station and got on the train, because — and this was the kernel and the shame of it, the thing he had been running from for ten years — because he had half believed the letter, and because his father's version of the world was the only one he had ever been taught to read, and because if the letter was true then he had been a fool, and if the letter was false then he had to choose between his father and the whole architecture of his life, and he was twenty years old, and he chose the architecture.

He had put her out of his mind the way a man puts out a lamp: by turning away from it. And in the years that followed, in the horse shows and the mess dinners, in Paris in the spring of 1940 with the army, at a hotel in Bad Nauheim in the summer of 1937 where the families had got together and where he had met Erika von Wense, who was thirty-two and patient and good with her hands, and where the match had been settled over two dinners by three old people who had known each other all their lives — in all those years, when the thought of Warsaw came to him, it came as a small room in the dark that he did not open.

And then in September 1939 he had ridden into Poland behind the guns, at the head of a column of the Seventh, and when he saw the name of the city on a signboard outside Błonie he had actually smiled, because the whole thing had seemed, for one appalling half-hour, like a kind of homecoming.

He turned away from the window.

"Herr Major?" Klara, in the doorway.

"Have the car brought round. I'm going to Praga."

"The Arbeitsamt at two —"

"My apologies to the Arbeitsamt."

Klara's pencil stayed still over her notebook for a moment, and then she said, "Very good, Herr Major," and went out, and Friedrich heard her telephone the motor pool in the flat voice she kept for the telephone, and then heard her say, more quietly, to somebody in the outer office, "*Nein. Nichts.* Nothing."

They drove east along the avenue and over the bridge, with Kellner at the wheel, Gefreiter Bruno Kellner, forty-one years old, a Berlin grocer who had been called up in 1941 and who had made it his life's work since then to keep a fat man's amiable competence between himself and the war. He drove the Opel with the easy slowness of a man who has learned that there is no profit ever in arriving first, and he had an enormous red face and a certificate in his pocket that said he had a weak heart, and Friedrich had never once asked him whether it was true.

"To the school, Herr Major?"

"To the school."

The Vistula went past, brown and flat under the rain, and the far bank came up with its warehouses and its chimneys and its own wet grey streets, and Kellner said, conversationally, "Praga's better off than the other side. The bread's cheaper. It's the river, you see, the smugglers use the river. It's always the same, Herr Major: wherever there's water, there's a way round the law."

"You sound as though you approve, Kellner."

"I'm a grocer, Herr Major," Kellner said, deadpan, looking at the road. "All men who approve of nothing starve."

The camp at Skaryszewska had, since Thursday, grown. There was another hundred metres of wire along the front of the school, and a second tower going up at the corner, flat brown scaffold boards and a man with a welding torch, and two lorries with their tailgates down in the yard and a work party of Poles loading them with sacks. The smell was the smell of any place in Europe in 1943 where four thousand people are kept waiting: disinfectant, unwashed cloth, urine, boiled cabbage, and the sweetish undertone of something that Friedrich's mind refused to name and which was, in fact, mostly the rain and the lime.

At the gate a Ukrainian auxiliary looked at his pass, then at him, then at the pass again, and then went for the commandant's clerk, because there was a protocol in these things, and the protocol had to be observed even at the third circle of a transit camp on a Friday in May.

The commandant was SS-Untersturmführer Oskar Bierbaum: twenty-nine years old, from Ludwigshafen, with a whippet's face and a bureaucratic sacredness, and a great appetite for paperwork, and, Friedrich noticed again, exactly the kind of shined boots that a boy wears to the first day of a new job he intends to keep for many years.

"Herr Major. An honour. We don't see the Wehrmacht often." Bierbaum's office was the chemistry laboratory of the school; there were still a few bottles in a cupboard with the labels turned to the wall, and a periodic table on the blackboard with one name rubbed out. "Coffee? I have real coffee. There's a circumstance."

"Thank you, no. I came about the labour allocations."

"Of course. The allocations." Bierbaum sat down and folded his hands on his desk like a man in a painting. "We meet our quotas, Herr Major. Forty per cent of intake to the Arbeitsamt for the Reich, thirty per cent to the General Government works, twenty per cent to your workshops and the Ostbahn and the hospital services, ten per cent retained for the camp. The Arbeitsamt sent a lawyer last month to complain that we were — they said 'over-counting' the retained category. I invited him to count with me. He counted. He has not written again." He smiled; his teeth were small and even, and there was a boyish cruelty in the smile that made Friedrich's stomach turn. "We all have our battles, Herr Major. Yours is theatre, mine is ammunition. Wait, no — you're not the theatre people. The billets. The workshops. What is it that you need?"

"I need to be sure of the camp's retained list. My office is being asked questions by the commandant's office about the quality of the hands we are being sent, and I would rather answer them with a signature than with a shrug."

"You may have any signature I possess," Bierbaum said, and rang the bell, and the whole thing took twenty minutes, and Friedrich left the office with a carbon copy of the retained list under his arm, having looked into the eyes of an SS officer for twenty minutes and said nothing at all about anything that mattered.

In the yard the work party was loading sacks. A row of women stood against the wall of the school's west wing, waiting for something — the food queue, the latrine, the bath house, Friedrich never knew, and never asked, because asking was not a thing one did, and by not asking he remained a decent man for one more day.

She was fourth in the row.

He had not expected to see her. He had come for a piece of paper; he had told himself that on the bridge and had believed it approximately. She was standing with her back against the wall and her eyes closed and her face turned up a little, and the rain was falling on it, and there was a bruise high on her cheekbone that had not been there yesterday, and her hair — the shorn hair was growing already, or had been growing for a week before the razor, and in the rain it was dark and flat against her head and made her look about sixteen. She was holding a piece of bread in one hand and there was a woman asleep against her shoulder, and the two of them were propped against the damp brick like two people on a station platform, and Friedrich stood thirty feet away in the middle of the yard with a driver behind him and a list under his arm and a great many people for whom he was responsible, and looked at her for the space of four breaths.

He did not go to her. That was the choice he made, standing in the mud of the schoolyard, and he made it in the full knowledge of what it was, which was the only courage available to him: if he walked to her there, in front of the Ukrainians and the work party and Bierbaum's window, then he was not a Major on the staff with an interest in a labour allocation; he was a German officer who had gone to a transit camp to stand beside a Polish woman, and in this city, in this year, that was a thing that men died for, and — worse, infinitely worse, and this was the thought that turned him round like a hand on his shoulder — the child would die for it first.

So he walked to the gate. And at the gate, because he was who he was, and because a certain kind of Prussian cannot leave a scene without tidying it, he stopped and said to the auxiliary, in German, in the flat voice of a man disposing of a minor matter: "The labour detail Three. The children. They are to be kept at sorting work until further instruction. No transfer, no transport, and no examination of any kind without the written order of the commandant's office. My office. Understood?"

The auxiliary did not understand, or did not care, and the clerk at the gate understood enough to write it in the book, and Friedrich got into the car and Kellner drove him away over the bridge through the rain, and all the way back up the avenue Friedrich stared out of the window at the wet city and thought about a card index, and about a stamp with a file number on it, and about how much of his life, from this hour, was going to consist of reaching across the desk of other men to alter papers.

At the office there was a message in Klara's careful hand: *The race office telephoned at 15.20 regarding retained list No. 3. Obersturmführer Dörffel's office, Szucha, requests the file on the Wojtera case. Referred to you. The Obersturmführer asks whether the Herr Major has an interest in the case.*

He read it twice, standing in the doorway in his wet coat, and then he said, "Who took the call?"

"I did, Herr Major."

"Then you know what to write."

Klara's pen was still. "I am not sure I know what to write, Herr Major. That is why I gave you the message."

Friedrich looked at her, at the pale, flat, careful face under the lamp, at the photograph of her parents on the desk, at the little silver church pin from Szpitalna Street on the collar of her blouse. She was, he understood suddenly, one of the very few people in the General Government who had kept her hands clean, and she had just discovered that keeping her hands clean was a job like any other, with its duties and its hours.

"You write," Friedrich said, "that the Wojtera case falls under the labour jurisdiction of the commandant's office. You write that the file will be forwarded when it has been completed, in the ordinary course. And then you will make a note in the diary and you will not say another word about it to anyone, ever, including Wollweber."

"Yes, Herr Major."

"And Klara." He had never used her first name before, and they both heard it, and it changed the temperature of the room. "Noted the date and the time of the call, and who asked. That is a matter of record. I have learned, in this war, that the only thing a German officer can do for a Pole is be scrupulous with the paper."

Klara Seidler looked at him for a moment with her pale, flat, careful eyes, and then she wrote, and the nib scratched, and outside on Nowy Świat the rain went on falling on a city that had sixty thousand people under sentence of one kind or another in the drawers of its thousands of offices.

Friedrich went into his own room and shut the door and stood with his forehead against the cold of the window and looked down at the wet street, where a small boy was running with a package held against his chest, and a tram was shrieking round the corner, and a German military policeman had stopped a woman to look at her papers, and the woman was smiling, the way they all smiled, the smile that had no bottom to it, the smile the city had invented and called *the Polish face*.

And he thought about what he had told her nine years ago in a pavilion in the Englischer Garten while a band played somewhere behind the trees: that he would never be like his father. And he thought, for the first time without flinching, about the fact that a child had been born in Warsaw on the third of December, 1933, and that the sum of all his father's careful cruelty, all his own careful cowardice, and all his family's careful pride, was shut in a schoolyard in Praga with a number after her name and a stamp on her card, and that the only currency he had left in the world, the only currency anyone in Warsaw had, was paper.
