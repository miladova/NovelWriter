# Chapter Six: The Courthouse

*Warsaw — Monday, 7 June 1943*

The welfare office on Marszałkowska opened at eight, and by half past seven the queue along the wall was two hundred people long, and Wanda Ostrowska had learned, in three years of standing on the other side of such queues, that the most dangerous thing in the world is hope with a number on it.

She came in through the courtyard, past the coal shed and the women already waiting there with their papers tied up in string, and up the back stairs, because the front stairs had the queue on them and the queue on the front stairs would have asked her questions; and in her office, which had a desk, two chairs, a stove, a filing cabinet with four hundred lives in it and a window with a view of a wall, she took off her coat and put on the grey cardigan she always wore at the desk, and became, for the day, a woman with an armband and a ledger, which was the best disguise in Warsaw, because nobody in the history of the world has ever looked twice at a woman with a ledger.

At half past eight Pani Jadwiga brought in the first ten cases, and she brought them in the way she had been taught to bring them in: by number, not by name, because a name said loudly in a waiting room is a name that somebody remembers.

Case 411: a woman of sixty with two grandchildren, the parents deported in January, no money, no ration cards because the cards had been issued to the parents' address and the address had been sealed. Wanda wrote her the yellow card, the child-feeding card, the milk ticket, and put her on the Thursday list for the shoe distribution, and told her, when she got up to go, quietly, without looking up from the ledger: "There is a woman at the paper shop on Wilcza who keeps sacks. Tell her that the swallows are back."

Case 412: a woman of thirty-five with a boy of thirteen who had a withered arm. She wanted a work certificate for the boy, because the boy had been called up by the Arbeitsamt for registration and she had heard that a registration meant a transport. Wanda looked at the boy's arm; and looked at the woman; and said, "This certificate will say that the boy assists in the house and is not fit for employment. It will be true. It will also be worthless, because the labour office does not care whether a boy is fit; the labour office cares whether a boy is on a list. Bring him back on Thursday and I will put him on the register of the children's home on Tarczyńska, which will make his address a home address, and a home address is looked at by a different desk than a private address, and a different desk is nine days of the boy's life. Nine days is not much. Nine days is what I have to give."

Case 413 was a woman Wanda had known before the war, from the committee at the Szymanowski school, and she had brought a letter from a priest in Żoliborz and asked for the second kind of help, the kind that does not go in the ledger. Wanda took her into the corridor, because the corridor was safer than the office for the second kind of help, and read the letter, and said, "Not this week. There's a boy coming out of the ruins this week and I can only make one hole in the wire at a time. Tell the priest to keep the family quiet and indoors and to tell the neighbours nothing, nothing at all, and to burn the letter."

"As God is my witness we have told nobody."

"Then tell the mirror," Wanda said, and gave the letter back, and looked at the woman with an expression that had nothing in it at all, and went back into the office, and shut the door, and stood for a moment with her hands flat on the desk.

That was the part nobody understood: that the work was not made of brave decisions. It was made of ledgers, and of arithmetic, and of saying no to a woman you had known since before the war because the hole in the wire had been booked for a different child; and if you wept about it, you wept at night, alone, in your own kitchen, and never, ever at the office, because a welfare worker who weeps in her office is a welfare worker who is watched, and a welfare worker who is watched is a welfare worker who gets other people killed.

The second kind of help that week was a bunker under Wilcza Street.

Since the sixteenth of May, when the fights in the ghetto had ended and the great synagogue on Tłomackie had been blown up with a German engineer's careful satisfaction, the city east of the courthouse had been a dead country: sixty-odd hectares of broken wall and standing chimney and cellar hole, three or four storeys of nothing, drawn with a ruler as you looked at it from the roof of the pralines factory on Żelazna — which Wanda had done, once, in the last week of May, with a man from the underground who wanted to know what could be seen from there, and the two of them had stood on the roof among the dish racks and looked east across the desert of the ghetto and had said nothing at all for a quarter of an hour.

But the dead country was not empty. That was the thing the Germans, with their rulers and their labour battalions, had never quite learned. Men and women were still down there: in the bunkers that had been dug before the fighting and not found; in the sewers, whose maps the Poles had and the Germans did not; in the cellars of the houses on the Aryan side whose back walls were in the ruin. There were, by the best count the underground had in June, between two and five hundred people living in the ruin and under it, in the dark, on rainwater and on what came over the wall at night, and every one of them was a person for whom the whole apparatus of the richest state in Europe had a single question, which was: *how did you get out, and who helped you?*

Wanda's job, that Monday night, was a boy of eleven called Edek.

He was the son of a woman who had been a bootmaker in the small ghetto, and he had been in a bunker under the ruins of Nowolipki since the eighth of May, when the group of fighters he had been hiding with had come up out of the sewers and gone to ground, and the fighter in charge, a young man named Marek who had lost the hearing in one ear, had sent a message out through the sewer to the only contact he had left, which was a woman who worked in the welfare office on Marszałkowska Street. The message had three words: *the child and the case was closed and Edek was the last of the group that had been in the cellar at Miła.*

They met in the yard of the Church of the Holy Cross at nine o'clock, when the curfew was an hour gone and the city had gone indoors: Wanda, and a man from the waterworks called Broda, who was fifty and drove a municipal truck and knew the length, depth, slope and smell of every drain in the central district, and two boys of fifteen who were the sewer boys, Wojtek and Ludwik, who went in and out of the ruin six times a month for the underground and the welfare office and the Jewish committee, and who had been paid, in the way of all sewer boys, in cigarettes and in being alive.

The entrance was a manhole in the courtyard of the empty factory on Gęsia: four hundred metres from the German guard post in the cordon, in a courtyard where a bone works had been until the Germans took it, and the cover had been kept moveable by the simple method of putting a shed over it and calling the shed a store.

The two women went down second: Broda first, in rubber waders, then the boy Wojtek, then Wanda, then the boy Ludwik, with the torches, and the rope.

The first ten metres were the worst because of the smell, and after ten metres the smell was the house you lived in and it stopped being a thing at all. The pipe was a metre and a half of brick egg, with the flow at the bottom, and the flow in June 1943 was the colour of black tea with a skin on it and it came to her mid-calf and it moved at a walking pace and it took every ash of the ghetto eastward to the river, and the two sewer boys went along it as easily as people walk along a passage at home, one hand on the wall, one hand for the torch, and the two torches made two small red circles on wet brick and made everything else look like the inside of a mouth.

They walked for twenty minutes and then they turned left into a side pipe and then right into a chamber, and then Broda stopped and said, "Now the short one," and they went into a pipe that was a metre and thirty and had not been maintained since 1937 and was full of things Wanda decided, at the start of it, that she was not going to look at, and then the pipe went downhill for fifty metres, and Wanda, whose head was down and whose eyes were on the boot of the man in front, heard, quite clearly, a violin.

Two notes, then a third, then a stroke of four, in the dark, in a sewer under Warsaw, played very softly and very well, a phrase in E minor that she recognized without being able to name, stopping and starting; and after it, a man's voice, low and patient, saying something that went down the pipe to her in a rhythm without words.

"That's Marek," the boy Wojtek said quietly. "He plays in the dark. He says it keeps the rats down. He's got a violin he brought down with him on the eighth of May and no strings for the E, so he can't play anything with an E in it, and he says that every piece he loves has an E in it, and when a person comes out who can get him a string he is going to sit down and cry."

They came up into the bunker through a hole where a wall had been knocked, and Wanda stood in the middle of a cellar ten metres by six, lit by three candles and a fat lamp made out of a tin of grease, in which there were eleven people: a man of seventy with his wife; three women of middle age; a boy; a girl of four; and the young man with the violin, who was twenty-four, who was so thin that his cheekbones had turned his face into something carved, and whose left ear was scarred with a burn.

They had: straw, four blankets, two buckets, a sack of potatoes, the violin, and a hole in the floor where the water came in from the pipe.

Marek said, when they had finished, quietly: "Take him and go. The girl goes nowhere; she's got the fever, she's had it since Friday. If you take a fevered child through the pipes she dies in the pipes, and the pipes are two hours long, and if she cries down there we all die, and I will not do that to you." He looked at Wanda with the flat, apologetic, savage calm of a man who has been living in a hole for a month. "Nobody else comes. The old ones cannot walk it. We have the water; we have what Ludwik brings. In September they'll have forgotten us. In September I will write a letter to a man in London and tell him what we did here, and if he answers, I will buy a new string for my E."

"He'll answer," Wanda said. "The violinist isn't finished."

The boy Edek was eleven years old and had been in the dark for four weeks and came up out of the hole like an animal coming up out of a burrow, with his fists and his shoulders up, blinking, shaking, silent; and he had a sack with a pair of shoes and a photograph in it; and he would not let go of Wanda's hand for the first hundred metres and then, in the middle of the long pipe, with the water at their knees and the torches making their two red mouths down the tunnel, he let go and walked by himself for ten metres and then took her hand again, and that, Wanda thought, was the boy's whole history, walked out in the dark in front of her: he had been afraid alone for four weeks, and now he was afraid next to somebody, and he was testing which one he could bear.

They came up out of the sewer at a manhole in the courtyard of the ruin of the Grodzki courthouse.

That was Broda's whole art, and she had never asked him how he knew it, and she never would: that on that side of the building, the wall of the courthouse yard was a wall of the ghetto, and on this side it was not, and the manhole was in the strip of the courtyard that had belonged to neither, and it was, in June 1943, twenty-five metres from the German cordon post and invisible from it, because the soldier there was a young man with a rifle and a bicycle who watched the empty street, and the courtyard behind him was a place where a person with a torch could stand in the open air and look up at the sky.

They stood in the ruin for the four minutes it takes for a boy to breathe.

The courthouse had been shot at in April and shelled in May, and one wall of the yard was down and two were standing with their window frames hanging out of them like the ribs of a whale, and the middle wall, the one with the gates, the one that had let four hundred children out of the ghetto one at a time in 1942 through the doors that opened on Leszno and closed on Ogrodowa, was still standing, blackened, with its arch and its tiles and the little carved stone face over the arch that had a mustache and looked down on the courtyard with an expression of complete indifference, which is, in the end, the only expression that architecture can have.

Above the wall, to the east, was the dead country, and a smell of dust and lime and something under the dust that was not dust, and, here and there, far off, in the grey of the night, a lamp: a work party of Poles under German guard, clearing the rubble at the corner of Nalewki by lamplight, because the Germans cleared the rubble all summer, for the brick and the iron and the salvage, and because in the rubble there were things worth selling, and among the things worth selling there were things that men had hidden under the floors in the winter of 1942.

And there was, Wanda knew, because Broda had told her, one other thing in the dead country that the Germans had found and were using: since the middle of May the ruins had been a place of execution, and twice a week the vans came from Pawiak prison, four hundred metres up the street, and the men from the Gestapo shot the prisoners into the cellars of the burnt houses, and the Poles of Żelazna and Leszno and Ogrodowa had learned to count the shots, the way people in the country count the strokes of a church bell.

"Under the wall," Broda said. "Now."

They went along the inside of the wall, and out through the gate on Leszno, and Edek walked with his sack of shoes like a boy going to his aunt's, and the four of them crossed Leszno Street, in June 1943, at ten minutes past eleven at night, in the darkness under the blackout paper and the shuttered lamps, and went up Karmelicka into the ordinary living streets of the Aryan city, where the walls had wallpaper on them and behind the wallpaper a woman coughed and behind the cough a wireless played the German news.

They put the boy in a flat on Żelazna, in a room where there was a bed, a chair, a table, a window with the paper on it, and a crucifix on the wall with a shirt hanging on it. The woman of the flat was called Pani Bronisława, she was fifty-one, she had a face like a nut and hands like a stonemason's, and she had, by Wanda's count, kept fourteen people alive in three years, four of them in that room, and she made the sign of the cross over the boy's forehead and said, "Antek. You're Antek Wiśniewski, from Krasnystaw, your mother is dead, your father is in the Reich, you came from your aunt's on Tuesday, and if anybody asks you anything, you cry. Cry like a real boy; real boys of eleven cry about once a day. Now eat."

The boy looked at the bowl of soup and then he said, in the local Yiddish vowel that he had been born with and that would be the death of him if he ever used it again, "*Nu*," and then, in Polish, with the accent of Nowolipki: "What's my name?"

"Antek."

"Antek," he said. "And after that?"

"After that," Pani Bronisława said, "every morning somebody tells you your name again. That's what 'after that' means for you now, so eat."

Wanda wrote the boy in the ledger on the table by the window: not his name, but a number, and against the number the words *Antek Wiśniewski, 11 years, from Krasnystaw*, and against that a date, and against that the name of the flat, and then she closed the ledger and put it in the tin under the third floorboard of the room, and drank the tea they gave her, and walked home at two in the morning along the middle of the street in the dark, keeping to the tram tracks because the tram tracks were the only place in Warsaw where a person could not be stopped by the police without a reason.

At four in the afternoon of Tuesday, she went to Hoża Street.

She went in, as always, through the gate past the caretaker, and Zofia Mrożek looked at her red cross armband and her boots and her heavy face, and said, without moving her lips, "Third landing. Wash on the line on the second landing, mind your head."

The cellar was dark, as always, and Rachel Perel was behind the coal bin, in her nest of coats, and she was not alone: the two of them were doing something with thread, in the dark, by touch, and when Wanda's foot came down off the last step, a piece of a song stopped in the air, and then a girl's voice — Marysia's — said, "*Mamo?*"

"It's me," Wanda said. "Pani Wanda."

The two children in the dark: one who lived in the cellar and one who had been let out of the camp for four hours and came down here, Wanda understood in one turn of the stomach, every Wednesday and Saturday, in the two hours between the tram and the curfew, because her mother had gone to work and she had to exist somewhere, and had found, in the dark under the coal, the only friend in Warsaw who could be trusted not to be able to tell.

"They let me out at two," Marysia said, out of the dark. "They let me out because I can sew, and the sewing is all done, so I came here. Nobody saw me. I came through the courtyard with the wash basket like Pani Zofia said."

"Marysia." Wanda's voice, she was glad to hear, was entirely calm. "Where is your mother?"

"At the laundry until six. She said she'd come here at seven."

"Good." Wanda sat down on the box by the door and looked at the two girls in the last of the daylight from the coal chute: one eight years old and hidden for her blood, and one nine years old and hidden for her father, and the two of them together in the dark, sewing, two children in a cellar in the fifth year of the war, doing the same thing in the same way, which was to make themselves unfindable.

She had known, of course, that Helena Wojtera's child was not on the transport lists; you heard things in the welfare office; the women in the courtyard talked. She had not known that the child was out of the camp on a regular basis and playing, on Wednesdays and Saturdays, in the same cellar as Rachel Perel, and the knowledge hit her in the middle of the chest the way the knowledge always did, with the picture that came with it: a cordon, a search, a dog, and two girls in a nest of coats, their two lives hanging on the same nail.

At seven o'clock Helena came, with the smell of the laundry on her and a paper parcel under her coat, and after the two children had eaten the bread and the sugar, and after Marysia had been sent up, through the courtyard, to her own room, with the basket, and the door had shut on the last of the daylight, Wanda said what she had come to say, and she said it without anything in front of it.

"The girl in the cellar must go."

Helena sat down on the box, slowly, and looked at the dark.

"She's been here since February," she said. "Nobody has come. I feed her out of my own ration and she never goes into the courtyard, she never goes to the window. She is the safest thing in this house."

"She is the least safe thing in this house, and you know it, and I will tell you why, and then there is something I am going to ask you, and it is a thing that will make you hate me." Wanda leaned forward. "In February there were two hundred people in this district looking for Jewish children, and they were looking with their ears, in the courtyards, at the gates. In June there are six hundred, because the ghetto has been destroyed and there is nothing left in the ruins for them to catch, so they are all here now, on this side, in the courtyards, at the gates. Do you understand what has changed? In February there were people who cared about nothing, and the ones who cared were a special kind of man, and their price was known. Now the szmalcowniks are ordinary men: a concierge, a taxi driver, a caretaker, a boy from a bakery. In February a child in a cellar was a secret. In June a child in a cellar is a rumour, and the whole profession of this city has one ear on the courtyard and one ear on the stairs, and a child in a cellar makes a noise, Pani Wojtera: the bedding, the coughing, the coal moved at four in the morning when your neighbours are awake and their windows are open because it is June."

Helena was silent. The coal shifted in the bin with a small dry sound.

"On Friday night," Wanda said, "I can move her. I have a place in the country, beyond Grodzisk, a farm, the man is a cousin of the man who fixes my roof. He has kept two children and a woman since 1941 and the village thinks they are his wife's sister's children. A child of eight on a farm: a labour unit, another pair of hands, the sort of child every Polish farm has. She will sleep in a bed. She will go out of doors. In two years she will be a village girl who can milk a cow." She looked at the shape of Helena in the dark. "And if the man is denounced, they will shoot him and his wife and burn the farm, and the children will be taken, and it will be because I sent them."

"Then why are you telling me?"

"Because the place is one place and there are four children on my list for it, and I am going to take the one who is in the most danger, so I have to decide which of the four that is, and I have been deciding it for a week, and I have decided it tonight, in your cellar." Wanda's voice was quite level. "Not because your girl is dearer to God. Because the arithmetic is the arithmetic. Rachel is eight, she is Jewish, she has no papers at all, and if she is found, they take her to Szucha and they find out in four hours through the caretaker's son that there was a flat above the coal bin. Your daughter has a labour card with a German signature on it, and a camp number, and a mother with papers, and if she is found in your cellar she can say — and she will say, because you will have taught her — that she was playing in the yard and came down out of the rain, and she will be believed, because she is a Polish girl with a Polish record, and the most that will happen is that you will lose your labour card, and the worst that will happen is that you will be taken to Szucha and interrogated for three days and released with your teeth gone. That is not the same risk as Rachel's. You know that it is not. You have known it for months. You have simply refused to say it out loud, and I have come here to say it out loud for you."

Helena put her hand over her mouth.

"The transport leaves the East Station on Friday at six in the afternoon; the man who takes the child will be at the number 8 tram stop at Zawiszy at four with a cart and a barrel of salt fish, and she will go in the barrel, and I will come here at half past three to fetch her." Wanda stood up. "It costs eleven hundred zlotys."

"Eleven hundred zlotys."

"For the transport, the papers, and the first three months of the farm, and then two hundred a month, in envelopes, from you, by the hand of the priest at St Barbara's, and if the money stops, I will bring her back to you, because the man's family eats the same as everyone else and I will not lie to him about that either." Wanda pulled on her gloves. "You have a hundred and ten zlotys from your father's watch. I know, because the man at the pawnshop on Żelazna buys for us sometimes and he mentioned your name, which is a thing I will come to in a moment. Where is the rest?"

Helena said, in a voice that did not sound like her own: "The violin."

"A Hopf? You could get six or seven thousand from the right buyer, and a thousand from the wrong one, and a thousand is what I need."

"Not the violin. I'll find it another way."

"There is no other way, and I have to know by Friday morning, and I have to know tonight, so that I can tell the man with the salt fish." Wanda looked down at her, and her heavy face was, for one moment, nothing but tired. "I have taken four hundred and six children out of this city in three years and I have buried sixty of them. I am telling you this not to impress you but to make you understand that I am not your friend and not your enemy. I am a woman with a list, and your child's name is on it — not on this list, on the other list, the one that thinks it is saving her with papers from the Wehrmacht — and I have known for two weeks that somebody on the other side is holding a door open for your daughter, because a labour reserve that survives a racial examination in Skaryszewska in the spring of 1943 does not happen; it is done."

Helena's hands were cold. "How do you know about the examination?"

"Because a clerk in the labour office talks to a woman in my office, because that is how everything in this city comes out in the end, like water coming through a wall." Wanda put her gloves on. "And I will say this one thing to you, Pani Wojtera, and then I will go, and then we will do business on Friday like two strangers discussing a barrel of salt fish. Whoever he is, whatever he is, that German who is keeping your child off the race list — he is not doing it because he is a good man. Good men in that building sign papers that send other people's children to Braunschweig, and I have the lists to prove it. He is doing it because your daughter is his business, and a man's business is the thing he dies for, and in this war, when a German dies, he does not die alone. He takes his house with him. He takes his name, his family, his photographs, and the Poles who touched his hand."

She was at the foot of the steps when Helena said: "I don't know the rest. The money."

"You have a violin," Wanda said. "And in the ledger of the things I have asked people to give up, there is a wedding ring, a piano, a silver samovar, six teeth, and a son. The violin is a violin."

She came up out of the cellar into the courtyard at half past seven. The acacias were in leaf and the light was golden and ordinary, and there were boys on the shed roof, and two women at the well, and Pani Halina Rowińska at her window with her elbows on the sill, and a tram going by in Hoża Street with a German soldier riding on the back platform; and on the bench under the second acacia, sitting with his legs out and his hat on his knee, was a small neat man of about fifty with a chin that went back into his collar like a man who has spent his life being careful with his face.

He had a notebook open on his knee. He was writing in it with a pencil, slowly, in a way that Wanda Ostrowska, who had spent three years learning to recognize the shape of a man writing down a person's face, recognized at a distance of thirty metres the way a doctor recognizes a fever across a room.

She walked past him without turning her head, out through the gate, into Hoża Street. She did not hurry. She had a red cross armband on her left arm and a welfare card in her bag and a face like a stone; and behind her, in the courtyard, the man with the notebook looked up from his writing and watched her go, and then looked back at the thing he had written, which was this:

*W. Ostrowska, welfare, Marszałkowska 72, boots like a man. Wk. 411 ledger.*

and under it, on a new line, in the neat small hand of a man who sells names in a city where a name is worth more than a man:

*Wojtera, H., Hoża 14. Music teacher. Out of Skaryszewska 22/v. Child in camp = interest. Ask at the laundry.*
