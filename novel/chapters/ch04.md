# Chapter Four: The Saxon Garden

*Warsaw — Monday, 24 May 1943*

The laundry of the Wehrmacht hospital on Wiejska Street was in a building that had been a municipal bath house, and it had three hundred and forty kilos of German linen going through it every day, and it was run by a German sergeant-major called Frau Piltz who had been a matron in a sanatorium at Bad Pyrmont before the war and who applied to the occupation of Poland the same standards of hygiene and the same low opinion of human beings that had made her a success at Bad Pyrmont.

Helena worked from six in the morning at the vats, in a steam that took the breath away in the first half-hour and after that took nothing at all; wringing, sorting, counting, marking the bundles with a wooden tally. There were twelve Polish women and one Pole who had been a notary in Lublin, and there were two German women soldiers of the Nachrichtenhelferinnen corps who fetched and carried and considered themselves superior to the vats, and there was Frau Piltz in a white coat with her sleeves rolled and a whistle on a cord that she had never once blown, because her voice was sufficient.

She had an apprentice's eye. She walked the line twice an hour, and the second time she walked it she came back to Helena, and stood in front of her with her hands on her hips and looked at her hands.

"You have never washed anything in your life," she said, in German.

"I have washed my own things for ten years."

"In a basin. Look at that." Frau Piltz took her by the wrist and turned the palm up, and Helena's palm was unmarked: no callus at the base of the fingers, no burn, no line across the tips. "You play something. Piano? Violin?"

"Violin."

"Hm." Frau Piltz looked at her for a moment, and there passed over her face something that Helena would not have expected to see there and would not see again: a movement of opinion, a small rearrangement, the look of a woman of the world recognizing another woman of another world, both of them now working for an army in a bath house. "Then do not spoil your hands, because when this is over there will be music in Bad Pyrmont and there will be no laundries. Sort the bundles." She turned away, and added, without turning back, "Here in the fourth vat, the water is hot. The third vat is hot only for the first hour. Everybody works in the third vat and I have stopped correcting them. Now you know. That is my fee. You will pay me back when you can play."

At a quarter past one a boy in a Hitler Youth shirt came into the laundry with a slip of paper and asked loudly for *die Polin Wojtera*, and the whole line of women stopped, because in the fourth year of the occupation there were only three reasons why a boy in that shirt came for anybody: work, interrogation, or a family matter with a German stamp on it.

The paper said:

*FRAU H. WOJTERA is required to attend the office of the Wehrmacht-Kommandantur Warschau, Referat für Arbeitseinsatz, Nowy Świat 71, Room 214, on Monday 24 May at 16.00 hours, concerning the registration of a minor child, labour case no. 3/Skaryszewska.*

*Attendance is obligatory. Bring this paper and the labour card.*

*For the Wehrmacht-Kommandantur Warschau — v. Brandt, Major.*

At four o'clock she was in the corridor.

It was a marble corridor, and the marble was good, a warm yellowish marble with the veins in it that had been quarried somewhere in Italy and shipped up the Oder and pinned to the inside of a Polish insurance building by men who had thought, in 1928, that they were building for their grandchildren. There was a smell of floor polish and cigarette smoke and wet overcoats. There was a queue of Poles at a window further down: contractors, foremen, an engineer with a rolled drawing under his arm, a man with a wheelbarrow license. Two German soldiers walked past with a filing box. A Polish boy of sixteen in a grey smock came along the corridor with a bucket and a rag and got down on his knees and began to wipe the skirting board, and kept his eyes on the skirting board the entire time, and Helena, standing with her back against the wall and her permit and her paper in her hand, understood that she was being shown, with the economy that the occupation applied to everything, exactly where in the order of the world she stood.

At ten past four a woman came out of Room 214 with a shorthand pad and said, in German, "Pani Wojtera? Bitte."

Klara Seidler was perhaps twenty-eight, with pale eyelashes and a long neck, and she looked at Helena for exactly one second longer than an office clerk looks at a person who is only a file, and then held the door and said, in German, "The Herr Major will see you now. You will speak German. You will stand until you are asked to sit. You will not address him unless he addresses you."

"Of course."

The room had a desk, a chair on each side of it, a map of the General Government with red pins in it, and a window with a view over the roofscape of Nowy Świat to the towers of the city. It had a cupboard with four hundred folders in it. It had a photograph in a leather frame on the side of the desk that Helena, sitting where she was told to sit, was very careful not to look at and then, when she was told to sit and had sat, looked at once.

It was a woman of about thirty-five with a broad unsmiling face and a hat that was not quite right, and it had been taken in a garden in about 1938, and there was no child in the picture, and there was a white space at the bottom left of the frame where a second photograph had been removed and the leather had not faded around it.

Friedrich von Brandt came in from the outer office with a folder under his arm and sat down and opened it, and said, "The child Maria Anna Wojtera. Detail three, Skaryszewska. Born 3 December 1933."

"Yes."

"Such a child is not a prisoner; that must be understood. She is a labour reserve. She is being kept at Skaryszewska because the labour office has not yet decided where her work can best be used." He turned a page, and his voice was the voice of an officer at a desk, and the whole of him was an officer at a desk: the tunic buttoned to the collar, the hands, the level flatness of the vowels, the eyes on the paper. "By regulation of the Reichsarbeitsministerium of 1942, a person under fourteen may be registered for light work under the guardianship of the labour office when the mother is employed in war work. You are employed in war work. That is your paper." He pushed a yellow slip across the desk. "It gives you the right to visit the child at Skaryszewska on Tuesdays and Saturdays between ten and eleven, in the visitors' yard, in the presence of a guard. It does not give you the right to bring food beyond one packet of two hundred grammes. It does not give you the right to correspond. There is no right of appeal."

"Thank you."

"Secondly." He turned the page again. "The camp administration has requested a racial examination of the child under the decree of 1941 concerning children of doubtful ancestry in the occupied eastern territories. This is a formality in most cases." The two of them looked at each other over the top of the folder with faces like two clocks. "In some cases it is not a formality. The examination is conducted by a doctor of the Institut für Rassenhygiene who comes from Posen every second Thursday. He will look at the child. He will look at you. He will ask for a family history of three generations, with dates and places. He will ask who the father was."

The corridor, on the other side of the door, had a sound to it like a distant railway.

"The father is dead," Helena said.

"So the register says. *Vater: unbekannt* — unknown." He shut the folder. "Which is a word, Pani Wojtera, and not a fact, and the difference between those two things is a matter of approximately two hours with a doctor from Posen."

Neither of them had moved. In the outer office, Klara Seidler's typewriter stopped for a moment and then went on, and the sound of it came through the door like rain on a roof.

"Thirdly," Friedrich said, and then he stopped, in the middle of a sentence, and Helena, watching him, saw his right hand come down flat on the folder and stay there, and saw the knuckle of the thumb go white, and she had a moment of such vertiginous double vision that she had to put her own hands in her lap: she was looking at a German officer who was the most powerful man she had ever stood in a room with, and underneath him, thirty-one years old, hollow-eyed, was the boy with the violin case who had walked her home along the Ludwigstraße in the rain in 1932 holding a newspaper over both their heads because they could not afford a tram.

"There is a matter which cannot be discussed in this building," Friedrich said. "There is an interview which, under the regulations governing the employment of foreign nationals, may be conducted in a place of recreation, provided that the responsible officer certifies in writing that no office accommodation was available." He looked up. His eyes were quite steady. He was, Helena realized, terrified, in the exact, controlled, orderly way in which he did everything. "It is ten past four. There is no office accommodation available. There is a park at the end of the street."

They walked along Nowy Świat towards the Saxon Garden, two metres apart, in the four o'clock slum of noise that was the city's traffic: the horse-carts, the rickshaws with their Poles pedalling Germans, the wooden-wheeled carts, the buses with the gas bags on the roof; and in the middle of the crowd, moving at a German pace, the men in grey who owned everything, and the women in fur coats who had come from Riga and Reval and Lodz and were now the queens of the cafés on Nowy Świat, and the Italian soldiers with their feathers, and the boys selling *Nowy Kurier Warszawski* with the news that Germany's friends were winning everywhere.

At the gate of the Saxon Garden there was a sign, in German, that said *Für Zivilpersonen deutscher Volkszugehörigkeit* — for civilian persons of German nationality — and a second sign beside it that told Poles where they were permitted to walk, and a third, on the pole, that said that the feeding of pigeons was prohibited.

They went in and walked down the lime avenue, past the fountain with the four river gods, and stopped at a bench that had, in green paint on its back, the words *Nur für Deutsche*.

Von Brandt stood in front of the bench and did not sit on it. He did not, Helena observed, sit on it for the entire hour. He took off his cap and turned it over in his hands like a man holding a bowl, and said, "There is a thing I have to ask you, and it can be asked out here, and I want you to answer it, and if you tell me to go to hell I will accept it and go on doing what I can, so you lose nothing by the answer."

"Ask."

"In the summer of 1933," he said, "you wrote to me that you had been mistaken in your feelings, and that you had taken the money my father offered, and that I was not to write to you again."

"Yes."

"Did you write that letter?"

The fountain ran; the lime trees dripped; somewhere along the avenue a nanny with a pram was walking with a soldier's wife from Kassel or from Posen, and the two of them were talking about rations. A long way off, past the iron railing of the garden, the trams went by on Marszałkowska.

"I wrote it," Helena said, "with your father's pen, at his desk, in his study, on the thirtieth of May, 1933, in German, at eleven o'clock in the morning, and he dictated it, and I signed it, and then I asked him whether I could have a cup of tea, and he sent for one, and he was very kind to me, and he told me that I was a brave girl." She heard her own voice and it was a flat voice, and it had in it exactly what his voice had in it when he read from the folder. "Your uncle's residence permit — my uncle, Stanisław, who had a shop in the Türkenstraße and had come to Munich in 1924 and had nothing in the world but that shop and a wife with a bad heart — was in the hands of a police official who had coffee with your father's adjutant on Thursdays. That was the first thing he told me. The second thing was that you would be finished: that the Reichswehr would not take a man married to a Polish woman without citizenship, that his name would be struck from the lists, that his father's name was known in Berlin and would be smeared. The third thing was that he would put a hundred and fifty marks on the table for my passage and five hundred for my uncle's debts, and that I could have the second of those in the form of a draft on the Bayerische Hypothekenbank, and that if I did not take it in the form of a draft he would take it as a sign that I intended to make trouble, and that he was, he said, a patient man, but that he had seen off better people than me." She looked at the grey water in the fountain. "I took the draft. I never cashed it. It is in a tin box under my floor, and it has been there for ten years, and if you want it back you can have it, because there is a limit to what I will be paid for anything."

He stood quite still with his cap in his hands. Two Polish girls came down the avenue past them, in coats that had been turned twice, and looked at Helena with her shorn hair and then at him in his grey uniform, and away, quickly, with the expressionlessness of the city.

"I came to Warsaw in September 1933," he said.

Helena's breath stopped in her throat.

"I had thirty-six hours' leave. I came on the night train, and I had eighty marks and a photograph, and I went to Hoża Street, and I stood in your courtyard." He was looking at the fountain and not at her. "There was a man in the yard. The caretaker's husband. He asked me what I wanted and I said I had come about the piano, which was the first lie that came into my head, and he said that the young lady was from home, and he went and stood by the gate with his arms folded until I had gone. I stood in the rain outside for an hour and a half. I looked up at your windows and I thought that if I went up and you told me it was true, the letter, I would do something stupid, and if you told me it was not true then I would have to go home and burn my father's house down, and I could not decide which of those two things I wanted, so I stood in the street, and then I went to the station." He turned the cap over, once. "There was a window with a curtain up on the second floor. It moved once. I told myself it was the wind. It was the wind, probably."

"It was not the wind," Helena said.

The silence between them went on for a long time. In the middle of it, everything that could be said about ten years was present and unsayable: that on the evening of the fourteenth of September 1933, she had been sitting at that window in an armchair with the eiderdown over her knees, knitting a bootee, three months pregnant, with a sickness in her that nobody but her father's housekeeper knew about; that she had seen a man come into the courtyard and stand in the rain among the acacias, and that she had gone down the two flights in her dressing gown and stockings in the dark, silently, sick with hope; and that by the time she reached the courtyard there had been nobody there but the wind, and that she had stood in the wet with her bare feet in her slippers and told herself that she was becoming someone who saw things.

"If you had come up," she said.

"Yes."

"No." She shook her head, once, hard. "It was better that you did not come up, because if you had come up I would have told you about the child, and you would have married me, and you would now be an officer in the German army with a Polish wife and a half-Polish daughter in a city where the Gestapo keep lists, and all three of us would be in the ground." She looked at him and her eyes were dry and bright and terrible. "So you did the right thing by accident, and I did the right thing by accident, and between the three of us — you, me, your father — we have kept a child alive for nine years. That is the whole truth of it. Nobody in this story is a hero and nobody in it is innocent; we are just two people who were careful."

"Two people who were careful," he repeated, as though tasting it. "That's what I have told myself every morning for ten years, dressed and shaved and put on a cap, and gone to the office and been careful."

"Now," Helena said. "The child."

"Now the child." He put his cap on at last, and the change in him was immediate and complete: the officer's face came down over the young man's face like a shutter. "At the examination, a doctor from Posen will ask you three questions. One: whether there is any German ancestry in your family within three generations. Two: whether the father of the child is known to you. Three: whether you consent to the examination of the child at all. If you say yes to the third, the child is examined and photographed and measured, and a file is made, and the file goes to Posen, and in six or eight weeks there will be a decision. If you say no to the third, the file does not go, and the child is transferred, as an uncooperative case, to the administration of the Sicherheitspolizei, which means Szucha, which means I cannot reach her with any paper in the world."

"Then I say yes."

"Then between now and the examination, which will be a fortnight on Thursday, you must do the only thing that will make that examination survivable." He said it without any change of tone. "You must give them a father. A Polish father, with a name and a parish and a date of death, and a family that can be looked up in a register, and no German ancestry at all, because a Polish file is a dead file: they do not investigate Poles. And you cannot stagger in there and invent a man: the doctor from Posen has interrogated four thousand Polish mothers, and he can smell a lie at twenty metres, and a lie at the examination means the file goes straight to Posen as a suspect case and then we are back to Szucha. It has to be a man who existed. It has to be a man who can be verified in a church register by a clerk in this city. It has to be a man who left a widowed mother who will say the same thing you say."

Helena stood in the lime avenue in the Saxon Garden in the late afternoon of the twenty-fourth of May 1943, in the year of the destruction of the ghetto, with her shorn head and her labour card and her two hundred grammes a week of visiting rights, and she thought about it: about her father, dead in 1939; about her mother, dead in 1938; about the cousin in Piaseczno who wrote once a year; about the men she had refused in ten years, and the two she had allowed to kiss her and regretted, and the notary and the doctor and the officer of the Home Army who had asked, and about the fact that not one of them, not one, was a verifiable man in a register with a widowed mother who would lie for her.

"There was a man," she said slowly. "Adam Wójcik. A railway man. He was in my father's section on the Wilno line. He died — it was said he died — in the spring of 1933, before the child was born; my father told people we were engaged. He was from Grodno, and there is nobody left of his family, they were all deported in 1940, and no clerk in Warsaw can check Grodno, because Grodno is Russian now."

Von Brandt looked at her, and it was the first time in an hour that his face had done something without his permission: a small, crooked, painful smile crossed it and was gone.

"That," he said, "is almost a perfect lie. Where did you learn to lie like that?"

"I have had nine years' practice," Helena said. "And there is one thing in it, Herr Major, and I want you to hear it: if you had come up those stairs in 1933, then the man who got the child would have been you, and I would have had a husband, and she would have had a name, and whatever else we were, we would have been an ordinary family with an ordinary sorrow." She looked at him. "As it is, she has a dead man for a father, and you have a dead man's place in a park, and when they ask me at the examination who the father is, I will say Adam Wójcik, and I will say it in front of you if you like, and I will say it every day for the rest of my life."

Friedrich von Brandt did not answer that. He stood with his hands behind his back and his face turned up towards the pale sky over the Saxon Garden, and he was silent for a long moment, and Helena, looking at him, saw his throat move and saw him, with an act of the will that was almost visible, put the thing away and become again what he had to be.

"Very well," he said at last. "Then here are the terms. You will not come to Nowy Świat again. You will not write to me, you will not send me anything, and if you see me in the street you will not know me. If you are taken by the police, you will say exactly this: that the labour office required you to give evidence about your employment, that you spoke to a clerk, and that you do not remember his name. You will not know my name. Say it."

"I will not know your name."

"There will be a paper every month for the child's reserve status; it is renewed from my office; it buys her the right to exist as a labour resource rather than as a racial case." He looked down at her. "Every month, understand, it will cost something, and the something will not be money, and it will not always be something you would like to give. There will be a morning when I ask you for a thing that makes you hate me. When that morning comes, I want you to remember this bench and this park and this hour, and to remember that you asked me for it."

"I have not asked you for anything."

"No," he said. "You have not, and that is the worst of it." He put his cap on straight. "Tuesday and Saturday, ten until eleven, at the wire. Bring the child a pencil. Do not bring her hope; it is not permitted at the gate."

He walked her to the Marszałkowska gate because it was, in the logic of the occupation, the correct gate for a Pole to leave by, and he went with her along the avenue as far as the last lime tree and then stopped and said, in a voice that carried, so that anyone listening would hear a German officer dismissing a Polish employee: "That is all. You will bring the paper. You will not be late again."

"Thank you, Herr Major."

She did not look back. She went out of the gate into the roar of Marszałkowska Street with the crowd and the rickshaws and the boys with the newspapers, and she got twenty metres down the street before she allowed herself to turn her head, and when she did she saw him at the head of the avenue: a tall grey-backed figure with his cap on, standing perfectly still in the middle of a path down which no Pole might walk.

And she saw, too, the other thing, the thing that made her heart go small and cold in her chest like a stone dropped in water: a young lieutenant with a beautiful shine on his boots, twenty-three years old, who had come out of the trees at the far end of the fountain with his hands behind his back as if he had been strolling for some minutes, and who was now standing at a correct distance behind his Major and waiting to be noticed, and who had — Helena was almost certain, from two hundred metres away, with her shorn head and her two hundred grammes of visiting rights and her one hour in a lime avenue — already been watching them for some time.

That night she wrote the statement on a half-sheet of paper at the kitchen table, by the light of a candle because the current was off in the whole street: *I, Helena Wojtera, music teacher, of Hoża 14, Warsaw, state that the father of my daughter Maria Anna was Adam Wójcik, a railwayman of Grodno, deceased in the spring of 1933. I have no further information as to his family. I have never applied to any German authority for anything concerning my daughter, and I ask nothing for myself.*

***

*Warsaw — Tuesday, 25 May 1943*

The visitors' yard at Skaryszewska was a triangle of gravel between the west wing of the school and the coal heap, with the wire running down the middle of it, and it was open on Tuesdays and Saturdays between ten and eleven, and it was, Helena understood in the first five minutes, not a place where people visited their children. It was a place where the camp conducted a lottery and charged nothing for the tickets.

There were thirty women at the wire on the Tuesday, and eleven of them were mothers of children in detail three, and the rest were mothers of men and women and grandmothers of everybody, and the whole crowd of them stood with both hands through the wire like people standing in a river, and talked at the same time, in the way people talk when every sentence is priced by the minute.

The guard was a man of about fifty in a black Ukrainian uniform who walked his twelve-metre beat with his hands behind him, and who was, Helena learned in the first hour, one of the three things in the whole camp that money could reach.

"You have twenty-five minutes and four seconds," he said to her, in Polish, as she took her place at the wire. "There is a Jew in the next yard and a Pole in the office, and I am a Ukrainian, and I do not care about any of you, and if you put your arm through the wire above the second line I will hit you, not for the rules, but because the second line is where the sergeant looks."

Marysia came out of the west door at ten past ten with eleven other children, in a line, in the grey shirts, with their hair grown out into dark caps.

The first thing Helena noticed was that the child had learned to walk in a line the way the children in the camp walked in lines: not shuffling, not slow, a good, quick, level, invisible walk, the walk of a person who has understood that any movement noticed by a man in a green uniform is a movement that costs.

The second thing was that the child was carrying something: a little bundle of rags, tied with thread.

"You," Marysia said, at the wire, in the flat voice of a person keeping count of a debt.

"Me."

"Three minutes to set up and then the guard comes back this way," the child said, and knelt down at the wire so fast that Helena's heart jumped, and put her hands to the rags, which were three-quarters of a hand high, and made of grey army cloth cut into a shape, and had a neck and two arms and a head, and were stitched, Helena saw at once, with real stitches, in rows, in white thread, the rows of a person who has learned to stitch on a corpse of rags under a German lamp.

"This is Fela," Marysia said, in Polish, very low, keeping her head down over her work. "Three years old. She doesn't talk. She came from the road at Zamość with her sister and her sister was taken out of the bath house on the fourteenth and Fela has not talked since, and they put her next to me at the sorting because I can count, and last week she started to shake at night and sing a thing with no words, and the guard came in with a torch and I put my hand on her mouth and now she has got the shakes all the time." The needle went in and out. "So I am making her a doll and it has to be finished before Saturday, because when a child has a thing of her own she stops shaking, that is the truth, Pani Rybak's boy told me, he said that they gave the children at the orphanage a piece of wood each and they stopped crying, and so I am stitching, and I need white thread and a needle and three more pieces of cloth, and I need them before Saturday, Mamo, because the doctor is coming here on Thursday."

"What doctor?"

"An officer doctor in a car with a chauffeur. He came last week and he sat in the office with Bierbaum and he has got a book with photographs in it, and there is a girl called Elschen in the next dormitory and she says he asks her name in German and she has forgotten her Polish name, she has been here since she was four, and they are going to send her to a German family and give her a doll with a china face, she says, and she was singing about it last night, it is the only thing she sings." The child's head came up, and the grey eyes held hers through the wire, and the eyes were steady and absolutely without self-pity, which was the most frightening thing Helena had ever been looked at with. "If he asks me about my father, what do I say?"

"You say his name and you say he died before you were born and you do not say anything else. Not about the violin, not about Munich, not about a single German word, unless the doctor asks for the counts in German and then you say them in a voice like you're saying the multiplication tables, like something learned by rote, without looking at anybody."

"Counts?"

"When you sort the buttons. You said you could count them in German."

"*Eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf, sechs*," Marysia said, all in one breath, fast, singsong, dull, in the voice of a child repeating what the guards shout, and then looked at her mother with one eyebrow up in a way that was so exactly her father's that Helena had to put her hand flat on the wire to hold on to herself.

"Exactly like that," she said. "Now listen to me. Twenty minutes. They will ask the doctor's questions here in the office or in the little room with the shutters, and there will be a nurse and a clerk, and the door will be open, and if anything at all in the room frightens you, you say, in Polish, in your ordinary voice, that you need the toilet, and they will take you out, and that is a rescue five seconds long, and you can find five seconds three times in an hour. That is all you have to remember. Five seconds, three times, and the truth about Adam Wójcik, which is not a lie, because I never told you one, and don't ever decide you know better than me about this. Give me your hand."

The hand came through the wire, small and quick, and it had a fresh scratch along the palm and the calluses of a person who has been lifting wet cloth for three weeks, and Helena turned it over and looked at it, and this, more than the shorn head, more than the grey shirt, more than the wire, was the thing that told her what had happened to her daughter in eight days.

"Sunday is for the piano," Helena said. "And in September, if God is awake the whole night, you will play the Szymanowski for the entire block, and they will all cry, and the guard will cry too, and you will be so vain about it that I shall have to slap you."

"Sunday is for the piano," the child said, as if reciting the second half of a lesson. "Mamo. There was a woman. In the bath house line, the first morning. She had a little girl of five and she was holding her by the hand and a guard came and said the child must go with the children's line and the woman wouldn't let go of the hand, she wouldn't let go, and they hit her hands with a stick, both hands, so that they swelled up like bread, and then they took the child, and the woman stood there with her hands out not doing anything, just shaking them, like this, in the air—" Marysia stopped. Then she said, in a very small voice, the smallest voice that Helena had ever heard her use: "I don't like the nights here. Please don't make me say I like it here. Everybody's aunt says it's not so bad and I want to scream."

"Then scream into your bedroll, and not into the air," Helena said. "Scream with your mouth in the cloth, and then be quiet, and go to sleep, and count the buttons in the morning. That is what my mother told me at Verdun, and I never told you that my mother told me that, did I?"

"No."

"Well, now you know."

"Twenty-five minutes," the guard said, walking past.

At the gate, going out, there was the ritual that the women had organized among themselves in three weeks: the packet, passed through the fence at the corner where the wire met the fence post, out of sight of the office window, two hundred grammes of bread and one hundred of lard, counted by the guard with the eyes of a man who had learned long ago that he could either be a gendarme or a man with a cook, and had chosen. And past the guard, along the row of women, went the thing that Warsaw measured in, the only gold that had not been taken out of the country: the small talk of the wire, in whispers, at speed, while the guard counted.

They were all there, the mothers of detail three: the mother of the twins, from Wola, who could not sing and did not care; the mother of the girl with the swollen glands; Pani Rybak, who had the boy of fifteen in the men's block, and who told Helena, with her chin up and her eyes on the guard, the thing she had not told her in the first week: "They are doing them now. The racial ones. Elschen went to Elschen's family in Posen, she is already gone, and there are two more in the office this week, and the office woman told me it is because the labour office in the city is playing another game, the Wehrmacht wants the children for their workshops, and the SS wants them for the families, and the two of them are fighting over the same name on the same list. Put the bread inside your coat, the woman behind you has been watching your pocket."

And the three of them — Rybak, and Helena, and the mother of the twins — stood at the gate of the camp at Skaryszewska on the twenty-fifth of May, 1943, and watched the office window, where a clerk was writing; and Helena understood that the sentence she had heard in the visitors' yard, out of the mouth of a woman who had heard it from a clerk who had heard it from an office woman, was the most important thing that anyone would tell her for a year; and that the fighting over her child's name was not a personal matter between herself and the Major and the doctor. It was the war: the war moving along the corridors of every office in the General Government, the war between the men who wanted the girl's hands for a factory and the men who wanted her name for a nation, and she and her daughter were not even the battlefield of that war. They were its supplies."

  She copied it twice in a fair hand for the file, and then she sat for a while with the pen in her hand and looked at the tin box on the table in front of her, closed, and at the three unopened letters, and at the photograph inside the lining of her coat, and then she put everything away under the floorboard, and blew out the candle, and lay down in the dark on the side of the bed she had made for herself ten years before, on the side nearest the door, listening, as every mother in Warsaw did, listening, all night, to the street.
