# Chapter Nine: The Notices

*Warsaw — Saturday, 16 October 1943*

The first notice went up on the walls at seven in the morning, and by nine every wall in the centre of the city had one, and by ten there was a man in a long coat reading one of them aloud outside the church on Marszałkowska, because half the city could not read the German and the other half could not bear to read the Polish.

They were printed at the top with the eagle, and at the bottom with the signature of the commander of the Security Police in the Warsaw district, and they said that the persons named below had been sentenced to death by a summary court for acts of sabotage and armed resistance, and had been executed on the sixteenth of October 1943 by hanging, in the streets named; and the names were printed in two columns with their ages, and there were fifteen of them, and at the bottom there was a paragraph that said that in future, similarly, every act of resistance would be met with the same severity, and that the population would do well to remember that the mercy of the German Reich was not without limits, and that the Reich reserved the right to take reprisals against families.

Helena Wojtera stood in the crowd on Marszałkowska at ten past ten in the morning, in her grey coat with the good collar, with her violin under her arm in its case, on her way to a lesson, and read the two columns twice, and found, in the second column, sixth name down, the name she had come to dread finding there since the first week of September:

*Henryk Zaremba, 17, of Warsaw, student.*

They had called him Kuba. He had been her first partner in the whole business of the war: a boy of seventeen with a bad haircut and a bicycle, who had taught her, in three afternoons in a courtyard on Wilcza Street, how to carry a package, how to walk a route, how to feel the eyes of the man behind her and put him off with a window, how to take a tram three stops from the end of the line to see whether the line was being watched, and how, at the moment of danger, to drop the parcel into the nearest doorway and turn into the nearest shop and be a music teacher asking the price of sheet music. He had taught her with terrible care, the way a boy teaches his mother to ride a bicycle: with jokes, and with his hands ready, and with a whistle at the corner of his mouth. He had been arrested on the twenty-eighth of September on the bridge, with leaflets in the lining of his coat; and he had been interrogated at Szucha for eleven days, and he had not given up the girl who had been his pupil, and nobody knows how it was, whether he had nothing to give or whether he had held on to what little he had, but he had been hanged in the street at dawn on the sixteenth, at the age of seventeen, with the leaflets still in his file; and on the same morning, at half past five, Irena Załuska, of the Women's Underground, had been hanged in the same way at the corner of Wolność Street, and she was nineteen; and the two of them hung on the same gallows in the middle of the city, and men in uniform took photographs for the newspaper, and the newspaper sold out by eleven.

Helena read the notice for the third time and then she walked on to her lesson, because it was ten past ten in the morning, and the lesson was at a flat in Mokotów, and part of the lesson — the folded envelope of the *Biuletyn Informacyjny*, four pages, hidden between the sheet music of the second violin part of the Brahms sextet — was in her case under her arm, and the second part of the lesson was due at the flat of a woman on Wspólna Street at half past twelve, and if she stood under the notice crying on Marszałkowska Street, she would be picked up before the clock struck eleven and everything that Kuba had given up his fingernails for would have been given up for nothing.

So she walked, and by the time she reached the corner of Wilcza she had the face on again, the smooth, shut, ordinary Polish face, the one she had learned from Zofia, the one that a city of two million people had learned to wear — the face that says to a policeman: *nothing here, nothing at all, only a woman with a violin.*

She had been in it for six weeks.

It had begun with Tomek on the first of September, in the little room at Hoża 14, with the lamp down and the blackout paper up, and three sentences he had been walking around for a month before he could say them.

"There is a thing that is worse than the leaflets," he had said, "and it is what we do with the people who carry them. Forty couriers in this city. Twenty-two of them are boys between sixteen and twenty, because boys run fast and boys are cheap. Eight of them are dead since July; two have gone over to the Ukrainian police; one is being watched and he doesn't know it yet. I have one woman in the whole district who can carry a parcel through a German check and look like a woman taking her mother's bread home, and she is fifty and she has cancer. Now I am going to ask you a question, and I want you to say no if the answer is no, because a courier who does not want to be a courier is a courier who gets other people killed."

"And the question?"

"Would you carry things for us?"

She had sat there with her hands in her lap and looked at him for a long time: at his flat wide face and his slow way of sitting, and at the line of his jaw, which had thickened in the summer.

And she had thought about all the reasons to say no, and she had found out, in the way that a person finds out the shape of a thing by walking round it in the dark, that the reasons to say no had been removed one at a time, over the summer, by the Germans.

Because in June they had let her bring the child to the wall on Tuesdays and Saturdays, and then, on the fourteenth of July, they had put the child in the room over the gate, and for five weeks she had not been allowed to see her at all. And on the twentieth of August she had stood at the gate of the camp for four hours in the rain with a parcel of bread and an old woman's asthma medicine, because the office had told her at eight in the morning that the child was there, and at eleven they had said she was not there, and at five in the afternoon a Polish clerk had come out to the gate and told her, kindly, that the child had been taken to the hospital on Jagiellońska Street with a boil on her neck the size of a hen's egg and that she had better go home, and she had walked to Jagiellońska and stood across the street from the hospital wall for two hours, because there was a window on the second floor and a nurse who came to it twice, and the nurse had put her hand up — just her hand, palm out, one second — and Helena had gone home and slept; and on the twenty-eighth of August there had been a paper at the gate from the Referat, with a stamp on it, renewing the reserve status, and a line at the bottom, in Klara Seidler's hand, in Polish: *the child is better; the boil was opened on the 26th; she asks for pencils; she is being kept in the labour reserve.*

She had known then, with the certainty of a woman who has spent a summer being moved around a board by other people's hands, that the thing that had kept her daughter alive when the notices arrived in the yard at Skaryszewska with the words *Reichssicherheitshauptamt* at the top was the paper, and that the paper was the Major's, and that the Major's paper was now the only thread holding the child in the world of the living, and that the thread ran through a man she had not spoken to since the twenty-fourth of May.

So in September, when the notices came and the shootings came and the city began to eat itself, she had gone to Tomek and said yes; and Tomek had taught her the last things, in a courtyard on Wilcza Street, with great care, in the way a young man teaches a thing he does not want to teach.

And on the twenty-second of September, in a courtyard on Wilcza Street, at a quarter to nine in the evening, she met, for the first and last time, the boy who had taught her everything.

He was on the wall of the courtyard, up where the roof of the shed met the wall of the house; he had a bicycle clip still on his right trouser leg, and hair that would not lie down, and he looked, in the light of the courtyard lamp with the blackout hood on it, about fifteen.

"You're the violinist," he said. "I know. You don't have to tell me your name and I don't want it. I'm Kuba; that's not my name and you'll forget it. Three rules, and then we'll walk once and I'll watch you and then we're finished with each other forever. One: never carry anything that is worth more than a lesson. A parcel of leaflets is eighteen hours of a man's life; a parcel of letters is forty; a parcel of medicine is a family. Two: never take the same route two days running, and never be early, because a man who is early is a man who is waiting, and a man who is waiting is a man who is looking. Three: if they stop you, be stupid. Not brave. Stupid. Cry. Be a music teacher who has lost her papers. Show them the violin. Nobody in the history of occupation has ever suspected a woman with a violin of anything except being a nuisance, and the ones who look at the violin case are looking at the wrong thing." He had come down off the wall in one movement, landed, dusted his hands, and grinned at her with the confidence of a boy of seventeen who has already decided that he is going to die and would rather not mention it. "Now walk behind me for two streets and tell me who is following us."

There was nobody following them. That was the lesson too.

At the end of September she had carried her first medicine: a packet of insulin and four ampoules of morphine, from a doctor's flat on Hoża to a cellar on Zielna, for the wife of a man who had been taken at Szucha, and she had done it in a box of piano strings, in her violin case, through a check at the corner of Marszałkowska where a Polish policeman looked at her card upside down for four seconds and a German gendarme looked at her legs for two, and had felt nothing at all except a strange, clear, cold happiness that was, she understood later, the thing that all of them felt, the boys and the girls and the men and the women who carried paper through the streets of occupied Europe: not courage, and not patriotism, but the physical happiness of being a person who was doing something instead of being a person to whom things were done.

In October, the sixteenth of October, she read Kuba's name on the wall on Marszałkowska Street, and walked to her lesson with the leaflets in her music case, eleven pages of Brahms between them, and taught a girl of eleven the second violin part of the sextet for fifty minutes, and went home and lay on her bed in the dark without eating, and did not say anything to anybody.

And that night Tomek came, very late, long after the curfew, with his cap in his hand and a cut on his lip, and stood in the middle of the room and said, "Kuba," and she said, "I know," and the two of them stood there with the lamp not lit, and then Tomek said the thing he had come to say, and he said it fast, as a man says a thing he has decided on and is afraid will be talked out of him:

"The night before he was taken, he gave me the name of the German."

"What German?"

"The Loaf," Tomek said. "The one who sold us the list in March. He gave me the name, because he said the contact was going to have to be a woman, and a woman can't go into a building without a name. The man's name is von Brandt. He is a Major in the labour section of the Wehrmacht commandant's office on Nowy Świat, and the department he works in deals with labour allocation for the workshops and the railways, and he is the man who put four hundred Poles on a train to the Reich in the first week of May and then took eleven of them back off it by telephone, in the middle of the night, on the third of May, and those eleven are alive because of it, and I know that because my own cousin is one of the eleven."

Helena stood in the darkness of the little room with her hands at her sides.

"Say it again," she said.

"Von Brandt."

"There are two of them in that building?"

"No. There is one. There is a Major von Brandt in the Referat für Arbeitseinsatz, and there is no other von Brandt in the General Government except a widow in Posen and a Generalmajor in the old-age reserve in Brandenburg, and before you ask me how I know that, the answer is that I have spent four weeks finding out, because a man who does a thing in March and a thing in June is not a fool, and a man who is not a fool must be looked at twice." Tomek was quiet for a moment. "I didn't know, Helka. I didn't know when I brought you in, and I didn't tell you when I found out, and I should have told you, and I didn't, and the reason I didn't is that I did not know which of the two facts was the important one: that your daughter's protector is a German Major, or that the man who has been selling us paper for seven months is the same man."

"What does the command want?"

"The command doesn't know," Tomek said. "I've kept it out of the reports. That's what I came here to tell you. Because the moment I put it in a report, it becomes a thing with a file, and a thing with a file in the underground moves, and the first movement will be that they will ask you to arrange a meeting, and the second will be that they will want to use him, and the third will be that they will want to know why he cares about your daughter, and that is a question I am never going to answer for them, and you are never going to answer for them, and so I have kept it out." He put his cap on. "There. Now that's out of me. Say something."

Helena said, after a while: "He has kept her alive."

"He has kept her alive."

"He has kept her alive four months, and he has not asked me for anything, and there will be a morning when he asks, and it will be a thing that makes me hate him, and I will do it anyway, because that is the trade." She heard her own voice in the dark and it was flat and without any weight at all. "And you're right. You should have told me. And you were right not to. Now go home, Tomek, because it's past one, and if you're stopped on the way I will never speak to you again in this world or the next."

At the end of October, on a Friday, the thing that the book has been circling came to her through a door on Świętokrzyska Street, in a flat with an upright piano and a cactus in the window, where a woman with grey hair and a black dress gave her tea and looked at the folded envelope and said, "Thank you, Pani Wojtera. There is one thing more, if you will. Do you know the smell of gangrene?"

"I know it. I was at the front in 1939 and I have been a hospital washer since May."

"Better than that. There is a boy in the back room who is nineteen years old and was born in Gelsenkirchen, and he is a soldier of the Wehrmacht, and on Tuesday night he walked out of the barracks at the Flak school at Wola with a rifle and sixty rounds and a pair of boots, and he came to us on his own legs, which takes a boy with a conscience and no sense, and on the way he was shot twice by his own side: once through the left forearm, which is not the problem, and once in the side, above the hip, which is. He has been here since two o'clock in the morning. The doctor was here at six and again at noon. He says the same thing both times, which is that the boy has twelve hours, maybe twenty-four, and that the wound is the kind that a German hospital cleans and a Polish cellar kills."

"And what am I for?"

"Because at half past six tonight there is a cordon on the block, the whole block, two streets, and the rickshaws are already stopped at the corner, and we have the boy and the rifle and the sixty rounds and a German uniform under the floor, and if they search this flat they will shoot the three of us, and the boy will be hanged in the yard, and one of us —" the woman stopped, and put down her cup without a sound, and said, "One of us will have to stay with him, and the other two have to walk out through the cordon with nothing at all, and there is a thing you can carry through a cordon tonight that nobody can take off you."

"What is that?"

"Blood," said the woman with grey hair. "You put the coat on him, and you lift his shoulder, and you hold him while the doctor works, and then you walk out of this building at half past six without washing. A woman with wet blood on her coat at a cordon in this city is four hundred zlotys of fine and a night in the station and, if the gendarme is honest, a beating; a parcel in her lining is a wall. That's not the reason I'm asking. I'm asking because you are the one with the papers and the face, and because the doctor needs four hands tonight and I have only got two, and because you said yes to a box of piano strings in September, and this is the same thing, only I'm not going to lie to you about which."

The cordon came at half past six, exactly as she had said: two green lorries at each end of the street, the men out and the lamps in the archways, and the whole street shut with the rickshaws standing in a line. In the back room with the boards up and the lamp turned to the wall, the doctor — Antoni Kwiatkowski, who had been a surgeon at the Praga hospital in another life, and who had hands like a pianist and the eyes of a man who has not slept since 1939 — operated on a boy of nineteen for two hours without anaesthetic except a rag soaked in schnapps between his teeth; and the boy had bitten the rag in two and had then, in the passing-out that comes before the body gives up, called for his mother, and then, in German, in a sobbing whisper, one sentence over and over, which Helena translated for the doctor with her hands in the wound cloths and her arms to the elbow in the boy's blood: *Ich hab nicht geschossen. Ich hab nicht geschossen. Ich hab sie gesehen und ich hab nicht geschossen.* — I did not shoot. I saw them, and I did not shoot.

At nine o'clock, when the search had come up the stairwell to the second floor and gone past the door of the flat with the piano (the woman with grey hair had put the sheet music on the piano and played, out of tune, at the third landing, an hour of Chopin with the pedal down, because the acoustics of a stairwell had been studied by the underground the way roads are studied by an army), the boy's side was closed, and his breathing had changed; and at half past nine they lifted him, the doctor and Helena, and carried him down the back stairs to the coal cellar, and laid him on a mattress in a recess behind the boiler which would, the woman explained, be invisible to a search unless they went into the recess, and if they went into the recess it was over for all of them; and it took the two of them fourteen minutes to carry him down one flight and across a yard, and by the end of it Helena's coat, which had been clean that morning, was black to the elbows, and there were two round marks on the front, exactly where a person's hands go when they are holding a shoulder, and a smear across the sleeve where the boy's head had fallen against her.

At ten o'clock she walked out of the courtyard, and the sentry at the archway of the Świętokrzyska cordon, a gendarme of about forty with the face of a man who wanted his dinner, put his torch on her and said, in Polish, "Stop. Papers."

She stopped, and gave him the labour card and the residence card and the pass to the Wehrmacht hospital laundry, and stood with her hands at her sides in the light of the torch, and the torch went down her and stopped at the front of her coat, at the two round marks and the smear, and stayed there.

"What is this?"

"From the laundry," Helena said. "It is the German linen. We are supposed to sort the linen of the surgical ward before the wash, and I am on the surgical linen this week, and if the gendarme wishes he may telephone Frau Piltz at the laundry on Wiejska Street and she will be delighted to tell him what her people look like at the end of a Friday."

The gendarme looked at the blood, and at her, and at the card, on which the stamp of the Wehrmacht hospital laundry was very large and very clean; and then he did what every official in Warsaw did when the possibility of a telephone call to another German official opened in front of him, which was to hand back the papers and step out of the way, and Helena walked out of the cordon on Świętokrzyska Street into the dark October city with a German soldier's blood drying on her coat in two round handprints and a smear.

She walked home through the tramless dark. And then, because it was after curfew and the streets were hers, she did what no careful person does: she did not go home at once; she sat for twenty minutes on the steps of the church on Świętokrzyska Street with her arms round her knees, and smelled the blood in the cold, and thought about a boy of nineteen from Gelsenkirchen calling for his mother in a coal cellar, and about a boy of seventeen from Warsaw hanging at dawn in Marszałkowska Street, and about what a person was supposed to feel in the autumn of 1943, and discovered, with a kind of horror at herself, that she felt nothing at all except the determination to be there again on Monday.

She did not wash the coat for a week.

Not from sentiment: from a reason that she wrote in the notebook she had begun to keep, in a hand so small and so flat that it could not be read across a room, in the ledger of what a woman in Warsaw could still have: *I have the coat cleaned on Sunday. If it is cleaned, on Monday I could not have walked through the cordon and the boy would be in the yard and the woman with grey hair would be at Szucha. Therefore the coat is not a coat; the coat is a tool; and a tool must be kept dirty, and I must learn not to hate the sight of it.*

And on Sunday, when she did wash the coat, in the copper in the courtyard with the soap she had been saving, she found that the blood had gone through the lining on the left breast, where her hand always went, and that the envelope sewn into the lining had a thin brown line across its lower corner, where the blood had come through the cloth, exactly across the lower right corner of the photograph, so that the two young people in it stood in an autumn garden with a red-brown stain spreading up from the edge of the frame towards their feet.

She sat with it in her hands for a long time in the empty courtyard, and then she stitched it up again with the same white thread, and sewed the seam shut along the old line, so that from the outside the coat looked exactly as it had looked before, which is the whole art of Warsaw in the fifth year of the war: not to hide the blood, which is impossible, but to sew the seam along the same line, so that the coat can be worn again on Monday.
