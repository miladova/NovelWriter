# Chapter Twelve: The Girl in the Laundry

*Praga — January 1944*

The barrack was brick, not wood, because before the war it had been the Ostbahn's stores office, and it had a stove in the middle and twenty-two bunks and a window at each end, and if you kept your place in the bunk next to the stove you were warm all night, and if you did not you were cold all night, and Marysia Wojtera had the bunk next to the stove because she had been given it in September for a reason that she had not understood at the time and understood perfectly by January.

The reason was that she could count.

The work was the trays of small brass fittings: the pieces came into the shed in wooden boxes, stamped, still sharp at the edges, and each box had a ticket with a number on it, and in the trays each fitting had its own little compartment, and the German foreman, Herr Bader, a man of fifty in a brown coat who had lost the top joint of his ring finger in a press in Essen in 1936, would come down the row twice a day with a wooden tally and ask how many trays, and the girls who could not answer in German correctly would be moved to the boxes with the heavy pieces, and the girls who could answer would stay at the fine work, and the fine work was in a warm corner of the shed.

Marysia could say, in German, without hesitating and without looking at her hands: *Einundzwanzig volle Trays, Herr Bader.* One-and-twenty full trays. And *vier Stück fehlen* — four pieces missing — when four pieces were missing, which made Herr Bader look at her twice the first time, and after that he only grunted, which was, in the mathematics of the Ostbahn depot in Praga, a promotion.

There were twenty-two children in the barrack in January 1944. The youngest was five and could not lift the trays; the oldest was fourteen and was called Irena and had a cough that would not go, and she slept in the bunk on the far side of the stove and did the best work in the shed because her hands were quick and her eyes were good. There were seven girls from the camp at Skaryszewska and eleven from the ruins of the little town at Zamość and four who had been in the cellar of the labour office at Wilcza Street for a month and had been brought to the depot in December with no explanation; and there were two boys of eleven who worked the hand-cart and one of them, whose name was Józek, had arrived in December with a bandage round his head and had not spoken for eleven days, and Marysia had given him half her bread on the third night and had not given him any since, because a person who is given something every day stops being able to get it for himself, and because Pani Zofia in the courtyard at Hoża 14 had said that once, to a beggar, out loud, and it had stayed with her.

At six in the morning the bell; at half past six the soup, which was turnip and millet and sometimes a piece of horse; at seven the roll in the yard by the watch shed, standing in two rows with your hands at your sides, counted by the Ukrainian guard, whose name was Didko and who was from a village near Ternopil and who called the children *the little ones* and who had once given Marysia a sugar lump for finding his glove under a tray in the shed; from half past seven until twelve and from one until six, the shed; and at six, the soup again, and the bread of two hundred and fifty grammes, which was more than the ration in the city, because the Ostbahn was an armaments enterprise and its labour reserve was fed at a rate that had been decided by a German official in Berlin on a printed sheet, and which had, in the whole of Poland in the winter of 1944, this one consequence: that a child could look at a German soldier in the street and know, with absolute precision, that she ate better than he did.

But what the child could not know on the printed sheet, and what she learned in the barrack, was the rest of the arithmetic.

The rest of the arithmetic was that on the seventh of December a girl called Bronka, from the second bunk on the right, who was nine and had a grandmother in Wola, had put her hand into the press to push back a tray that had gone crooked, and the press had gone down and taken off the ends of three fingers; and the German foreman had sent for the doctor's assistant, who had put a dressing and a paper figure round her hand and had said she would heal; and in January Bronka was still there, at the end of the shed, sorting the big washers with one hand, because a girl who is injured in an armaments enterprise does not go home, because there is no home, and because the paper figure round her hand is a form that keeps the enterprise's record clean.

The rest of the arithmetic was that two of the children had disappeared in November, in the middle of the night, without any noise, and Herr Bader had not looked up from his trays in the morning; and that when Marysia had once asked Didko where they had gone, Didko had said, in his Ukrainian, without any malice, "To the Reich, to work on a farm, it's better there, they're fed there"; and that Irena, who was fourteen, who knew everything, had said afterwards, "They're not on farms. They were on the list for the homes. The SS came for them."

The rest of the arithmetic was the letters.

On Sunday afternoons, in the hour after the soup, you could write a letter, one page, and it went into the box outside the depot office and it went, if the guard took it, to wherever you addressed it, and there was a rule in fine print that letters from the labour reserve must be in German; and there was no rule at all about what a child could do with the two stub pencils that came in once a month in the box from the Arbeitsamt with the same quantity of paper. So Marysia wrote, every Sunday, in the small, careful, upright letters she had learned from her mother, and the letter was in German, and it said:

*Für die Mutter. Ich bin gesund. Ich arbeite bei der Ostbahn. Es ist warm im Saal. Bitte schicken Sie mir den Bleistift und den Faden. Deine Tochter, Maria.*

For my mother. I am well. I work at the Ostbahn. It is warm in the hall. Please send me a pencil and thread. Your daughter, Maria.

It was a good letter, and it was the third version of the letter. The first version had been in Polish, and it had been taken by the guard and not sent, and Marysia had learned from that. The second version had said, at the end, *Ich vermisse dich sehr* — I miss you very much — and it had been returned to her two weeks later, unsent, with a mark in pencil by a German clerk, and the mark was a small line labelled *unnecessary*, so she had learned that too.

The third version, the one that went every Sunday, was the letter of a girl who was well and warm and had no feelings at all: and it was sent, and it reached her mother wherever her mother was, whether it be the laundry on Wiejska Street or the cell in Serbia at the prison on Dzielna Street, because the German post, in the middle of the greatest catastrophe in the history of the world, was, god help them all, efficient; and every third or fourth Sunday an envelope came back to the depot office for her, and the envelopes were also in German, and the safe ones said the same six things:

*Meine Tochter. Ich bin gesund. Arbeite gut und sei ruhig. Iss alles. Ich bin immer bei dir. Deine Mutter.*

My daughter. I am well. Work well and be quiet. Eat everything. I am always with you. Your mother.

They were all the same, those letters; and they were all in her mother's hand, and the handwriting was not well, and once the paper smelled of lye and once, in December, it had a brown marker on the corner and the word *Krankenhaus* at the top, which Marysia did not understand until Irena explained that it meant that the letter had been written in a hospital; and Marysia did not cry, because a person who cries in the barrack is a person who gets looked at, and instead she folded the three safe letters and the two with the hospital marks on them into the lining of her jacket, on the left side, over her heart, which was where her mother had told her, in May, in the yard at Skaryszewska, that a thing of your own should always be carried.

And there was Sunday, and there was the shed, and there was the cold, and there was the counting, and there was, in the middle of January 1944, the day of the two visitors, which is the day that must be described properly, because it is the axle on which the whole of this part of the book turns.

*The yard, and the man who knew her father*

The depot yard was sixty metres wide and three hundred long, and it was, in the winter of 1944, the busiest place in Warsaw that no Pole talked about: the Ostbahn's works at Praga repaired the rolling stock of the German armies in the east, and the yard had eight kilometres of sidings and four long sheds and a coaling stage and a turntable, and at any hour of the day or night there were, in that yard, between two and six hundred Polish workmen and forty Germans and two hundred wagons, and the wagons went east empty and came back full and went east full and came back empty, and everybody in the yard knew, by the beginning of 1944, exactly what a railway was.

It was in the yard, on a Sunday in January, that Marysia met Wiktor Sadowski, in a manner that was not an accident.

Because on Sundays, between the soup and the letters, the children of the reserve were allowed — in the way that children in a labour reserve were allowed anything, which is to say not permitted, but not prevented — to walk along the fence at the eastern end of the yard, where the coal was, and to watch the trains.

She had gone there with Irena and Józek on the second Sunday of January, and the three of them had stood at the fence with their collars up, looking at a train come in: thirty wagons of coal from the mines at Zagłębie, with a Polish brakeman in a padded jacket standing on the last wagon's step with the cold making his nose run, and a German railway guard in the brake van with the stove going and the window slid open; and Irena had said, into the cold, without any expression at all: "That's how they'll take me."

"Take you where?"

"Łódź. Or further. On a train like that; except not with coal. My cousin was taken in November from the camp at Skaryszewska, twenty-nine of them, with two guards and a paper for each child, and they went in a wagon with the doors wired and the addresses chalked above the doors, and the chalk said *Konstantynów* and the paper, everybody knows, said *Arbeitserziehungslager*, and you know what happened to my cousin?" Irena's eyes were on the train. "Nothing has happened to my cousin. There is no letter and there is no grave and there is no list, and that is what happens: nothing. That is the thing they do, they make it be nothing."

"Then how do you know she went to Łódź?"

"Because Didko told me, because Didko is a Ukrainian from a village with a mother in it, and he has a nose for the ones who are going to be sent, and when the wagon came in, he told me to go and stand at the fence, and he came and stood next to me, and he watched it go, and he said, in his Polish, 'Look at it, girl, because it is better to look at a thing you cannot stop than not to look at it, and because your cousin asked me — she asked me at the ramp, in November, with her hands tied, she asked me to make you look at the train.'" Irena blew on her fingers. "That's Didko. He is twelve years older than me and he is a guard, and he gives sugar lumps to children, and he stood next to me by this fence and we watched her wagon go out of the yard, and then he went back to the shed and I stood here for another hour."

Marysia stood at the fence and looked at the coal train, and thought about a thing that had happened, once, in the shed, when the water pipe had frozen and a small boy had come in with a bucket and had put the bucket down and said, in the Polish of the country, to nobody, "*They went east, the ones in the third wagon*," and then had picked up his bucket and gone out.

The whistle went. The train pulled away east along the river flats, and the three children stood and watched the wagons go until the brake van's stove was a red dot in the grey, and then a voice behind them said, in Polish:

"That is the Warsaw–Mińsk line, and it does not go to Łódź."

He was a man of about fifty-five in a padded workman's jacket with a leather cap, and he was standing at the fence six metres away with a cigarette in his hand, not looking at them at all, looking at the departing train, and he had come up without any of them hearing him, which was a thing that men did in the Warsaw of 1944, and which children learned to notice.

"The train to Łódź goes north out of the yard on the roundabout line," he said, "and it goes out in the middle of the night and it comes back empty, and it is one of the things in this yard that nobody talks about, and children who go and look at it and write down the numbers of the wagons are children who lose a finger and get moved to the packing shed. My name is Wiktor. I am a fitter on number three shed, and I had the honour of knowing your father."

Marysia looked at him, and her heart did a thing that she had taught it, over the last year, not to do.

"My father is dead," she said. "Adam Wójcik. He was a railwayman, and he died before I was born."

"Yes," the man said. "So I am told." He dropped the cigarette and stood on it, and looked at her, for the first time, and his face was a workman's face, grey and seamed and careful, the face of a man who has spent forty years looking at metal and knows exactly what will break and where. "The one I knew was Stanisław Wojtera, an engineer on the Wilno line, who had a wife from Piaseczno and one daughter who played the violin, and who died in September 1939 helping to dig the anti-tank ditch at Okęcie, and who was the best hand with a worn axle that ever came out of the workshops at Wilno; and before the war, in the yard at Wilno, in 1931, he showed me the photograph of his daughter in a green dress with a violin, in a garden, with a laurel tree." He straightened up. "I have been at this depot four years and I have heard the name Wojtera in the reserve detail every month on the paper, and I am not a brave man and I am not a fool, and I am not going to speak to you again; I am going to speak to the coal yard. But I want you to know that you are not nobody. The man whose name you carry was a good engineer, and the woman who taught you to count was a girl in a green dress in a garden, and if the day comes when the shed's counting papers can do a thing for you, then papers are counted by men, and men can be counted too. Good afternoon, girls. Mind the coal."

He walked away along the fence and did not look back, and Irena said, under her breath, in the voice of a person who has just seen a small and genuine miracle:

"What was that?"

"That," Marysia said, "was a man who knew my grandfather," and stood looking after him for a long time, with her hands in her sleeves, at a fence at the end of a railway yard in Prague in the winter of 1944, with the whole of her life in front of her and six people in the world who cared whether she lived, one of them a German lady with pale eyelashes, one of them a guard from Ternopil who gave children sugar, one of them a fitter with a leather cap, one of them in a cell in Serbia, one of them a boy who had not spoken for eleven days, and one of them a Major in grey who had taken a pencil from behind her ear and put it back.

The first visitor was the German lady from the office on Nowy Świat.

She came every second Tuesday, in the afternoon, by tram from the city, with a leather case and a list, and she was young, perhaps twenty-eight, with pale eyelashes and a long neck and a grey coat with the collar up; and she came to the depot office to compare the labour returns of the armaments enterprise with the schedules of the commandant's office, and she did it in the depot office with Herr Bader standing at her shoulder being agreeable and the depot's clerk hovering; and every visit, at the end of it, she needed a girl to carry the certified copies of the returns from the office to the depot's gate, because she was not permitted, as a woman, to walk through the depot's yard alone, and so the yard guard's instructions said, and so she did.

She chose, every visit, the same girl.

And in the fifty metres between the office door and the depot gate, walking at a measured pace in the slush with a bundle of certified copies in her arms and the German lady beside her, the girl was given, in the voice of an official discussing a discrepancy in a return, the news of the world.

*Your mother is alive. She is in the prison on Dzielna Street. Do not ask anybody about her. Do not send letters to the prison; the letters are read, and a letter from you to her is a mark on the file.*

*The rest of the children from the camp at Skaryszewska went to a factory at Łódź in November. Twenty-nine of them. Do not ask about them either.*

*Eat the bread. The bread in the depot is counted; the bread in the parcel is not. There is a parcel at the gate on Sundays, for the girl Wojtera, with thread and pencils and a piece of soap; that is not from your mother and you are not to say who it is from, and if you say it, it stops.*

*You can read German, cannot you. Yes. Then read the tickets in the shed and never say you can. A person who can read the tickets is a person who is useful; a person who is useful goes to the Reich. A person who cannot read, and cannot be trusted with any paper, stays where she is and cleans the trays. Which would you rather be?*

*Good. Then that is the whole of your education, and it is not much, but it is more than Goebbels gives to the children of this city.*

On the last visit, at the gate, in the middle of January, with the sleet coming down and the yard guard watching from his box, the pale girl in the grey coat had said, in the same flat voice, with her eyes on the gate:

"On Friday the Referat makes its inspection of the depot. There will be an officer. He will be the Major. You will be presented to him as the best of the reserve detail, because Herr Bader has to show that his detail can count and read, and he will choose you because you can say 'einundzwanzig Trays' and the other girls cannot." She had taken the copies back into her arms and turned away, and then, over her shoulder, in the last two seconds before the gate, she had said the only sentence in eleven months that had anything in it at all: "Girl. Whatever you do on Friday, do not be afraid of him. He is the only man in this whole apparatus who will not eat you. Do not tell anybody I said that either."

That was Tuesday. On Friday the yard was swept, which in the Ostbahn depot in January meant that the snow was pushed into two long ridges along the fence, and the shed floor was swept and the trays were levelled and the children were washed in the washroom with cold water and the ends of their fingers were blue, and at eleven o'clock in the morning the cars came in through the depot gate.

There were three men with the Major. There was Herr Bader, and there was a fat oberzahlmeister in a leather coat with a laugh like a door, and there was a young lieutenant with a face like a choirboy who looked at the shed the way a person looks at a place where he has been told he will find a thing that will disappoint him; and there was the Major, who was tall, and grey, and whose cap left a red line across his forehead because he had been riding in the car with the heater on, and who walked into the long shed with the brass trays in the racks and stopped at the end of the aisle and looked down the whole length of the room, the way a man looks at a thing he has built and is going to have to break.

Herr Bader presented the detail: twenty-two children standing at their trays with their hands at their sides.

The Major went down the row. He stopped at Irena, whose cough was in her chest, and asked her how long she had been there, and looked at the white of her eye, and said nothing; he stopped at Józek, whose head was bandaged no longer, and asked him his name and where he came from, and Józek said nothing at all, and the young lieutenant laughed, and the Major did not; and then he came to the fourth tray on the left, and stopped, and looked down at a girl of ten with a rag tied over her hair and a stub pencil behind her ear.

"Your name."

"Maria Anna Wojtera."

"Your work."

"Feinsortierung, Herr Major. Ich zähle die Trays. Einundzwanzig volle Trays heute."

The Major looked at the tray; and then he did something that made the fat Oberzahlmeister raise an eyebrow, which was that he took off his glove, and picked one of the brass fittings out of the tray with two fingers, and turned it over, and looked at it, and put it back in its compartment exactly as it had been, and said, in Polish, quietly, so that only the girl, and perhaps the young lieutenant, could hear: "Has anybody taught you to sort like that?"

"My mother taught me to count," the girl said, in Polish, and then, remembering, added, in German: "Herr Major."

And the Major said, in German, in the voice of a man dictating a report: "It is good work. It is work which the enterprise requires to be maintained, and the persons of this detail are to be maintained in it, and the returns are to continue to my office." And then, still in German, still in the voice of the report, with no change in his face at all, he said: "Also there is a thing which has interested me. The child of the reserve has a musical name. Is there music in this shed?"

The room went quiet. Behind them, Herr Bader's mouth opened and shut.

"There is counting, Herr Major," Marysia said. "There is no music."

"Then there is a loss," said the Major, "since every German enterprise requires counting and no enterprise is improved by it. Stand straight." And he reached out and did the most ordinary thing in the world: he took the stub pencil from behind her ear and looked at it, and turned it over — it had been sharpened at both ends with a knife, and it was no longer than a joint of a finger — and he put it back behind her ear, and his knuckles brushed her hair, which was the first time a grown man had touched her head since the guard cut her plait off in the bath house in May.

And then, because she was ten years old and because she was tired and because she had been holding the whole of herself together for eight months, and because the hand smelled of soap and cold wool and boot leather from the car, and because a long time ago, in a room on the second floor at Hoża 14 with the piano tuned flat, a man used to lift her onto the stool by both hands under her arms, she said the word.

She said, "Papa."

In the long shed at the Ostbahn depot in Praga, in the middle of the inspection of the labour reserve of the Wehrmacht commandant's office in Warsaw, in January 1944, a child of ten looked up at a Major in a grey tunic and said, quietly, in Polish, in the ordinary voice of a person making a report:

"*Papa*, the tray is empty, I have counted them all."

Nobody heard it. That was the mercy, and it lasted for about two seconds: Bader was three metres away and had the hearing of a man who had been a foreman in Essen, and did not know a word of Polish; the young lieutenant heard the sound of it and understood nothing and would remember it in March and say so, in a room with a desk lamp and a stenographer, in a different building; and the fat Oberzahlmeister heard nothing at all, because he was thinking about the customs clearance on a case of Polish vodka that was, at that moment, in the boot of the car.

But the Major heard it. The Major heard it the way a man hears a door close somewhere in a house he believed was empty; and his face did not move at all, and his hands did not move at all, and he stood in front of the tray of brass fittings and looked down at the girl for the length of three breaths, with the whole of his life going past him like the Ostbahn's wagons in the yard outside; and then he said, in German, in a clear, level, carrying voice, so that everybody in the shed could hear him:

"The detail is satisfactory. The persons are to be kept at work and kept in health. The Major will note it in the file."

And he turned and walked down the length of the shed and out into the snow of the yard, where the three cars stood steaming, and the whole shed let out its breath; and Herr Bader said, "Weiter, weiter," and the trays began to move again; and the girl did not cry, because a person who cries in the shed is a person who gets looked at.

She stood at the tray with the pencil behind her ear and the brass fittings going into their compartments in their long, even rows, and she thought about the word.

She was ten years old. She had been told, all her life, in a voice that was calm and level and final, that her father was Adam Wójcik, a railwayman, dead before she was born, and she had said it to the doctor from Posen and to the clerks and to the guards because her mother had told her to; and she was not so simple as to believe a thing only because she had been told it, because in this war she had watched the whole city be told things; and she had, in the last year, learned three facts that had no other explanation, and they were these.

One: that when she was taken to the room with the shutters, nobody hurt her, and a German doctor with soft hands gave her chocolate and told her she sang on pitch.

Two: that when the children of detail three were taken from Skaryszewska in November, she was not taken; and the list had had nineteen names on it and she was not on the list, and she had seen the list, because it was on the wall of the shed for two days with the German heading and she could read it.

Three: that a German Major had walked out of his way, in the snow, in the middle of an inspection, to take the pencil from behind her ear and put it back.

She fitted a brass fitting into a compartment, and then another, and then another, and she made, in the middle of the ninety-fifth tray of her life, the decision that a child makes in a war, which is to hold the two things at once, because holding both is the only way to stay alive: she would go on saying the name of the dead railwayman of Grodno, every time, exactly as her mother had taught her, because to say anything else was to break the one thing her mother had ever asked of her; and she would also, from that day, be watching the Major; and she would not tell her mother, because her mother was in the prison on Dzielna Street and there was nothing to tell, except that a man in a grey tunic had touched her head in the snow, and that she had said a word to him that she had never said out loud to any living person on earth, and that nobody, in the whole German depot at Praga, had punished her for it; and that she had liked it, and was ashamed, and was glad.

She worked until six. She ate the soup and the bread, and put the two hundred grammes of bread away in her bunk against the morning, and lay down in the dark next to the stove with her mother's letters inside her jacket over her heart; and in the morning she was at the tray again before the bell, because that is what children in the labour reserve of the Wehrmacht at Warsaw did in January 1944, and while she worked she did a thing that she had told no one about, not her mother and not Irena, and it was this: in the corner of her bunk, where the boards met the wall and the mortar had fallen out and nobody could see, she had begun to keep a list, written on the good paper from the office that she was given to wrap the trays in, folded to the size of a stamp and hidden in the mortar.

On the list were the names of the children who had gone.

*Bronka — 3 fingers — January*
*Fela — Skaryszewska — took away with the camp list — August — I gave her the chocolate*
*Irena's sister — Łódź — November*
*Józek's brother — ?*
*The two boys from Zamość — night — no names*

and then, at the bottom of the sheet, in the afternoons when she was alone, in letters as small as she could make them, she wrote the names of the people who were still there, so that when they disappeared there would be somebody to know it:

*Irena. Józek. Herr Bader's tally. Didko's sugar. The girl Kallman's cough. Me. Maria Anna Wojtera, daughter of Adam Wójcik, of Grodno, deceased 1933, and of Helena Wojtera of Hoża 14 in Warsaw, who is alive.*
