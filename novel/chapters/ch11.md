# Chapter Eleven: The Tram at Szucha

*Warsaw — Thursday, 6 January 1944*

They came at five in the morning, which was the hour of the occupation, and they came quietly, which was worse, because a loud entry is a raid and a quiet entry is a name.

Helena woke because the gate in the courtyard had not squeaked, and the gate always squeaked, and she lay in the dark for one second with her heart going like a bird in a box, and then she was up and at the window and looking down through the corner of the blackout paper, and there were two cars in the yard, and men in the yard, and one of them was holding a torch on Zofia Mrożek in her nightdress with the caretaker's keys still on their string round her neck.

She had four minutes, and she used them the way Kuba had taught her: the folded pages of the Biuletyn went from the violin case into the stove and were lit with one match and burned down to a black flake while she held the lid up with her foot; the pencil-written name of the man at the Flak school — one word, no surname — went the same way; the tin came out from under the floorboard and went under the mattress; the photograph stayed where it had been for eleven years, in the lining, over her heart.

Then she sat on the edge of the bed in her nightdress with her hands in her lap and waited.

The first man through the door was not German. He was Polish, in a good coat and a soft hat, thirty-five, with a face like a shopwalker, and behind him came two of the Sicherheitspolizei in plain clothes and behind them, at the door, a gendarme with a rifle; and the Polish one went through the room the way a man goes through a shop he has been in before: the cupboard, the shelf, the violin case, the case emptied on the bed with the instrument left on the blanket where it fell, the mattress, the floorboards under the rug, and the fourth floorboard lifted up, and the joist, and the hole, and the empty hole.

"Where is it?"

"Where is what? The board is loose, the house is sixty years old, everything in this street is loose."

The Polish one put his hand into the hole and felt it, all round, carefully, with two fingers, like a man feeling a hole in a tooth; and then he stood up and wiped his fingers on his handkerchief and looked at her, and he had, she saw, a small gold filling in his canine tooth, and she thought: *if I live, I will remember that tooth.*

"You know what I am, Pani Wojtera," he said, in Polish, quietly. "I am not one of them. I am a Pole from this city and I have a wife and a mother and a boy of eleven. I do what I do because the alternative is a camp in Germany and my boy with nobody to feed him. I am going to tell you one thing for nothing, because I do not want your death on me: the order for you came from Szucha, and the paper behind the order has your name on it in the hand of a man who sells, and the man who sells is a small man with his chin in his collar and he lives on the third floor at the Rialto, and he has been paid seven hundred zlotys for you. That is all I know. Dress warm. Take the coat with the good lining."

She dressed warm. She took the coat with the good lining.

They did not put the handcuffs on until the courtyard, in front of the windows, because that was the correct way to do it: the neighbours at their windows with the blackout paper pulled aside, and Pani Halina Rowińska of the third floor with her hand over her mouth, and Zofia standing at the gate in her nightdress with her keys in her hand, and the shopkeeper's wife from the second floor crossing herself in the doorway, and the boys from the fourth floor watching the car with their faces full of the terrible appetite of boys of fourteen, and Helena went past all of them with her head up and her eyes straight ahead, and got into the back of the car between two men in plain clothes, and said, in German, to the driver, as a thing that cost her nothing: "The violin on the bed is my father's, and it is worth a great deal of money. If it is still there when I come back, I will play at your wedding."

The car went down Hoża Street and turned left, and in the grey the front of the building on Aleja Szucha came up like the front of a bank, and it was, in the winter dark with the lamps still on in the arches and the sentry boxes, exactly what it had been before the war: the Ministry of Religious Affairs and Public Education, with the stone urns on the parapet, and a hundred and eleven steps along the entrance, and, in the basements, the cells.

They did not take her picture, and they did not take her name, at first. They took her along a corridor and down a flight and along another corridor into a cell four metres by two with a bench, a stove, a bucket, a window at the height of a man's shoulder with the glass painted grey, and the stink of the cell, which was the smell of a hundred years of Polish ministries, and they left her there for nine hours.

The cell was called a tram. She learned the name the next day, from the woman who was put in beside her: a woman of sixty with a face like a farm, who sat down on the bench with a great sigh and said, "Good afternoon. *Tramwaj*. Because we come in, and we wait, and we go out, and there is no timetable. And you can hear everything through that pipe." She pointed with her chin at the fat iron pipe that ran up through the corner of the cell, from the cellar to the roof. "Everything. If you are clever, you learn more from the pipe than from the Germans. What are you in for?"

"I don't know."

"They never tell you. That's the first thing they do not tell you; the first thing they do tell you is the name of your home town, to make you think they know everything." The woman settled herself, and pressed her hands together, and shut her eyes. "I am Krysia Zaremba. My husband has a workshop on Chmielna, radiators; my son went to the market on the twenty-second of December with a sack of flour and did not come back; I went to the police to ask about him and they kept me, because I asked twice. Now we are seven. There is a girl in the next cell who is a scout, and a doctor from Ochota who has not spoken for two days, and a woman who is nothing at all, from the country, who does not even know how she got here. Sleep when you can. They wake people at night; that is the trick of this building, the night, and the lamps."

At eleven o'clock at night she was taken up one floor and along the corridor to a room with a table, three chairs, a cupboard, and a picture of the Führer over the table, on the second floor, where the interrogations were done; and the man behind the table was reading a file and eating a piece of chocolate.

"Pani Wojtera. Sit down." Obersturmführer Werner Dörffel looked at her across the table with his long pale face and his unshadowed reasonable eyes. He was in his tunic this time, with the collar tabs and the iron cross with the ribbon, and his hair was combed wet, and he looked, in a way that no one who saw him in the street could have imagined, like a man who had come to work early and was pleased with the morning. "You have been here nine hours and nobody has touched you, and I want to say at the beginning: nobody is going to touch you, unless you make a wall for them to break, and you are too intelligent to make a wall. We are going to be reasonable, and I am going to tell you the truth, which is more than the File is going to do, and then we are going to drink some coffee, because my tea was confiscated by the Army Ordnance Office in November and I am reduced to coffee like a Frenchman. Do you take sugar?"

"No."

"Neither do I." He pushed the chocolate aside and opened the file, and went through it with one finger as if he were checking the entries in a ledger: "Helena Wojtera, born Warsaw 1910; conservatory in Munich, 1931 to 1933, quite a good student, and I say that as a man who was dragged to the piano as a boy for nine years and remembers nothing but the smell of the keys; out in 1933, no reason given; Warsaw since 1933, gave music lessons, employed since May 1943 at the Wehrmacht hospital laundry on Wiejska Street; unmarried; one child, Maria Anna, born 3 December 1933, in the labour reserve of the Wehrmacht commandant's office, presently quartered in the sorting room at the Ostbahn depot, and, I am obliged to tell you, that is a favour — a child of nine on the reserve of a Wehrmacht office, well fed, living in a heated barrack; there are four hundred thousand Polish children in the General Government who would call it paradise."

"You have my file," Helena said. "You do not need me."

"That is what everyone says, and it is never true, and I will show you why." Dörffel turned a page. "In June, a doctor of the Institute for Racial Hygiene examined you and made a note: *correspondence checked — none*. He meant, of course, that there is no correspondence between your ear and your daughter's, which is a fact about ears and nothing else; but a fact is a nail, and a good interrogator is a man with a hammer. Then in October the file of that same doctor received a letter from a Generalmajor a.D. in Brandenburg, dated, mine to delight in, 1933, signed by your late father, Stanisław Wojtera, engineer, of Warsaw, and directed to the father of the officer who is presently the Referent for Labour Deployment at the Wehrmacht commandant's office in this city: a letter in which your father wrote that his daughter had refused to marry a German, that his daughter's refusal was final, and that the Major's family would oblige him by not writing and by not sending money. It is in the folder at the Institute. It has been in the folder since 1933, because the Security Service in Munich kept a copy, and their copy went into the file, and the file came to Warsaw after the General Government was created. Do you know what that means, Pani Wojtera?"

"That my father was a fool," Helena said, "and that the Reich keeps paper like a nation of shopkeepers."

Dörffel laughed out loud, and it was a real laugh, short and dry, and it made the room worse.

"It means," he said, "that in this file the question of your daughter's father has an answer that I have not yet proved and do not need to prove to anybody but myself. But we are not here about the father. We are here about the Major." He closed the file and looked at her with his unshadowed eyes. "On the nineteenth of April 1943, the SS began to destroy the Jewish quarter of this city. On the twentieth of April, Major Friedrich von Brandt requisitioned two hundred metres of rope and sixty tarpaulins from the Wehrmacht stores in Warsaw for a work detachment at the Gęsiówka, which was routine and correct. On the twenty-third of April he transferred eleven Polish workers out of the labour office's transport for the Reich, by telephone, at four in the morning, which was not routine and was not correct, and the eleven men are alive and one of them is a cousin of a man in the Home Army, and I know this because I had a man in the labour office from the beginning of the year. On the twentieth of May he signed a disposition that took a nine-year-old girl off a transport list. On the fifth of June he began buying coffee in the name of a police fund at a transit camp, which means he is paying. In August he moved a child from Szucha's custody back into Wehrmacht reserve on a medical technicality, and there is not a person in this building, or in the Arbeitsamt, or in the Ostbahn who has ever seen a German officer do such a thing, and there are people in this building who are paid to notice such things." He folded his hands. "The Major is not a criminal in the ordinary sense. He is a man of the old Prussian kind who has walked into this war slowly, one paper at a time, and every paper has been smaller than the last excuse; and at the end of that path there is a wall, and the name on the wall will not be yours, Pani Wojtera; it will be his; and there is nothing on earth that will save either of you except the truth, told early, to me."

"So it is true," Helena said. "You do not want me."

"I do not want you at all. Look at you." He said it without contempt, as a fact of inventory. "You are a music teacher from Hoża Street with a violin and a forged aunt in the country. You are worth four weeks of my department's time and then a transport to Ravensbrück, and the cost of transporting you is more than you are worth, and I am a man with a budget. The Major is worth a great deal." Dörffel leaned back. "Here is the arrangement, and you will not like it and you will consider it and you will take it, and I will tell you exactly why you will take it. You will sign a statement, in your own hand, of the following: that Friedrich von Brandt, Major, of the Wehrmacht commandant's office in Warsaw, is the father of your daughter; that you have concealed this since 1933; and that you have been rewarded for the concealment by the removal of your daughter from transport lists and by sums of money. You will name two occasions on which he gave you money. You will name the officer in whose presence he removed your daughter from the list — there is a young lieutenant in his office, a good Catholic boy from Pforzheim, and I want his name in the statement, because a second witness in a case of this kind is the end of a career and a life. And in return: your daughter is entered in the file of the Zentrale für Kinderfürsorge, that is, she leaves the labour reserve and goes to the home on Krakowskie Przedmieście, and she is fed, and she is not transported; and you are held here under investigation, which is a great deal better than being transported; and in the spring, when the case has been disposed of, you may be exchanged for a German civilian from the internment camp at Lublin — I have four such exchanges pending, they are done all the time."

Helena sat with her hands in her lap and looked at him.

"You are asking me," she said slowly, "to write a page that will hang a man."

"I am asking you to write a page that will hang a traitor," Dörffel said. "There is no difference in the paper and a great difference in the accounting."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you go to Pawiak, and your case is closed, and the child goes back to Skaryszewska; and the SS does not care about ears and blood as much as the Institute does, and in the spring they will use her on the ramps at the Ostbahn, because there is a labour shortage, and a child of nine on the ramps is a child of nine who will not see ten." He looked at her without malice. "Do not decide tonight. I am not a man who wants the answer from the mouth of a person who has been awake for twenty-one hours; it is a bad foot to start on. Go back to the tram and sleep, and I shall send you coffee with sugar, and in three days we shall talk about the statement in my office with a stenographer and a Pole from the prosecutor's office; and the coffee is not a bribe. The coffee is what we give to persons of quality, because there are so few of them here."

She was in the tram for three days.

The pipe in the corner of the cell taught her the building in the way Krysia Zaremba had promised. She learned the times of the interrogations by the sound of the boots in the corridor above; she learned to know the difference between the sound of a man being beaten and the sound of a man being talked to; she learned the two sentences that came through the pipe more often than any other in Aleja Szucha, and they were *I don't know* and *I have told you everything*, and she learned that the second was almost always a lie and the first was almost always the truth.

On the second day they brought the girl from the scouts into her cell for half a day, because the cell next door was needed, and the girl was twenty years old and had been beaten across the soles of the feet, and she lay on the bench and told Helena, in a whisper, without any self-pity at all, all the details of the boy she had been in love with, whose name was Tadeusz, and who had been shot in the forest in the autumn; and in the evening, when they took her away, she said, "If you get out, tell my mother I said nothing — tell her I said nothing, on my honour," and Helena said, "I will tell her," and the girl said, "She lives at Grójecka 107, third courtyard, the door with the bicycle," and Helena said, "I know," and the girl went away and did not come back, and Helena, alone with the pipe, learned the next morning that the girl's name was on the list of the people who had been shot at the wall in the ruins of the ghetto the night before, and that the list was read out in the camp office, and that the reading of the list was how the building told itself what it had done.

On the third day the unmarked van took her across the city to Pawiak.

It went slowly, because of the snow, and she sat on the bench inside with the small high window and saw the city through it in strips: the snow on the tram wires, the closed shops, the people with their collars up, the boy with the sledge, the long queue at the Kurier Warszawski stand, the queue for the release of the *Volksdeutsche* at the corner of Królewska, the two men in the doorway of the Rialto with their hands in their pockets and their hats down — and at the corner of the Rialto she saw, through the small window of the van, the man with his chin in his collar, standing in a doorway with a newspaper, in the snow, in his good coat, looking at the van going by; and he saw her, and he knew her, and he did not move; and Helena Wojtera looked at him through the window of the van and did the only thing that a woman in her position could do, and it was not to scream and not to weep: she smiled at him, and lifted two fingers off the bench in a little salute, and held his eyes with hers until the van turned the corner into Dzielna Street.

Pawiak was on Dzielna Street and had been the biggest prison in Poland since 1835 and it was, when the van went in through the gate between the two kennels of the Gestapo dogs, the biggest prison in Europe by number of prisoners per man; and it was in the hands of the Security Police since 1939, and the cells were as they had been built, and the rules were not as they had been built, and the women's wing was called Serbia, because a Pole will give a name to everything, and the name had been invented in 1939 by a woman who had been in the Serbian campaign, and it had stuck.

They registered her at the desk: name, age, address, sentence — *in Untersuchung*, under investigation, which is the category that has no term and no end — and they took her watch and her money and her wedding ring, which she did not have, and her mother's ring, which they did not find because it was in the hem of the coat, and they put her in a cell in Serbia with six other women, and there was a plank bed, a blanket, a window with bars and no glass and boards over it, and a wheel with the number of the cell, and one of the women had a baby of four months.

*Serbia — the first week*

The women's wing at Pawiak was on the left of the yard, above the workshops, and it had been built for four hundred and in January 1944 it held eleven hundred, and the arithmetic of eleven hundred women in four hundred places had been solved by the Germans in the standard way, which was to make each cell hold what it would hold and to forbid the guards to look in.

Cell 165 in Serbia had six bunks and eleven women.

They were, in the order in which Helena learned them: Krysia Zaremba, who had come across from Szucha in the same van and who had become, in three days, the person who knew everything; a teacher of French from the school on Młynarska, who was fifty and had been in Pawiak since October and who had made a calendar out of a strip of wallpaper and a piece of charcoal; a woman of about thirty who did not give her name and who had a baby of four months and had been arrested at the gate of the ghetto in 1942 while carrying a package, and who was called, by everyone, *the mother of the small one*, because it was not safe to use names and because the small one was the most important person in the cell; two sisters from the country who had been caught hiding a man from the forest; a girl of twenty who had been a sales assistant on Nowy Świat and who cried every night for eleven nights and after that stopped crying and was, from the twelfth night, the hardest person in the room; and four women whose business nobody had established, because in Pawiak the rule was that a woman told you her village and her crime and never her name, and the crime was usually the same crime, which was food, or paper, or the fact of having a son.

At seven in the morning the cell door opened and the bucket was emptied and the bread came; at noon there was a soup of swede and water, sometimes with a piece of horse; at four there was the second roll call; at six, in winter, the lights went out and the cell went on existing in the dark, and the dark was when the women talked, because the dark was the only time in the whole of the prison when they were not being counted.

Helena learned the rules in three days.

Rule one: a name is a coin that you spend once. Rule two: the wall of the washroom in the basement is the newspaper of Serbia, and the newspaper is written in scratched letters and there are, on any given morning, between forty and ninety names on it, and every woman who goes down with her can reads the wall, and the ones whose men are on the wall learn it there, and afterwards they stand for a moment with their hand flat against the plaster, which is the only funeral in Warsaw. Rule three: the Patronat.

She learned about the Patronat on the second day, when the door of the cell opened, and a woman of about fifty in a grey skirt and a knitted jacket came in with a canvas bag and looked round the cell the way a woman looks round a kitchen she has come to work in.

"New one," the woman said. "Warsaw? Music teacher?"

"Yes."

"Zaremba's told me. I am Danuta Wierzbicka; I am the Patronat, which means I am nothing and I am the only thing in this prison that is not German. This"—she took out of the bag, one by one, and put into Helena's hands—"is a spoon, because you do not own a spoon, and it is a crime to have no spoon; this is soap, half a piece, which you keep in your shoe, and if you wash with it in front of the others you will not have it in the morning; this is a pencil two centimetres long, which is worth more in this building than the soap; and this is a piece of paper, which is the real gift, because in Pawiak paper is the currency. Write to your people. Address it to the Patronat office, Marszałkowska; the Patronat is permitted, under the German rules, to receive and forward letters for prisoners, and it is permitted because it is useful to the Germans, and it is useful to us." She sat down on the end of the bunk. "Now. Two things. The first: what is your case?"

"Under investigation. Szucha. The Obersturmführer, Department Four."

"Then you will be taken to Szucha by the van twice a week, or you will not be taken at all, which is worse; and you will be fed here at the rate of the prison and after three months you will not be able to stand up in the morning. So: the second thing. Have you people outside?"

"I have a child."

Danuta Wierzbicka looked at her for a long moment, and Helena saw, very clearly, the thing that made this woman dangerous and useful in equal measure: she had heard the sentence a thousand times, in a thousand forms, and she had learned to work with what a sentence contained rather than with what it felt like.

"Where?"

"Ostbahn depot. Praga. The labour reserve of the Wehrmacht commandant's office." Helena said it flatly, because in cell 165 in January 1944 there was no advantage in a lie, only in a price. "There is a parcel that comes to the depot gate on Sundays — thread, pencils, soap. It comes from a German office clerk. If the Patronat can get a letter to the depot office, into the box for the labour reserve, addressed to the child Wojtera, and if it can come back, then name your price to me and I will pay it in labour, not in money: I can read and write German and I have been a courier on the Aryan side, and I am not going to be here forever."

"You are a remarkable person to meet on your second day," said the woman from the Patronat. "Very well. The price is this: you will help with the small one at night, and you will teach the girl from Nowy Świat to read, because she cannot, and if I am ever taken away you will go on doing it, and in return you will have your letter, one page, once a month, in German, and the reply will come through the same hand. And I will tell you the truth about your case for nothing, because you have the face of a woman who is going to find it out anyway, and because nobody told me mine." She stood up. "An investigation under Szucha that does not end in six weeks does not end at all. It does not go to trial. It goes on, and you are moved to a camp when the transport list wants a hundred bodies, and the only thing you have in your hand is the fact that somebody in the Security Police wants something from you and cannot have it yet. Make that last. Every day you are alive is a day that the man who wants the thing from you wants it a little more. And child: the ones who last are not the strong ones. They are the ones who have decided the date to live till. Fix a date. Mine was my daughter's wedding, and my daughter was married in 1941, and I have never once been able to think of a new date, and I have been in this prison for four years, so do not ask me how it works."

She lay on the plank in the dark on the night of the tenth of January 1944, and listened to the cage in the corridor and the changing of the guard, and looked at the ceiling, and thought about the two doors of the building on Aleja Szucha and the man who had offered her coffee and the girl from the scouts and the man with his chin in his collar in the doorway of the Rialto; and she came, at some time in the small hours, to the thing that she had been feeling her way towards for three days in the tram like a woman feeling along a wall in a dark room, and the thing was this:

He had not once asked her about the underground. He had not asked her about the leaflets or the parcels or the boys or the routes or the desk on Wilcza Street, and he had a file on her that any policeman in the General Government would have paid a month's salary for. He had not asked, because he did not want it, and he did not want it because the underground was not his business; and the only thing in the whole file that was his business was a man in a grey tunic in a room on Nowy Świat with a signature and a coffee fund and a nine-year-old girl in a labour reserve; and the reason that Dörffel had not hurt her in three days, the reason that the SS in the corridor had brought her coffee with sugar and a cushion for the bench, the reason that a woman with a Polish name and a forged aunt in the country was still alive in Warsaw in January 1944 was that she was not a prisoner and not a suspect and not a Pole.

She was a door.

And a door is not tortured and a door is not killed and a door is not sent to Ravensbrück: a door is oiled, and tried, and left standing in a house that a man is going to walk into on a night when he has no lamp; and so long as she stayed a door, and did not become a wall, she would live; and the man on the other side of her — the one with the grey eyes and the terrible careful hands and the coffee bought in the name of a police fund, the one who had stood in a courtyard in the rain in September 1933, and had not come up the stairs, and had gone home, and had married Erika von Wense — had, without ever being told, without ever asking, and by the simple process of remaining, for eleven minutes at a time, a person who could not walk away, signed the warrant for the two of them: him, and her, and, if the men in that building had their way, the child.

She turned her face to the wall of the cell in Serbia and shut her eyes and said, out loud, in Polish, to the plaster, in a voice that was almost nothing:

"Bar eleven. From the top. Slowly."

And then she slept, because sleep was the only weapon a prisoner had, and because she had a great deal to do in the morning, which included staying alive for a child in a heated barrack at the Ostbahn depot, and finding, somewhere inside the hands of the men who owned her, one single thread that led out.
